On the Different Meanings of the Name of Jesus.

Jesus is the joy of those who mourn, the eternity of those who live, the health of the sick, the abundance of the needy, and the fullness of the hungry.

The author cites Bernard: “Jesus is honey in the mouth, music in the ear, and joy in the heart. If you write, it has no taste for me unless I read the name Jesus there. If you debate or hold a discussion, it has no taste for me unless Jesus sounds there.”

The name of Jesus is a name of goodness, holiness, and friendship. It is a name of supreme dignity, reverence, magnificence, kindness, and confidence. It is a name that awakens devotion, obtains answers to prayer, strengthens us in distress, helps us in need, and saves us in danger.

The name of Jesus is a name for the working of miracles, for the rout of enemies, for the advancement of virtues: a name strong, glorious, fruitful, healing, life-giving, and sweet to the soul. It is a name of praise, confession, and thanksgiving.
The name of Jesus is hateful to the corrupt, glorious to holy people, and rich in reward. It is effective for the conversion of sinners, for spiritual refreshment, for appeasing divine wrath, and for meritorious action. It brings God’s help—or, rather, God’s companionship—and his blessed visitation.

The name of Jesus obtains the grace of justification, contemplation, and devotion. It is desirable, venerable—yes, doubly venerable—impossible to hide, worthy of praise, and worthy of wonder. The name of Jesus must be venerated by offering sacred worship, presenting gifts, and avoiding perjury. It is a name to be heard devoutly, spoken rightly, and forbidden to no one.

From the name of Jesus comes glorification that gives honor, divine glorification, and the peaceful dwelling of the Holy Spirit. The name of Jesus is powerful for the forgiveness of sins, for receiving the Paraclete, and for obtaining the glory of heaven. Through the name of Jesus, our sight is directed and our love is trained.

In the name of Jesus, we must live, believe, rejoice, and repent. The name of Jesus must be spread throughout the world. It must be preferred even to one’s own life. For that name we must suffer, pray, work, speak, glory, make it known, call upon it, commit it to memory, attach it to our petitions, bring it into our discussions, and keep it as a protection for every action.

We should praise the name of Jesus in three ways: by living devoutly and honorably, by directing our actions to his praise, and by blessing him with heart and mouth.
The goodness of the name of Jesus is shown for four reasons: because it communicates grace, gives light, stirs the soul, and leads us forward.

The name of Jesus is called oil because it pours into us the love of contemplation, the richness of devotion, the sweetness of consolation, readiness for spiritual practice, grace amid uncertain thought, and the nourishment that restores us.

The name of Jesus is called oil because it lessens pain, renews us after labor, softens hardness, cures weakness, blends like a healing liquid, increases strength, seasons the taste of the soul, kindles warmth, and feeds the fire of love. The oil of the lily cleanses, loosens what is bound, draws out what must be brought forth, and is useful, delightful, and fitting.

The name of Jesus must be praised by harmony in our conduct, by mortification of the flesh, and by spiritual action. As the author says, “Give glory to his praise,” because praise adds glory to him: by making him known, by repeating his name often, by delighting in him, by lifting him high, and by honoring his incorruptibility.

From knowing the name of Jesus, we merit divine protection, the granting of prayer, fellowship with God, escape from tribulations, and the attainment of glory. This name perfects, strengthens, attracts, and raises us up.

Honor is the showing of reverence together with the testimony of virtue. We ought to revere the name of Jesus because it was given by God, consecrated from eternity, foretold by an angel, and exalted above all. We ought to revere the name of Jesus by devout genuflection, by adoring Jesus himself, by worthy salutation, and by avoiding perjury.

The name of Jesus has power to make us like God and to make us blessed. The sweetness of Jesus is wonderful, incomparable, lasting, unconquerable, endless, and able to continue without weariness.

The power of the name of Jesus is shown in the advancement of the Christian faith, the conversion of sinners, the overthrow of enemies, escape from harmful things, victory over torments, the wearing down of dragons, the subjection of demons, the speaking of new tongues, deliverance from disgrace, the healing of diseases, and the raising of the dead.

This name, Jesus, was prefigured by the fathers, desired by them, foretold by prophecy, and consecrated. This name, Jesus, surpasses every other name in dignity, sweetness, power, and usefulness. Those worthy of the fruit of this name are true believers, true penitents, those who hope in God, and those who return to the Lord.

This name, Jesus, drives demons away, creates wonder, heals the body, strengthens the fearful, torments the corrupt, joins God to the human person, forgives sins, pours in grace, and glorifies the saints. It also purifies, heals, enriches the devotion of the mind, enlightens, and obtains what is asked.

Sermon of Master Gerhard on the Life of Saint Godehard, Bishop of Hildesheim.

We long for Saint Godehard’s help from heaven. So while we are still in this world, let us also receive his teaching and follow his example. Otherwise, if we reject his warnings and abandon his deeds, we should not expect to feel the help we hoped for. No one deserves to be protected by the saints if he refuses to follow the saints’ examples and violates their commands.

As people move forward through this life—this journey toward eternal things—they fall into three groups. Some must live as subjects, some as companions, and some as leaders. Subjects are related to those above them; companions to their equals; leaders to those below them. Those who are subject must learn the humility of being under authority. Equals must learn the harmony of living together. Those placed in charge must learn the prudence required for governing others.

Saint Godehard advanced through these stages in proper order. In each one he marked out a path so clearly that others could imitate it. He showed his followers bright footprints, so to speak, by which they could run well in each of these states of life, reach the goal, and receive the prize.
So, as a boy in school, Godehard strove to be obedient and humble. Later, in the ordered fellowship of monastic life, he lived as a true companion among his brothers. Then, when he held the office of a ruler, he was careful and faithful. He did not learn to govern others before first learning to be governed by others, and then learning how to live among others.

He heard the prophet say, “In your beauty and your splendor, give attention; go forward prosperously and reign.” The author explains it this way: “In your beauty” means, “in the beautiful obedience by which you submit to those above you.” “In your splendor” means, “in the beautiful fellowship by which you live with your equals.” Then, “give attention, go forward prosperously, and reign” means, “preside over those below you.”

And notice what the prophet places between beauty, splendor, and kingship. First, “give attention”—to teaching, and also to the command of superiors. Then, “go forward prosperously”—in friendship with companions and equals. Only after that does he say, “reign”—that is, rule those under you with justice and with a burden they can bear. He does not say, “leap forward and reign,” as if someone should rush ahead too quickly. He says, “go forward”—step by step, in the right order.
For companionship follows obedience, and the authority of leadership follows companionship. A man who is going to place the weight of commands on others must first have carried that weight under others. Then, living among others, he must test by his own strength what another person’s shoulders can bear.

Otherwise, if he has never endured anything heavy himself, he will not know how to moderate his commands. He will become not a ruler, but an oppressor. He will deserve the rebuke given to the Pharisees: they laid heavy burdens on people, but would not move those burdens with even a finger. He will be unlike the One who first began to act and then to teach, and who therefore could truly say that his yoke is sweet and his burden light.

But some people jump straight from the cradle to the teacher’s chair. From childish games they are lifted into positions of authority. Whether they are children in behavior or children in age, they get used to speaking like tyrants, threatening scorpions instead of ordinary discipline. No wonder. In their own estimation they grow so great that they dare to say their little finger is thicker than their father’s back. And so they become a cause of schism. They give commands without discernment, and even when the command itself is right, they do not impose it in a bearable way. The result is that people are forced either to collapse under the burden or to rebel.
And why would people not collapse, when such leaders load them beyond their strength and do not support them—not even with the comfort of a word, let alone the help of a hand?

What is this newly promoted teacher? As Persius would put it, he is not even a three-penny donkey-driver. In fact, he is worse than a donkey-driver. After all, a man who drives a little donkey may load the poor animal with a sack during the day, but at least he comforts it with fodder in the evening.

Saint Godehard was different. As a boy, he humbly obeyed his teachers. As an adolescent and a young man, he kept friendship with his companions and lived sociably among them. Therefore, when he became a mature man, he governed those under him faithfully and like a father. He sought to help more than to dominate. He fed the little sheep entrusted to him with teaching and speech. He fed them also by his life and example. He fed them with spiritual help and with material support. In this way he fulfilled the threefold command, “Feed,” which Peter heard.

And so the true Prince of shepherds crowned him unfailingly among the greatest shepherds. Now he intercedes for us in heaven: he was born from among us, lived among us, and later became our bishop on earth. May this be granted by the One who is blessed forever. Amen.
