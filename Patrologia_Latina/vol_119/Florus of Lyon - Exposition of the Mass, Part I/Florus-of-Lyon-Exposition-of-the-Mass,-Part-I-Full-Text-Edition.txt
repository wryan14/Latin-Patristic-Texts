
by Florus of Lyon
Patrologia Latina Volume 


Exposition of the Mass

Here begins a short work on the action of the Mass. It has been gathered as fully as possible and arranged in order from the words of the holy Fathers: Cyprian, Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome, Gregory, Fulgentius, Severian, Vigilius, Isidore, Bede, and Avitus. The letters of their names, marked in the margin at the appropriate places, show which Father is being used in each passage. Some words have also, when necessary, been taken from ancient books of the mysteries. But those same names have not been inserted everywhere into the running text of this little work. Necessity required this, so that constantly interrupting the text with names would not create an awkward and inconvenient break in the wording and in the flow of thought. In this brief explanation, however, what is set forth and commended is not so much the wording, which is quite simple, but rather the meaning and action of the mystery itself: that is, with what faith it must be celebrated, and with how much devotion it must be embraced.
[Augustine] In the mystery of the body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, which the holy Church offers throughout the whole world for that same world’s salvation, humility is especially commended to us. For the Lord Jesus Christ himself made a way for us through humility, because through pride we had withdrawn from God. We could not return to God except through humility, and we had no one set before us whom we could imitate. All mortal humanity was swollen with pride. And even if there was some man humble in spirit, as the prophets and patriarchs were, the human race disdained to imitate humble human beings. Therefore, so that a human being would not disdain to imitate a humble human being, God became humble. In this way, at least, the pride of the human race would not disdain to follow the footsteps of God.

Now, as we read, the earlier sacrifices of the Jews, according to Aaron, consisted in animal victims. This was in mystery, as a figure; there was not yet the sacrifice of the body and blood of the Lord, which has now been spread throughout the whole world. So let us place two sacrifices before our eyes: that one according to the order of Aaron, and this one according to the order of Melchizedek. For Scripture says, “The Lord has sworn, and he will not repent: you are a priest according to the order of Melchizedek,” in Psalm one hundred nine, verse four. This is clearly said about our Lord Jesus Christ.

Who, then, was Melchizedek? Surely, as we read, he was the king of Salem. Salem was the earlier name of that city which, as learned writers have handed down, was afterward called Jerusalem. Therefore, before the Jews reigned there, that priest Melchizedek was there, the one written of in Genesis as the priest of the Most High God. He met Abraham when Abraham had rescued Lot from the hands of his pursuers, struck down those by whom Lot was being attacked, and freed his kinsman. After this rescue of his kinsman, Melchizedek met Abraham. So great was Melchizedek that Abraham was blessed by him. He brought out bread and wine, and he blessed Abraham; and Abraham gave him tithes. Notice what he brought out, and whom he blessed. And afterward it was said, “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” David said this in the Spirit long after Abraham. But Melchizedek lived in the time of Abraham. About whom else, then, does he say, “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” except about the one whose sacrifice we know?
For the sacrifice of Aaron has been taken away, and the sacrifice according to the order of Melchizedek has begun. Therefore our Lord Jesus Christ willed that our salvation should be in his body and blood. But from what did he commend his body and blood to us? From his humility. For unless he were humble, he would not be eaten, and he would not be drunk.

Look at his height: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God,” in the Gospel of John, chapter one, verse one. Behold the eternal food. But the angels eat it; the everlasting powers eat it; the heavenly spirits eat it. They eat and are filled, and yet what fills and gladdens them remains whole and undiminished. But what could a human being do with that food? Where would the human heart come from that was fit for such food? That table, then, had to become like milk and reach little children.

But how does food become milk? How is food turned into milk, unless it passes through flesh? That is what a mother does. What the mother eats, the infant eats. But because the infant is not yet capable of feeding on bread, the mother, so to speak, embodies that bread; and by the service of the breast, and through the liquid nourishment of milk, she feeds the infant from that very bread. So how did the Wisdom of God feed us from that bread itself? Because “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us,” in the Gospel of John, chapter one, verse fourteen.

See, then, the humility: a human being eats the bread of angels. And Scripture says, “He gave them the bread of heaven; a human being ate the bread of angels,” in Psalm seventy-seven, verse twenty-four. That is, the eternal Word by which the angels are fed, the Word equal to the Father, was eaten by a human being, because, “though he was in the form of God, he did not consider equality with God something to be seized,” in Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter two, verse six. The angels are filled by him; but “he emptied himself,” so that a human being might eat the bread of angels, “taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of human beings, and being found in appearance as a human being. He humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even death on a cross”—so that from the cross itself the flesh and blood of the Lord might now be commended to us as the new sacrifice.
[Augustine] Therefore, he himself, our priest sent by the Lord God, took from us what he would offer to God. He offered to God, as a whole burnt offering, those holy firstfruits of flesh taken from the womb of the Virgin. He stretched out his hands on the cross, so that he could say, as Scripture says in Psalm one hundred forty, verse two: “Let my prayer be directed like incense in your sight; the lifting up of my hands, like the evening sacrifice.” For we know that the Lord hung on the cross toward evening, and our sins were atoned for; otherwise they would have swallowed us up. Rightly, then, we sang to him in the psalm, Psalm sixty-four, verse four: “And you will atone for our sins.” You are the priest; you are the victim; you are the one who offers; you are the offering. He is the priest who has now entered behind the inner veil. There, he alone, of all who have borne flesh, intercedes for us. As a figure of this, among that first people and in that first temple, one priest entered the Holy of Holies, as described in the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter nine, verse seven. All the people stood outside, while the one man who entered behind the inner veil offered sacrifice for the people standing outside. The Jews did not understand what was taking place among that people; yet now they know. For it was said about them, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter three, verse fifteen: “As long as Moses is read, a veil lies over their heart.” There, the veil is the figure; but the figure will be removed, and the truth will appear. And when will the veil be removed? Listen to the Apostle, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter three, verse sixteen: “But when someone passes over to the Lord, the veil will be removed.” So, once our sins and acts of ungodliness have been atoned for through that evening sacrifice, we pass over to the Lord, and the veil is removed. That is why, when the Lord was crucified, the veil of the temple was torn.
And so we do not circumcise the flesh of the foreskin according to the rite of the Mosaic law. We do not abstain from the meats that the same law calls unclean. We do not keep their Sabbaths, new moons, and feast days in a merely bodily way. We do not sacrifice animal victims to God. We do not celebrate Passover in the same way, with a lamb and unleavened bread. And the same applies to any other ancient sacraments there were. The Apostle calls all of them “shadows of things to come,” because they pointed toward realities that were to be revealed in their own time, realities that we now receive as already revealed. The shadow has been removed, so that we may enjoy their unveiled light. For by putting off the old humanity, we are circumcised in the stripping away of the body of flesh. The foods they forbid among animals, we avoid in our behavior. We present our bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God. To him we pour out our souls with all their desires in place of blood, understanding this in a spiritual sense. We are cleansed from every sin by the blood of Christ, the spotless Lamb. Because he came “in the likeness of sinful flesh,” he was also prefigured in the old sacrifices by the goat; and anyone who recognizes in him the greatest victim does not refuse to see him, in the flesh of the cross, as the bull as well. When we find rest in him, we truly keep Sabbath. The observance of the new moon is now the sanctification of a new life. Our Passover is Christ, and our unleavened bread is the sincerity of truth, with no leaven of oldness. Whatever else was foreshadowed by those ancient signs finds its fulfillment in him, whose kingdom will have no end.
For everything had to be fulfilled in him who came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets, but to fulfill them. Therefore, he did not invalidate those old signs by condemning them; he changed them by fulfilling them. There had to be one set of signs to announce that Christ had now come, just as there had been another set of signs to announce beforehand that he was going to come. So, as it is written in the Song of Songs, chapter two, verse seventeen, “the day has dawned.” Let the shadows be removed. Let the spiritual meaning now shine out. Let the bodily celebration now come to rest. Let us hear the Lord rejecting the old sacrifices of the Jews and, through the prophet Malachi, very openly promising the new sacrifice of Christ. He says, in the book of Malachi, chapter one, verse ten: “I have no pleasure in you, says the Lord, and I will not receive a gift from your hand. For from the rising of the sun to its setting, my name is great among the nations; and in every place sacrifice is offered, and a clean offering is offered to my name, because my name is great among the nations, says the Lord of hosts.” Surely the Jews cannot deny this: not only does God not receive sacrifice from their hands, but they themselves no longer offer it to him with their hands. For the law of the Lord established one place where he commanded sacrifices to be offered by their hands, and he absolutely forbade them to be offered anywhere else. They lost that place because of their own deserts; and since the sacrifice was allowed to be offered only there, they do not dare offer it in other places. See, then, how completely the prophet’s word has been fulfilled: “And I will not receive a gift from your hands.”
Nor was this foretold and fulfilled in such a way that the prophetic statement leaves them any answer. They might say, “We do not offer flesh with our hands; we offer praise with heart and mouth,” according to the saying in the psalm, Psalm forty-nine, verse fourteen: “Offer to God a sacrifice of praise also.” But the one who says, “I have no pleasure in you,” contradicts them precisely here. Then, so that the Jews may not think that, because they do not offer sacrifice and God does not receive it from their hands, no sacrifice at all is offered—though God certainly does not need sacrifice, since he needs none of our goods—still, because sacrifice is not absent, and because it is useful not to him but to us, let them hear the Lord himself testifying. Better yet, let them open their eyes and see: “from the rising of the sun to its setting,” not in one place as had been established for them, but “in every place,” the sacrifice of Christians is offered. And it is not offered to just any god, but to the one who foretold these things, the God of Israel. Thus he says elsewhere to his Church, in Isaiah, chapter fifty-four, verse five: “And your Redeemer, the God of Israel, will be called the God of the whole earth.” This clean sacrifice is offered to the God of Israel, not by the one nation of the Jews, from whose hands he foretold that he would not receive it, but by all nations, who say, “Come, let us go up to the mountain of the Lord.” It is not offered in one place only, as was commanded in the earthly Jerusalem, but in every place, even in Jerusalem itself. And it is not according to the order of Aaron, but according to the order of Melchizedek. For it was said to Christ, and foretold in advance about Christ alone, in Psalm one hundred nine, verse four: “The Lord has sworn and will not repent: You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” What does “The Lord has sworn” mean, except that he has confirmed it with unshakable truth? And what does “he will not repent” mean, except that he will in no way change this priesthood? God does not repent the way a human being does. Rather, “repentance” is attributed to God when he changes something he had instituted, something people believed would remain. Therefore, when he says, “He will not repent: You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” he shows clearly that he did repent of the other priesthood—that is, that he chose to change the priesthood he had established according to the order of Aaron. We see both things fulfilled. There is now no priesthood of Aaron in any temple, while the priesthood of Christ endures forever in heaven.
The Apostle gives magnificent praise to this priest and this priesthood when, distinguishing the grace of the Gospel from the royal ceremonies, he writes to the Hebrews: “And the others were made priests in greater number, because death prevented them from remaining; but he, because he remains forever, has an everlasting priesthood. Therefore he is able to save forever, as he comes to God through himself, always living to intercede for them. It was fitting that we should have such a high priest: holy, innocent, undefiled, separated from sinners, and made higher than the heavens; one who would not have daily need, as the priests do, first to offer victims for his own sins, and then for the sins of the people. For he did this once, by offering himself.” This is cited as the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter seven, verse twenty-eight. And again: “We have such a high priest, who has sat down at the right hand of the Majesty in the heavens, a minister of the holy things and of the true tabernacle, which God and man made.” This is cited as the Letter to the Hebrews, chapter eight, verse one.
Therefore, our holy fathers in the Old Testament offered victims to the one God, the Creator of all things. God himself willed that these victims be offered to him, and through their likeness he promised the true victim. Through that true victim, in Christ Jesus our Lord, he reconciled us to himself by the forgiveness of sins. A likeness was offered to him, promising the reality of the sacrifice—to him to whom the reality itself was to be offered: the reality given back in the passion of the body and blood of Christ. Before the coming of Christ, the flesh and blood of this sacrifice were promised in likeness through sacrificial victims. In the passion of Christ, they were given in the reality itself. After the ascension of Christ, they are celebrated in memory through the sacrament. Therefore the first sacraments, those observed and celebrated according to the law, announced beforehand the Christ who was to come. Once Christ had fulfilled them by his coming, they were taken away; and they were taken away because they had been fulfilled. For he did not come to abolish the law, but to bring it to completion. Other sacraments were then instituted: greater in power, better in benefit, easier in practice, fewer in number. This happened as the righteousness of faith was revealed, as the children of God were called into freedom, and as the yoke of slavery—fitting for a hard people devoted to the flesh—was removed. Such are, in the Church, the baptism of Christ, the eucharist of Christ, and the seal of Christ.
This is why the Church of the living God, the pillar and foundation of the truth, makes such a careful distinction between the times: the time when Christ was still to come, and the time after he has come. The Church sets aside the sacrifices by which Christ’s future suffering was promised, and now offers this sacrifice, by which Christ is shown as already having suffered. He is the true priest precisely because he offered himself for us as the true victim. As the Apostle testifies: “Be imitators of God, then, as beloved children, and walk in love, just as Christ loved us and handed himself over for us, an offering and a victim to God, a sweet-smelling fragrance”—Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, verse one. So the forgiveness of sins takes place in this one who is both priest and sacrifice: that is, through the mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus. Through him, once our sins have been cleansed, we are reconciled to God. For nothing separates human beings from God except sins. And in this life, sins are not cleansed by our own strength, but by divine mercy; not through our power, but through God’s forgiveness. Even the little strength that we call our own has been granted to us by his goodness.
For this reason, grace has been given to us through the Mediator: we were defiled by sinful flesh, and we were to be made clean through the likeness of sinful flesh. That is, through what we were and what we were not. We were human beings, but we were not righteous. In his incarnation, there was true human nature, but it was righteous human nature, not sinful. This is the mediation by which a hand was stretched out to those who had fallen and were lying helpless. This is the seed arranged through angels. In their decrees, light was also being given, because they commanded that the one God be worshiped, and they promised that this Mediator would come. By using this sacrament in faith, even the righteous people of old deserved to be cleansed by living devoutly. The blessed apostle Peter bears clear witness to this when he speaks to those who were trying to be justified by the law: “Why are you testing the Lord by placing a yoke on the necks of the disciples, a yoke that neither we nor our fathers were able to bear? Rather, through the faith of the Lord Jesus, we believe that we are saved, in the same way as they are”—the Acts of the Apostles, chapter fifteen, verse ten.
So the whole multitude of human beings, for whose sake Christ came to free them from the rule of demons, have in him a most merciful cleansing of mind, spirit, and body. He took up the whole human person without sin, so that everything that makes up a human being might be healed from the plague of sins. God’s grace could not have been presented to us in a more gracious way than this: the only Son of God, while remaining unchangeably himself, took on humanity, and through the human Mediator gave human beings hope in his love. By that love, human beings would be united to the one who had been so far from them: the immortal from mortals, the unchangeable from changeable creatures, the righteous from the ungodly, the blessed from the miserable. And because he has planted in us by nature the desire to be blessed and immortal, he remained blessed while taking on mortality. In order to give us what we love, he taught us, by suffering, to look down on what we fear. By this grace of God, through which he has shown us his great mercy, we are guided in this life by faith; and after this life, by the direct vision of unchangeable truth, we are brought to the fullest perfection.
This grace of our cleansing and redemption was entrusted to us by the Lord and Savior in the sacrament of his body and blood. Long before he suffered, he proclaimed it publicly among the people; but on the day before he suffered, that is, on the night when he was handed over, he handed it on to his disciples at supper. Blessed John the Evangelist says that immediately after the miracle of the five loaves, by which five thousand people were fed, Jesus fled to the mountain. He withdrew from the crowds, because they were stirred by admiration for so great a sign and wanted to seize him and make him king. Then, when night came, he walked on the sea and came to Capernaum. The next day, when the crowds met him there, they asked, among other things, “What must we do to perform the works of God?” He answered them, “This is the work of God: that you believe in the one whom he has sent.” They replied, “Then what sign do you perform, so that we may see and believe you? What work do you do? Our fathers ate manna in the desert, as it is written: He gave them bread from heaven to eat.” Jesus said to them, “Amen, amen, I tell you: Moses did not give you the bread from heaven. Rather, my Father gives you the true bread from heaven. For the bread of God is the one who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world.” A little later he says, “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will not hunger, and whoever believes in me will never thirst.” And again: “Amen, amen, I tell you: whoever believes in me has eternal life. I am the bread of life. Your fathers ate manna in the desert and died. This is the bread coming down from heaven, so that if anyone eats from it, he will not die. I am the living bread who came down from heaven. If anyone eats from this bread, he will live forever. And the bread that I will give is my flesh, for the life of the world.” And again, after some further words: “Amen, amen, I tell you: unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you will not have life in yourselves. Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up on the last day. For my flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink. Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood remains in me, and I in him. Just as the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so also the one who eats me will live because of me.” After this the Evangelist adds, “He said these things while teaching in the synagogue at Capernaum”—the Gospel of John, chapter six, verses twenty-eight, thirty-five, forty-seven, fifty-four, and fifty-nine. As the very text of the Gospel according to John shows, all of this clearly took place a full year before the Lord’s passion. For, as the same Evangelist writes, when the miracle of the five loaves took place, “the Passover, the feast day of the Jews, was near”—the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse four. Yet John records that Jesus came to his passion not at that Passover, but at the Passover of the following year.
This is why, at that paschal supper when the Lord was handed over, blessed John says nothing about the mystery of Christ’s body and blood itself. Clearly, he had already explained it more fully and more sublimely in the earlier parts of his Gospel. He also knew that the other three Evangelists had carefully handed on what was given at that same supper, in remembrance of the Lord’s passion, for the salvation of the faithful. Matthew’s words are these: “While they were at supper, Jesus took bread, blessed it, broke it, gave it to his disciples, and said, ‘Take and eat. This is my body.’ And taking the cup, he gave thanks and gave it to them, saying, ‘Drink from this, all of you. For this is my blood of the New Testament, which will be poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins’”—the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-six, verse twenty-six. Mark says it this way: “While they were eating, Jesus took bread, and blessing it, broke it, gave it to them, and said, ‘Take. This is my body.’ And taking the cup, he gave thanks and gave it to them. And they all drank from it. And he said to them, ‘This is my blood of the New Testament, which is poured out for many’”—the Gospel of Mark, chapter fourteen, verse twenty-two. Luke’s account is given in these words: “And taking the cup, he gave thanks and said, ‘Take this and divide it among yourselves. For I tell you that I will not drink from the fruit of the vine until the kingdom of God comes.’ And taking bread, he gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to them, saying, ‘This is my body, which is given for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ Likewise also the cup after he had supped, saying, ‘This cup is the New Testament in my blood, which is poured out for you’”—the Gospel of Luke, chapter twenty-two, verse seventeen.
Blessed Paul also, recalling the mystery of that most holy supper, says among other things: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you: that the Lord Jesus, on the night when he was being handed over, took bread, and giving thanks, broke it and said, ‘This is my body, which will be handed over for you. Do this in remembrance of me.’ Likewise also the cup after he had supped, saying, ‘This cup is the New Testament in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me’”—Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, verse twenty-five. How great this mystery of salvation is! How beyond words is this sacrament of loving devotion! It was foretold with such solemn witness and entrusted with such truth that the Lord declared it to be his body and blood before his body and blood were handed over for us. No one can grasp the sacraments of the Lord’s passion, and of his body and blood, in a way that matches the majesty of the reality itself.
[Fulgentius] We must also note carefully that, at this same supper, the Lord both ate the Jewish Passover, which had to be taken away, and gave the sacrament of his body and blood, which had to be established for the salvation of the faithful. He says, “With desire I have desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer.” So he ate the Passover by which his coming suffering was signified, before he voluntarily suffered for us. He ate the Passover of the Jews, in which Christ was promised, so that he might come to our Passover, in which Christ was sacrificed.
[Augustine] For the Gospel says plainly that, while they were eating, Jesus took bread and blessed it. Earlier it had also said, “When evening came, he was reclining at table with the twelve. And while they were eating, he said, ‘One of you will betray me.’” After that, he handed on the sacrament. So it is completely clear that, when the disciples first received the body and blood of the Lord, they did not receive it fasting. Still, no one should use that fact to criticize the universal Church, because the sacrament is always received by people who are fasting. For from that time on the Holy Spirit was pleased that, in honor of so great a sacrament, the Lord’s body should enter the mouth of a Christian before any other food. That is why this custom is observed throughout the whole world. And because the Lord gave it after food, that does not mean that brothers and sisters should come to receive this sacrament after lunch or dinner, or mix it with their own meals, as those people did whom the Apostle rebukes and corrects. For he says, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, verse eighteen, “When you come together as one, it is no longer to eat the Lord’s supper,” calling this very receiving of the Eucharist the Lord’s supper. And he says, in the same chapter, verse twenty-one, “Each one goes ahead with his own supper to eat.” For this reason he says that it was received unworthily by those who did not distinguish it from other foods with the special reverence owed to it. Indeed, immediately after saying, “He eats and drinks judgment upon himself,” he added, in the same chapter, verse twenty-nine, “not discerning the body of the Lord.” The Savior, then, wanted to commend the depth of that mystery all the more powerfully. Since he was about to leave his disciples and go to his Passion, he chose to impress this last thing on their hearts and memory. And therefore he did not command in what order it should afterward be received, so that he might leave this matter to the apostles, through whom he had determined to order the Churches.
[Cyprian] For although the Lord offered the cup after supper, that does not mean we too must celebrate the sacrifice of the Lord’s Day after supper. Christ had to make the offering toward the evening of the day, so that the very hour of the sacrifice would point to the decline and evening of the world. As it is written about the Passover lamb in Exodus, chapter twelve, verse six: “The whole multitude of the children of Israel shall sacrifice it in the evening.” And again in the Psalms, Psalm one hundred forty, verse two: “The lifting up of my hands is an evening sacrifice.”
[Augustine] But we celebrate the Lord’s resurrection in the morning. When the Apostle, speaking about this sacrament, says, “Therefore, brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another. If anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, so that you do not come together for judgment,” he immediately adds, “The rest I will set in order when I come,” in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, verse thirty-three. From this we are given to understand that it would have been too much for him to explain to them in a letter the entire order of the action. This order, which the whole Church throughout the world observes, is to be understood as arranged by the Apostle himself, in so far as it is not varied by any of these differences. So too, when writing to Timothy, he says, in Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse one: “I urge, therefore, first of all, that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made.” He is understood to have established what every Church, or almost every Church, regularly does. We should understand “supplications” as those made in the celebration of the sacraments before what is on the Lord’s table begins to be blessed. “Prayers” are made when it is blessed and sanctified and broken for distribution; nearly the whole Church concludes this whole blessing with the Lord’s Prayer. “Intercessions,” or, as some manuscripts have it, “petitions,” are made when the people are blessed. For then the bishops, like advocates, offer those entrusted to them, through the laying on of hands, to the most merciful power of God. When these things have been completed, and when so great a sacrament has been shared, thanksgiving brings everything to its close; and the Apostle commended thanksgiving as the last of these in the very words just quoted.
The main reason he said these things was this: after briefly prescribing and indicating this liturgical order, he did not want what follows to be neglected. He continues, in Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse two, “for all people, for kings and for those in high positions, so that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all godliness and chastity.” Otherwise, because human thinking is weak, someone might suppose that these prayers should not be made for those from whom the Church was suffering persecution. But the members of Christ were to be gathered from every class of humanity. Therefore he adds and says, in the same chapter, verse three, “For this is good and acceptable before our Savior the Lord, who wants all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.” And to keep anyone from saying that there could be a way of salvation through good conduct and the worship of the one almighty God, apart from participation in the body and blood of Christ, he says: “For there is one God, and one mediator between God and human beings, the man Jesus Christ.” In this way, the statement that God “wants all people to be saved” must be understood as being fulfilled in no other way than through the mediator: not through Christ as God, which the Word always was, but through the man Christ Jesus, when “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.” For Christ is called the mediator between God and human beings because, as God and man, he stands between the immortal God and mortal humanity, reconciling humanity to God. He is the true king and the true priest. Therefore, so that a human being may not sin, let that person be ruled by Christ the king; and if that person should sin, let that person be cleansed by the same Christ, the priest.
So, first, the faithful come together and stand in the house of God. The singing of divine praise has gone before; the reading from the apostles and the Gospels has gone before; sometimes a sermon and exhortation by the teachers has also gone before. The confession of the Creed has been added, the people’s offering has been made, and the consecration of the sacraments has begun. Through all these things, the minds of those standing there are prepared to think about and desire divine and heavenly realities. Then the priest stands at the altar, about to celebrate the divine mysteries. He prays as he greets the Church, and greets it as he prays, saying:
“The Lord be with you.” This greeting is shown not to have been composed by human choice, but to have been taken from the authority of divine Scripture, where it is often found in both the singular and the plural. In the singular, the angel says to blessed Mary, in the Gospel of Luke, chapter one, verse twenty-eight: “Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with you.” Likewise, in greeting Gideon, the angel said, in Judges, chapter six, verse twelve: “The Lord is with you, most valiant of men.” In the plural, we read in the book of Ruth, which, like the other books of sacred Scripture, certainly contains divine and mystical meaning, that Boaz greeted the reapers by saying, “The Lord be with you.” And they, greeting him in return, said, “May the Lord bless you,” in Ruth, chapter two, verse four. So also, in the narrative of the book of Chronicles, a prophet sent by God is found to have greeted Asa, king of Judah, together with his army, as they were returning victorious from battle after calling on God’s help. For he says: “The Lord is with you, because you have been with him.” And he added: “If you seek him, you will find him; but if you abandon him, he will abandon you,” in First Chronicles, chapter twenty-eight, verse nine.
From this it is clear that, just as nothing can be worse for a person than to abandon God and be abandoned by God, so, on the other hand, nothing is better than to have God and to be with God. And because to greet someone is nothing other than to wish that person salvation and well-being, this is the true greeting: the wish for true salvation. It means that the Lord is with his Church as in his temple, as the Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter three, verse sixteen: “For the temple of God is holy, and that is what you are.” And the Church has the Lord with it: dwelling in it and enlightening it, ruling it and protecting it, so that he may be its God and it may be his people. As the prophet promised when he said, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter six, verse sixteen: “I will dwell in them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.” Thus, by the gift of such blessedness, the Church of God both possesses the Lord and is possessed by God, so that the Church is the Lord’s inheritance and the Lord is the Church’s share of inheritance. In the Church, then, what the Psalmist says is fulfilled, in Psalm thirty-two, verse twelve: “Blessed is the nation whose Lord is its God, the people whom he chose as an inheritance for himself.”
For this is the highest good of a human being: to possess God and to be possessed by God; to love him alone, to serve him alone, and to cling to him alone, so that the devout soul may say to him, in Psalm seventy-two, verse twenty-eight: “For me, it is good to cling to God.” And again, in Psalm sixty-two, verse nine: “My soul has clung after you.” In this way, by clinging to the Lord, the soul becomes one spirit with him, as the Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter six, verse seventeen: “Whoever clings to the Lord is one spirit.” The Apostle himself also gives this kind of greeting in his letters. Sometimes he says, “The Lord be with all of you.” Sometimes, “May the God of peace be with all of you.” Again, he says, in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter six, verse eighteen: “May the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. Amen.” And sometimes, distinguishing the persons and invoking the whole Trinity, he greets them in this way, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter thirteen, verse thirteen: “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit be with all of you.” In all these greetings, as he writes to the faithful, he desires the same thing that the priest desires as he stands at the altar: that those who have God may not be abandoned by him. Therefore, when the Church receives such a saving word from the priest, it rightly prays as it greets him in return, and greets him in return as it prays, saying:
“And with your spirit.” The Church can find nothing better to wish for the priest than what the priest wishes for the Church: that the same Lord who is pleased to be with the Church may also be pleased to be with the spirit of the priest. In this way, for both of them, that gracious promise of the Lord is fulfilled—the promise he made to all the faithful when he was about to ascend into heaven: “Behold, I am with you all days, even to the end of the age,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-eight, verse twenty.

So this is the great blessing that priest and Church ask and wish for one another: that, enlightened by his grace, strengthened by his presence, and guarded by his protection, we may experience him remaining with us always, just as he promised.

The Apostle also uses this kind of greeting at times. He uses it in the plural, as mentioned earlier, when he says, “The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. Amen,” in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter six, verse eighteen. And he uses it in the singular when, greeting Timothy, he says, “The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.”

It is fitting to wish that the Lord be with the human spirit, because the human being was created in spirit and rational mind according to the image and likeness of God. There, in the spirit and mind, the human person is able to receive divine grace and illumination, so that, as far as it is granted, one can perceive the sweetness of his goodness and the brightness of his presence. Then what the Psalmist says comes true within that person: “Taste and see that the Lord is sweet,” in Psalm thirty-three, verse nine. And again: “Come to him, and be enlightened, and your faces will not be ashamed,” in Psalm thirty-three, verse six.

For when the human spirit has been made holy by God dwelling within it, then the person becomes a temple even in the body. Hence the Apostle says, “Your bodies are the temple of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God,” in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter six, verse nineteen.
Likewise, the bishop’s greeting to the people—when he says, “Peace be with you,” or, “Peace be to you”—was not invented by human cleverness. It too was taken from the authority of Holy Scripture.

In the Old Testament, the angel is found saying to the prophet Daniel: “Do not fear, man of desires. Peace be to you. Be strengthened, and be strong,” in Daniel, chapter ten, verse nineteen. And in the New Testament, that is, in the Gospel, the Lord is almost always read as greeting the apostles in this way, saying, “Peace be to you.” He also entrusted this same form of greeting to those apostles when he said: “Into whatever house you enter, greet it, saying, ‘Peace to this house.’ And if a son of peace is there, your peace will rest upon him; but if not, your peace will return to you,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter ten, verse thirteen.

So it is fitting that the successors of the apostles—that is, the rulers of the churches—use this greeting when they greet the house of God, where everyone ought to be a child of peace. For Scripture says, “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five, verse nine. Then the greeting of peace, resting upon them, can bear fruit both for those who give the greeting and for those who receive it.

But the peace that is invoked upon the faithful—whether by an angel, by the Lord, or by the shepherds of the churches—is not worldly or passing. It is divine and eternal. It reconciles us to God; it guards us, under his protection, against the temptations of the devil and the hardships of this world; and it joins us calmly to our neighbors in love.

This is the peace the Lord entrusted to his Church when he said, “Peace I leave with you; my peace I give to you.” And immediately he added, “Not as this world gives do I give to you,” in the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen, verse twenty-seven. Again he says of this peace: “I have spoken these things to you so that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have distress; but take courage: I have conquered the world,” in the Gospel of John, chapter sixteen, verse thirty-three.

The Apostle also asks this peace for the faithful when he says: “And may the peace of God, which surpasses every understanding, guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus,” in Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter four, verse seven.

If this peace must be preserved at every time, and faithfully kept everywhere, then how much more carefully and devoutly must it be kept at that hour when we stand in the house of the Lord and in the sight of God, and when the prayers and offerings of all are presented on the holy altars. At that moment, we cannot make the divine Majesty favorable to us in any other way unless, with sincere love, we make every effort to fulfill what the Lord specifically commands about this matter: “If you offer your gift at the altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, leave your gift there before the altar, and first go be reconciled to your brother; and then come and offer your gift,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five, verse twenty-three.
[Gregory] In this matter we must consider this: if every fault is resolved by an offering, how serious must the fault of discord be, when for its sake even the offering is not accepted.

So we must go in our minds to our neighbor, even if that neighbor is far away and far removed from us. We must humble our heart before him and appease him with humility and goodwill. Then, when the Creator sees this kind of offering in our mind, he will release us from sin, because he accepts the offering in place of the fault.

We have learned from the voice of Truth himself that the servant who owed ten thousand talents received from his lord the cancellation of his debt when he repented. But because that servant did not forgive the debt of his fellow servant, who owed him one hundred denarii, he was ordered to pay again the very debt that had already been forgiven him.

From these words it is clear: if we do not forgive from the heart what has been done against us, then what we rejoiced had already been forgiven us through repentance will be demanded from us again.
[Cyprian] It is absolutely necessary that, when we stand for prayer, and when such holy and awe-inspiring mysteries are being carried out before the eyes of God, we keep watch in prayer with our whole heart. Every fleshly and worldly thought must go away. The mind must think of nothing except the very thing for which it is praying.

That is why, before that most holy prayer, the priest first gives the preface and prepares the hearts of the brethren by saying, “Lift up your hearts.” And when the people answer, “We have lifted them up to the Lord,” each person is being reminded that he must think of nothing but the Lord. His heart is to be closed against the adversary and open to God alone. He must not allow the enemy of God to come near him during the time of prayer.

For the enemy often creeps in and makes his way inside. By a subtle deception he draws our prayers away from God, so that we have one thing in our heart and another on our lips. Yet it is not the sound of the voice, but the mind and understanding, that should pray to God with sincere attention—as if there could be anything more important to think about than the fact that we are speaking with God.

How do we ask to be heard by God, when we ourselves do not even hear ourselves? We want God to remember us, while we ourselves do not remember ourselves. That is, when we pray carelessly, we offend the majesty of God by our neglect in prayer. We are awake with our eyes and asleep in our heart, although a Christian ought to keep watch with the heart even while sleeping with the eyes. As Scripture says, speaking in the person of the Church in the Song of Songs, chapter five, verse two: “I sleep, and my heart keeps watch.”

Therefore the Apostle gives this careful and watchful warning when he says in Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter four, verse two: “Persevere in prayer, keeping watch in it.” He is teaching and showing that those whom God sees keeping watch in prayer are able to obtain from God what they ask.
So what does it mean to have our hearts lifted up, except to seek the things that are above and to set our minds on the things that are above? We seek them by loving them, and we set our minds on them by understanding them. In these very words the Apostle warns us in Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter three, verse one: “If you have risen with Christ, seek the things that are above, where Christ is seated at the right hand of God; set your minds on the things that are above, not on the things that are on earth.”

The Psalmist shows us that this ascent of our heart to God is made from the valley of weeping—that is, from the lowliness of affliction—not by our own strength, but with God’s help. He says in Psalm eighty-three, verse six: “Blessed is the man whose help is from you; he has arranged ascents in his heart, in the valley of tears.”

No ascent can be useful unless we first remember that we must climb up from the valley of humility. A valley is a low place in the earth. The Lord himself teaches that we must ascend from the valley of weeping, since for our sake he was willing to be humbled even to death on a cross, and to suffer. Let us not abandon this example. The martyrs understood this valley of weeping, and from it they climbed up to receive their crowns.

Holy love lifts us toward the things above and sets us on fire for eternal things. A soul tied down by earthly love has, as it were, birdlime on its wings and cannot fly. But when it has been cleansed from the filthiest attachments of this world, then, like a bird with wings spread out and both wings freed—that is, with the two commandments of love of God and love of neighbor—it flies to the Lord. It rises by flying, because it rises by loving, toward that heavenly Jerusalem, the mother of us all, who is in heaven.
From that city we are exiles in this life. We long to return to it, miserable and burdened as we are, until we do return. And our fellow citizens, the angels, have not abandoned us in our exile. Rather, they announced to us that the King himself would come to us.

And he did come to us. Among us he was despised by us, and afterward he was despised along with us. He taught us to accept being despised, because he himself was despised. He taught us to endure, because he endured. He taught us to suffer, because he suffered. And he promised that we would rise, because he rose, showing in himself what we ought to hope for.

So if the prophets and the ancient fathers, before the Lord Jesus Christ came in the flesh, before he died, rose again, and ascended into heaven, still longed for that city, then how much more ought we to desire the place to which he himself has gone ahead of us, and from which he has never departed.

The priests of earlier times were not so concerned with polished speech as with the salvation and building up of the people. So, for the sake of the uneducated and the rural poor, they used to say the words “Lift up your hearts” not in their correct form but in the everyday pronunciation, so that so great a matter might be made clearer to everyone’s understanding.

The priest then continues his admonition and says:
“Let us give thanks to the Lord our God.”

Thanksgiving is always owed by us to the Father of lights, from whom, as Scripture bears witness, every good gift and every perfect gift undoubtedly comes down. No one is blessed by God’s gifts if he is ungrateful to the giver. For even this command—that during the sacred mysteries we are told to have our heart lifted up—we can carry out only with God’s help, just as we are reminded of it by God’s command.

And therefore it follows that, for so great a good as a heart lifted upward, we should not give glory to ourselves, as though it came from our own strength. Instead, we should give thanks to the Lord our God. We are immediately reminded that this is right and just. We must always remember where these words come from, amid what sacred actions they are commended to us, and with what great holiness. We should hold fast to what we receive, possess what we receive, and give thanks to the giver.

For although receiving and possessing are things we do, still what we possess is what we have received. To the proud person, who wickedly boasts about what he has as though he had it from himself, Truth speaks through the Apostle in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter four, verse seven: “What do you have that you did not receive? And if you received it, why do you boast as though you had not received it?”

So rightly, in that most true and unique sacrifice of the Lord, we are reminded to give thanks to our God. Each person is to recognize that, if he lives rightly in any way, he has this by the grace of God; and that he will not obtain perfection in the love of righteousness from any other source. For this way of thinking is what makes a person devout. This is that wisdom called piety, by which the Father of lights is worshiped—the one from whom every good gift and every perfect gift comes.

He is worshiped by the sacrifice of praise and by acts of thanksgiving, so that his worshiper may boast not in himself, but in him. As the same Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter two, verse twelve: “We have not received the spirit of this world, but the Spirit who is from God,” so that we may know the things given to us by God.

And what is the spirit of this world, if not the spirit of pride? By that spirit, the foolish heart was darkened in those who knew the Lord, but did not glorify him as God by giving thanks.
So let us consider these things, and let us always give thanks to him for all God’s benefits. And among all his works, there is nothing we should more gladly contemplate or more sweetly praise than this: Christ became humble, and freely came down to the human being who, by falling, had been cast down to the lowest depths, so that the one lying there might believe he could be raised up.

In short, to lift our hope to heaven, Christ first lifted our flesh to heaven. And so that we would not doubt that what he promised us would follow, what he received from us has already gone ahead of us to that place.
[Fulgentius] Therefore, especially in the sacrifice of the body of Christ, we begin with thanksgiving in order to show the truth: Christ is not someone who is still to be given to us; he has already been given to us. And by giving thanks to God in the offering of the body and blood of Christ, we recognize that Christ is not still to be killed for our iniquities; he has already been killed. Nor are we still waiting to be redeemed by that blood; we have already been redeemed.

For the preaching of blessed Peter is true when he says in Peter’s first letter, chapter one, verse eighteen: “You were not redeemed from your vain way of life, handed down from your fathers, by corruptible silver or gold, but by the precious blood of Christ, like that of a spotless and undefiled lamb.”

So when the priest gives this good admonition and says, “Let us give thanks to the Lord our God,” the Church answers:
“It is right and just.”

For what could be more worthy of God, or what could we more justly give back to him, than to offer the thanks we owe for so great a mercy of God—mercy that we cannot repay in kind? We hear the Apostle urging us when he says in Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter one, verses twelve through fourteen: “Giving thanks to God the Father, who has made us worthy to share in the lot of the saints in light; who has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of his love, in whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins.”

For God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son, and by the grace of God he tasted death for all. Therefore, through this grace, we are rescued from the power of darkness—that is, from the devil and his angels—and transferred into the kingdom of the Son of God’s love, so that we may become a chosen race and a royal priesthood, possessing in that same Son, through his blood, redemption, the forgiveness of sins.

So what more worthy thing can we do than what the blessed apostle Peter urges when he says in Peter’s first letter, chapter two, verse nine: “Proclaim the virtues of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light; you who once were not a people, but now are the people of God; you who had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy”?

The priest therefore confirms this response of the Church. With his mind lifted up to God, and with the whole Church joining in this acknowledgment, he proclaims and says:
“It is truly right and just, equitable and saving.” What is just is also equitable. But repeating the idea strengthens the truth, because justice and equity are one reality. So we sing in the Psalter about the Lord: “He will judge the whole world in justice, and the peoples in equity,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm ninety-five, verse ten. A thing is called “saving” when it is wholesome and brings salvation. So it is truly fitting that we should give thanks without ceasing: fitting because God’s benefits are so great; just because of the service we owe him; equitable by the very weight of reason; and saving by the evident fruit of salvation. For those who praise God are saved, while the ungrateful are rejected by his judgment.
For those who faithfully praise God are truly saved, as the Psalm says: “Praising, I will call upon the Lord, and I shall be saved from my enemies,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm seventeen, verse four. But the ungrateful are rejected. The Lord himself shows this plainly in the Gospel. Ten lepers were cleansed by his power, yet only one returned to him in supplication and gave thanks with a loud voice. The Lord praised him and saved him by the grace of faith, saying: “No one was found to return and give glory to God except this foreigner.” And at once, in his mercy, he added: “Rise, go; your faith has made you well,” from the Gospel of Luke, chapter seventeen, verse eighteen.
As for the other nine: they did believe that they could be cleansed through him, when they cried out together, “Jesus, Master, have mercy on us.” Yet because they were ungrateful after such a mighty act, he showed that he did not know them, treating them like unbelievers, when he said: “Were not ten cleansed? And where are the nine?” from the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-five, verse twelve. For God’s “not knowing” someone means that the person is rejected. That is why he will also say to some people: “Amen, I say to you, I do not know you.” This shows clearly how grave an evil it is to neglect the thanksgiving owed to God: even those whom he cleansed when they believed, he afterwards cast aside as unbelievers because they were guilty of this fault, even though they had been cleansed.
But we must give thanks always and everywhere—that is, at every time and in every place—so that what we sing in the Psalm may be fulfilled in us: “I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall always be in my mouth,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm thirty-three, verse two. And again: “In every place of his dominion, bless the Lord, my soul,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm one hundred two, verse twenty-two.
[Augustine] So who is it who blesses the Lord and gives him thanks at all times, if not the one who is humble in heart? Our Lord taught this very humility in his own body, because when he entrusts to us his body and blood, he entrusts to us his humility. Be humble, then, if you want to bless the Lord at all times, with his praise always in your mouth. Job did not bless the Lord only when he had plenty of everything, during the time when, as we read, he was rich. Everything was taken from him all at once, and he fulfilled what is written in this Psalm when he said: “The Lord gave; the Lord has taken away. As it pleased the Lord, so it has been done.” Then he added: “Blessed be the name of the Lord,” from the Book of Job, chapter one, verse twenty-one. Here is an example of someone blessing God at all times. Job had nothing left to hold on to, but he still had someone to praise. He rested in the praise of his God as if he were resting in an abundance of possessions. God was his comfort, so that the loss of property would not destroy him. After all, had he lost God, even though he had lost what God had given?
For it is better to possess the giver than to possess the things he gives. If the things he gives are beautiful, how much more beautiful is the one who gives them? If they are great, how much greater is the giver himself? And certainly, whatever God gives in this earthly life either leaves us while we are still living, or else we leave it behind at death. But God himself is an eternal possession, always present; he never abandons the one who possesses him. So let the desires of the faithful be pure. Let temporary goods become cheap in our eyes, and let the eternal good come. Let the devout soul cling to God, lifted up to him in prayer; joined to him in a spiritual embrace, let it desire what he gives together with himself. What would it amount to for God to give us something, if he were unwilling to give us himself? So if the goods of this world are available, let them become material for works of mercy. If they are lacking, let that become an opportunity for faithful patience.
With devotion like this, thanks are given to God in every place as well. For the one who is present everywhere recognizes his worshiper everywhere. Even when the mouth is still and the tongue is silent, the faithful soul blesses him in the hidden affection of the heart, doing silently what is sung in the Psalm: “Bless the Lord, my soul, and all that is within me, bless his holy name,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm one hundred two, verse one. So, if you are looking for a holy and suitable place in which to pray to God and give him thanks, cleanse your inner self. Drive out every evil desire from within, and prepare for yourself a hidden place in the peace of your heart, since you want to pray in a temple. Pray within yourself, and always live in such a way that you are God’s temple. For God hears prayer where he dwells.
“It is truly right and just, equitable and saving, that we should give you thanks always and everywhere.” Next he adds to whom he directs the prayer, saying: “Lord, holy Father, almighty and eternal God.” And he adds through whom he directs that same prayer: “Through Christ our Lord.” So when he says “Lord,” this refers to the subjection of every creature. About this we sing in the Psalm: “For all things serve you,” from the Book of Psalms, Psalm fifteen, verse one. And elsewhere: “I said to the Lord: You are my God, for you have no need of my goods.” What are “my goods,” after all, except goods given by you? How could the one from whom every good is given need any good thing? Therefore he is the true Lord, because he has no need of his servant. And what servant would he need?
When he adds “holy Father,” this can refer in a special sense to the begetting of the only-begotten Son, in which the Son is co-eternal and co-equal with the Father. About this begetting the Prophet also says: “Who will tell of his generation?” from the Book of Isaiah, chapter thirty-five, verse eight. Or it can refer more generally to the adoption of all God’s children. The grace of this adoption is so great that, although God had one only Son, he did not want him to remain alone. Instead, so that his Son would have brothers, God adopted those who would possess eternal life together with him. Praying for them to the Father, the Son himself used these same words: “Holy Father, keep in your name those whom you have given me,” from the Gospel of John, chapter seventeen, verse eleven.
So this way of addressing God—calling him “holy Father”—belongs properly to the only Son by nature, and it belongs in common to all God’s adopted children by grace. Through that grace, he also allowed them to say in prayer: “Our Father, who are in heaven,” from the Gospel of Matthew, chapter six, verse nine. He is truly almighty and eternal God: almighty, that is, by the power with which he created all things, holds all things together, and governs all things. Scripture speaks truly when it says, “Nothing is impossible for you.”
[Augustine] Yet God is almighty in such a way that he never tears apart the order established by his own reason. True eternity also belongs to him: for him, to be is to exist forever. Since he has no beginning, he has no end, and he admits no change.

All prayer and thanksgiving, which we owe always and everywhere, is directed and offered to God the Father through Christ our Lord, as through the mediator and reconciler of God and human beings. Of him the Apostle says: “For there is one God, and one mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a redemption for all”—Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse five.

Christ, who in the nature of his divinity is one with the Father, became our mediator with the Father by taking on our nature. He is mediator between God and human beings because he is God with the Father and human with human beings. He is not mediator as a human being apart from divinity, nor as God apart from humanity. Divinity without humanity is not the mediator; humanity without divinity is not the mediator. Rather, between divinity alone and humanity alone, the mediator is the human divinity and the divine humanity of Christ.

He also offered himself in his passion for us, so that we might become the body of so great a head, according to the form of a servant. This is what he offered; in this he was offered, because according to this human nature he is mediator. In this nature he is priest; in this nature he is sacrifice.

Through him, therefore, the Church offers its petitions and praises to God the Father. The Church knows that only through him can she receive what she asks, and only through him can the praise she offers be pleasing. As he promises in the Gospel: “Amen, amen, I say to you, if you ask the Father for anything in my name, he will give it to you”—the Gospel of John, chapter sixteen, verse twenty-three.

This is the grace of mediation through which the Church is heard. In keeping with it, the Apostle urges us: “Through him, then, let us always offer to God a sacrifice of praise”—the letter to the Hebrews, chapter thirteen, verse fifteen—that is, the fruit of lips confessing his name.

For the same mediator between God and human beings is God above us and human for our sake. Through his humanity he intercedes for us before the Father; through his divinity he hears and grants together with the Father. As he himself testifies: “Because I go to the Father, and whatever you ask in my name, I will do this, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son”—the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen, verse twelve.

About this mediation and advocacy, blessed John the Apostle also says: “We have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous, and he is the atonement for our sins”—John’s first letter, chapter two, verse two. For the one who, through his humanity, intercedes for us before the Father is the same one who, through his divinity, is gracious to us together with the Father.

So the priest prays for the Church, and the Church prays for the priest, just as the Apostle prays for the people and the people pray for the Apostle. He says: “Praying also for us, that God may open for us a door for the word”—Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter four, verse three. And the Church prayed for Peter when he was in chains, just as Peter prayed for the Church, because all the members pray for one another. The Head intercedes for all, as it is written: “He is at the right hand of God, and he also intercedes for us”—Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verse thirty-four.
[Gregory] For the only-begotten Son to intercede for humanity means this: before the Father, who is coeternal with him, he shows himself as human. And to have pleaded for human nature means that he has taken that same nature up into the height of his own divinity.

So the Lord intercedes for us not by speaking aloud, but by showing mercy. What he did not want to be condemned in his chosen ones, he saved by taking it upon himself. And because the nature he laid in the tomb by dying, he raised above the angels by rising again, the priest rightly adds and says:
“Through whom the angels praise your majesty.”

This is the inexpressible glory of our Savior and mediator. As the Apostle says, “Having made purification for sins, he sits at the right hand of the Majesty on high, having become so much greater than the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent than theirs”—the letter to the Hebrews, chapter one, verse three.

Explaining the height of that name elsewhere, the Apostle says: “Therefore God exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bend, in heaven, on earth, and under the earth”—Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter two, verse nine.

Thus, through this same mediator, not only the Church of human beings on earth, but also the Church of angels in heaven, praises, adores, and trembles before the majesty of God the Father.
[Augustine] The whole Church must be understood in this way as one: not only that part of the Church which is on pilgrimage on earth, praising the name of the Lord from the rising of the sun to its setting, and singing a new song after the captivity of the old life, but also that part which is in heaven. From the moment it was created, that heavenly part has always clung to God and has never suffered the evil of falling away.

This heavenly Church remains blessed in the holy angels, and it helps its pilgrim part as it ought. Both parts will be one in the shared life of eternity, and even now they are truly one by the bond of love.

So that part of the Church which is on pilgrimage on earth has been redeemed from every sin by the blood of the Mediator, who has no sin. Here a few words are missing from the printed Latin text. “If God is for us, who is against us? He did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all”—Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verse thirty-two.

Christ did not die for the angels. Yet the redemption of human beings also benefits the angels, because through Christ we are restored to friendship with them, after the hostilities that human sins had created between human beings and the holy angels. And through this redemption of humanity, the losses caused by that angelic fall are repaired.

For this reason the Apostle says that “all things are restored in Christ, the things in heaven and the things on earth”—Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter one, verse ten. The things in heaven are restored in him when what fell from there among the angels is given back from among human beings. The things on earth are restored when those human beings who have been predestined to eternal life are renewed from the decay of the old condition.

And so, through that one unique sacrifice in which the mediator was sacrificed, heavenly things are reconciled with earthly things, and earthly things with heavenly things. As the Apostle says: “In him all fullness was pleased to dwell, and through him to reconcile all things to himself, making peace by the blood of his cross, whether the things on earth or the things in heaven”—Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter one, verse twenty-two.

About him, the same Apostle says elsewhere: “And he is the head of the body, the Church”—Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter one, verse eighteen. And again: “And you have been filled in him, who is the head of every principality and power”—Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter two, verse ten.

Therefore all the righteous who have existed from the beginning of the world have Christ as their head. They believed that he would come; we believe that he has already come. They were healed in the same faith in which we too are healed. Thus Christ is the head of the whole city of Jerusalem, with all the faithful counted together from beginning to end, and with the legions and armies of angels joined to them. There will be one city under one king, like one province under one emperor, happy in everlasting peace and salvation, praising the Lord without end, in a blessed end.

As for those three orders of spirits—that is, angels, dominions, and powers—who are said to praise, adore, and tremble before the divine majesty, this is the difference among them.
[Gregory] Angels—translated into Latin as “messengers”—show us their office by their very name. They are sent by God and announce his will to human beings. In the Old Testament, we read of angels sent to Abraham, to Lot, to Jacob, to Moses, to Joshua, to Daniel, and to Tobias; and in the New Testament this happens often.

So the Psalmist says: “Bless the Lord, his angels, mighty in strength, doing his word, ready to hear the voice of his speech”—Psalm one hundred two, verse twenty-nine. And again: “He makes his angels spirits”—Psalm one hundred three, verse four. It is as if Scripture were saying plainly: the ones whom God always has as spirits, he also makes angels—that is, messengers—whenever he wills.

Dominions are those companies of angels that stand out with wondrous power, because the other spirits are subject to them in obedience. Powers are those who, in their own order, have received this with greater strength than the others: hostile powers are subject to their authority and are restrained by their power, so that they cannot tempt human hearts as much as they would like.

These blessed and exalted spirits in heaven, then, praise the majesty of God through Christ our Lord. The psalm joyfully addresses them: “Praise the Lord from the heavens; praise him in the heights. Praise him, all his angels; praise him, all his powers”—Psalm one hundred forty-eight, verse one.

God also speaks of them in the book of Job, saying: “When the stars were made, all my angels praised me with a loud voice”—Job, chapter thirty-eight, verse seven. They also adore the same divine majesty, as the hymn of confession in Esdras says to God: “You yourself, Lord God, made heaven, the heaven of heaven, and everything in it; you give life to all these things, and the host of heaven adores you”—Second Esdras, chapter nine, verse six. They also tremble, as is said figuratively of them in the book of Job: “The pillars of heaven tremble and are afraid at his nod”—Job, chapter twenty-six, verse ten.
[Gregory] For even the powers of heaven, who look upon God without ceasing, tremble in the very act of contemplating him. But for them this fear is not a punishment; it is not terror, but wonder. So, since among the heavenly beings there is such devotion in praise, such reverence in worship, and such trembling in wonder, let the human being consider this—the human being to whom it was said, “Why is earth proud?” And let him listen to the Apostle’s warning: “Work out your own salvation with fear, reverence, and trembling”—Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter two, verse twelve. The priest continues in the praises of God and says:
“The heavens, the Virtues of the heavens, and the blessed Seraphim celebrate it together with shared exultation.” We should understand the words already mentioned: they celebrate “your majesty.” For, as was said above, not only angels, dominions, and powers praise, adore, and tremble before that majesty; the heavens, the Virtues of the heavens, and the Seraphim also celebrate it together—that is, they celebrate it in common, praising it with one devotion and one shared joy. A “celebration,” after all, means an assembly of people gathered in praise; and we call things “celebrated” when they are done in a crowded, public assembly. So that blessed people of the angels celebrates, and celebrates together, the majesty of God. Scripture says of their indescribable multitude: “Thousands of thousands ministered to him, and ten times a hundred thousand stood before him”—the book of Daniel, chapter seven, verse ten. For the face of God is always present to them, as the Lord says in the Gospel: “Their angels in heaven always see the face of my Father who is in heaven”—the Gospel of Matthew, chapter eighteen, verse ten. Since God is seen without failing and loved without failing, he is loved and praised; the citizens he has chosen live in pure joy, and in their assembly they all rejoice with one another. This is the eternal house of God, of which the Psalms sing: “Blessed are those who dwell in your house; they will praise you forever and ever”—Psalm eighty-three, verse five. And again: “The dwelling of all who rejoice is in you”—Psalm eighty-six, verse seven. So it is well said here that they celebrate God’s majesty together with shared—that is, united—exultation. As has already been said, they praise him without interruption, and they rejoice together over him and over themselves in him. This is clearly read about the Seraphim themselves in Isaiah: “They flew, one toward another, and said, ‘Holy, holy, holy,’” and so on—the book of Isaiah, chapter six, verse three.
[Augustine] They rightly praise the one whom they serve, before whom they stand, and to whom they give glory in the highest. For to him they owe what they are; to him they owe that they live; to him they owe that they live rightly; and to him they owe that they live blessedly. We must not think that only human beings depend on the grace of God. What was an angel before he was made? And what is an angel if the one who created him abandons him? Therefore the heavens praise and celebrate this Creator of all things, just as the sun and moon praise him, and all the stars and light. Hence the Psalm says: “Praise him, sun and moon; praise him, all stars and light. Praise him, heavens of heavens, and let all the waters above the heavens praise the name of the Lord”—Psalm one hundred forty-eight, verse three.
[Augustine] Likewise, in the hymn of the three young men, all the elements are summoned to praise God—not because the mute elements have a conscious power to praise, but because everything, when rightly considered, gives rise to praise, and the heart is filled by contemplating creation until it pours out a hymn to the Creator. And because, in this thanksgiving, it would have been too long to list one by one all the orders of angels—of which nine are found in Scripture—the Virtues are named after the heavens. This name, “Virtues,” is sometimes used in a general way for all the heavenly spirits, as in the Psalm: “By the word of the Lord the heavens were established, and by the breath of his mouth all their power”—Psalm thirty-one, verse six. And again: “The Lord of powers, he is the king of glory”—Psalm twenty-three, verse ten. Strictly speaking, however, among the orders of angels, the Virtues are those spirits through whom signs and miracles are most often done. Last of all the Seraphim are named. They are the highest order of angelic spirits, and they are truly blessed, because, from their uniquely close nearness to their Creator, they burn with an incomparable love. This is why “Seraphim” is interpreted as “burning ones” or “those who set on fire”: the more subtly they gaze upon the brightness of the Godhead, the more strongly they blaze with love for him.
[Vigilius] We should also know that “Cherubim” and “Seraphim,” when pronounced with the letter em at the end, are, according to the proper usage of Hebrew, masculine in gender and only plural in number. But if they are pronounced with the letter en at the end, as happens in psalms and hymns and in the present thanksgiving, then by Greek declension they are changed into the neuter gender. It is like when we say, in Latin, “bright stars,” “beautiful groves,” or “pleasant shores”: in the same way, we understand “blessed Seraphin” as neuter in gender and plural in number. But we must recognize this very carefully: earlier, the holy angels and the other powers of heaven were said to praise, tremble before, and celebrate with shared exultation the majesty of God the Father, through Christ our Lord. This should not be understood as though they offered these acts of veneration and glorification to God the Father alone, and not also to the Son and to the Holy Spirit. Rather, we must hold and know with absolute faith that, just as there is one divinity and one undivided majesty of the whole holy Trinity—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit—so also equal honor and undivided glorification are due to the Trinity in all things. In no way can the Father be praised, adored, or honored with any service of divine worship without the Son; nor can the Son be praised, adored, or honored without the Father and the Holy Spirit.
[Vigilius] For there is no separation within the Trinity. If someone adores the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit are there. If someone prays to the Son and to the Holy Spirit, the Father is certainly found there as well. For when you invoke the Father or the Son, you embrace the whole unity of the Godhead, the whole Trinity. If we truly and faithfully believe that the Father is in the Son and the Son is in the Father—as Isaiah says, speaking to that same Son, “God is in you, and there is no God besides you”—the book of Isaiah, chapter forty-five, verse fourteen—and as he says in the Gospel, “I am in the Father and the Father is in me”—the Gospel of John, chapter ten, verse thirty-eight—then there is no doubt that the sacrifice is offered by us to the Son as well as to the Father, since the Son remains in that same Father as one of the same substance. For since they remain in one another in such a way that they cannot be separated, believe that whatever you offer to one is offered to both. Finally, the Son says: “Whoever has seen me has also seen the Father. I and the Father are one”—the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen, verse nine. Thus the vision of the Son is the contemplation of the Father; and honoring the Son is the honor or glory of the Father, as the same Son says, so that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father.
[Augustine] What if there are people who honor the Father, but do not honor the Son? He says: that cannot happen. Whoever does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him. But in case you try to honor the Father as greater, and the Son as lesser, the Son himself corrects you and calls you back, saying that everyone must honor the Son—not with a lower honor, but just as they honor the Father. So whatever we offer to the divine majesty in obedience to heavenly commands—whether in the rite of sacrifices, or in prayers of supplication, or in any other kind of generous service—is owed equally to the Father and to the Son. The Father possesses no private honor of veneration that does not also belong to the dignity of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. For if baptism, the sacrament of regeneration, belongs equally to them, since that blessed grace of the second birth is celebrated in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, then no one should doubt that the sacrifices of the mysteries also belong to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit by one shared honor of dignity, because the Trinity itself is joined together in the inseparable unity of the Godhead.
But the holy apostles, with the Holy Spirit ordering this through them, established that prayer should be directed to the Father alone in this way. They were trying, by preaching the one God, to tear out the errors of many gods. Therefore, while preserving the mystery of the Trinity, they decided that in the rite of sacrifice supplication should be made to one person, so that those who preached one God in order to root out the errors of false gods would not be accused—by people who did not understand the inexpressible mystery of the Father and the Holy Spirit—of having fallen into a plurality of gods. It was therefore most rightly established that, since the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are of one Godhead, one nature, one equality, and finally one power, one person among them should be invoked in the sacrifice, so that the mystery of unity might be maintained. And it should be no other person than the first, the Father, in whom the other two persons exist, naturally abiding.
As for the fact that we invoke the Father through the Son, when we say to him, “Through Jesus Christ your Son,” this is to be understood, as was said earlier, in relation to the role by which the Son performed the office of priest for us. That was when he offered to God the Father the spotless victim of his own flesh on the altar of the cross. Hence the chosen vessel says, “Consider Jesus, the apostle and high priest of our confession, who is faithful to the one who made him,” in the letter to the Hebrews, chapter three, verse one. Therefore, since he was made priest for us, he must intercede for us; but he does this, as we have said, in the respect in which he bears human nature. That is why the apostle attached this mystery of Christ’s intercession not to his divinity, but to his humanity, when he said, “For there is one mediator between God and human beings, the human being Christ Jesus,” in Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse three.
That prayer and adoration are offered to the Son just as they are to the Father is shown by Isaiah, who says, “The nations will pray to him, and his name will be glorious,” in the book of Isaiah, chapter eleven, verse ten. And again, where he introduces God the Father speaking to the Son, he says, “The labor of Egypt, the trade of Ethiopia and of the Sabaeans, lofty men, will pass over to you and will be yours. They will walk after you, bound in chains; and they will adore you and pray to you, because God is in you, and there is no God apart from you,” in the book of Isaiah, chapter forty-five, verse fourteen. Likewise in Joel: “Everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved,” in the book of Joel, chapter two, verse thirty-two. The apostle teaches that this was said about the Son when, reproaching the unbelief of the Jews, he says, “Everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved. How will they call upon him in whom they have not believed?” in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter ten, verse thirteen. David too prays to the Son, the one confessed as seated on the highest thrones, when he says, “You who sit upon the cherubim, appear before Ephraim, Benjamin, and Manasseh. Stir up your power and come, and save us,” in Psalm seventy-nine, verse three.
Listen also to the Holy Spirit, and hear how he commands the sacrifice of praise to be offered to the Son, to the Father, and to himself. Here the truly undivided Trinity is shown more clearly together with the distinction of the persons. The same Holy Spirit says in the Psalm: “Offer to God the sacrifice of praise, and pay your vows to the Most High; and call upon me in the day of your trouble, and I will rescue you, and you will glorify me,” in Psalm forty-nine, verse fourteen. See, then: the Holy Spirit urges worshipers of the truth to offer the sacrifice of praise to God the Son, vows to the Most High Father, and to himself the offering of invocation and glorification.
[Augustine] For the apostle says about him: “Do you not know that your bodies are the temple within you of the Holy Spirit, whom you have from God, and that you are not your own? For you were bought at a great price. Glorify and carry God in your body,” in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter six, verse fifteen. What God does he mean, if not the Holy Spirit, whose temple he had said our bodies are?
[Vigilius] Glory, then, is owed to the Holy Spirit. And that the angelic Powers praise and glorify the Son together with the Father is shown to us in Deuteronomy, where the heavenly army is invited to venerate the Son in this way: “Rejoice, heavens, together with him, and let all the angels of God adore him. Rejoice, nations, with his people, and let all the sons of God glorify him.” Likewise in the Book of Revelation, blessed John first saw the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders fall down before the Lamb, who had opened the sealed book, saying, “You are worthy to receive the book and to open its seals, because you were slain and redeemed us for God by your blood.” Then he added the voices of the heavenly host rejoicing over our redemption, and said: “And I saw and heard the voice of many angels around the throne; and their number was thousands of thousands, saying with a loud voice: Worthy is the Lamb who was slain to receive power and divinity and wisdom and strength and honor and glory and blessing,” in the Book of Revelation, chapter five, verses nine through twelve.
After this, he says, “I saw, and behold, a great multitude, which no one could count, from every nation and tribe and people and language, standing before the throne and in the sight of the Lamb, clothed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands. And they cried out with a loud voice, saying: Salvation belongs to our God, who sits upon the throne, and to the Lamb,” in the Book of Revelation, chapter seven, verses nine through ten. See how the choir of saints offers the sacrifice of praise without distinction to the Father and to the Son. The multitude of angels confirms their voices by adoring and by answering, “Amen,” just as the same book immediately continues: “And all the angels stood around the throne, and they fell on their faces before the throne and adored God, saying: Amen,” in the Book of Revelation, chapter seven, verse eleven. Blessed Peter also proclaims the Holy Spirit in the same height of glory when he says, “The Holy Spirit sent from heaven, into whom the angels long to gaze.” The priest then continues and says:
“Together with them, we pray that you command our voices also to be admitted.” [Gregory] The voices of the angels, in praise of the Creator, are their very wonder in inward contemplation. So what kind of voices must ours be, when we pray that they may be admitted—that is, brought in—before God together with the praises of the angels? We should be able to say, “Let it enter into your sight.” And again: “Let my prayer come to you, to your holy temple.” Certainly voices of this kind, and a cry of this kind, do not consist in the sound of the mouth, but in the desire of the heart. For in the most hidden ears of God, it is not our words that make our voices, but our desires. If we ask for eternal life with our mouth, but do not desire it, then even while crying out we are silent. But if we desire it from the heart, then even when we are silent with the mouth, we cry out in silence. In this way Moses was silent, without the noise of words; and yet, while silent, he was heard by the ear of divine mercy. That is why it is said to him, “Why do you cry out to me?” in the book of Exodus, chapter fourteen, verse fifteen. So inwardly, in desire, there is a hidden cry. It does not reach human ears, and yet it fills the hearing of the Creator.
And because that heavenly city is made up of angels and human beings, and because we believe that the human race will ascend to it in a number equal to the elect angels who remained there, as Scripture says, “He established the boundaries of the nations according to the number of the angels of God,” the holy Church is right to praise God already now on earth with the very voices with which the holy angels praise him in heaven. For the Church is to be joined with them in heaven and to remain with them in the praises of God. It does this not with proud presumption, but with humble confession, desiring to believe in and confess the majesty of God in this world as it is seen and praised by the angels in heaven. This confession is not the confession of sin, about which we are commanded, “Confess your sins to one another,” in the letter of James, chapter five, verse sixteen. And the psalmist says, “I said: I will confess my injustice against myself,” in Psalm thirty-one, verse five. Rather, it is the confession of praise and thanksgiving, as we understand it in the Psalm where we sing, “Let them confess the mercies of the Lord, and his wonders to the children of men,” in Psalm one hundred six, verse eight. It is the kind of confession that the Lord Jesus, who had no sin at all, offers to God the Father in the Gospel when he says, “I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because you have hidden these things from the wise and revealed them to little ones,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter eleven, verse twenty-three. Therefore, through one confession—that is, through praise and thanksgiving—they are already now joined with the angels, praising God concerning heavenly and earthly things, as the one who made heaven and earth, and saying:
“Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Sabaoth, heaven and earth are full.” These are the words of the angels in heaven as they sing this hymn of God’s glory. The prophet Isaiah testifies that he heard them when he saw the glory of God and heard the seraphim praising him together with these words. And blessed John, in Revelation, presents the same praise under the figure of the four living creatures. For in Isaiah we read this about the seraphim as they give praise: “And they cried out, one to another, and said: Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Sabaoth; the whole earth is full of his glory”—the book of Isaiah, chapter six, verse three. But in Revelation we read this about the four living creatures: “And they had no rest, day or night, saying: Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, who is, and who was, and who is to come”—the book of Revelation, chapter four, verse eight.
So it is clear that, in these words of divine praise—which the Church sings in imitation of the heavenly powers, following Isaiah—one word has been added from Revelation. In Isaiah we read only, “Lord Sabaoth”; but in Revelation we read, “Lord God Almighty.” Therefore, it is not by human choice, but by the joined authority of both Scriptures, that we say: “Lord God Sabaoth.” Likewise, in Isaiah we read, “The whole earth is full of his glory,” as though the angels were saying this not directly to God, but to one another about him. But we turn our voice toward him and say: “Heaven and earth are full of your glory.” This too is not something presumed by human opinion; it is taken and added from another Scripture, where God himself speaks through the prophet Jeremiah and says: “I fill heaven and earth.” And whatever Scripture says in praise of God may rightly be said both to God himself, in joyful thanksgiving, and about God to others, in exhortation or instruction.
[Jerome] Now Sabaoth is one of the ten names of God among the Hebrews. In Latin it is translated “of armies,” or “of powers,” as it is often used in the prophets: “Thus says the Lord of armies.” And in the Psalm: “The Lord of powers, he is the King of glory”—Psalm twenty-three, verse ten. He is truly called Lord of armies and Lord of powers because every host of the heavenly army, all the powers above and the angelic authorities, are subject to his command and serve his will.
[Gregory] We rightly call the angelic spirits an army, because we know that they fight against the powers of the air. Yet they carry out these battles not by toil, but by command, because whatever they desire to do against unclean spirits, they are able to do through the help of the one who governs all things. For of this army, when our King was born, it is written: “Suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly army”—the Gospel of Luke, chapter three, verse thirteen.
[Ambrose] What, then, is the meaning, in this angelic hymn, of the threefold repetition of the one word “holy”? If it is repeated three times, why is there only one praise? And if there is only one praise, why is it repeated three times—unless because the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are one in holiness? It was not said once, lest the Son be set apart. It was not said twice, lest the Holy Spirit be passed over. It was not said four times, lest a creature be joined to them. And to show that there is one Godhead, after saying for the third time, “Holy, holy, holy,” it adds in the singular, “Lord Sabaoth.” Therefore the Father is holy, the Son is holy, and the Spirit of God is holy; that is, the whole Trinity is adored and praised by the angels. The seraphim glorify this Trinity, as do all the heavenly authorities and powers. And to this hymn of the angels they add words taken from the Gospel: the words of the crowds praising God and our King as he came to Jerusalem, saying:
“Hosanna in the highest. Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord. Hosanna in the highest.” In this way the full praise is completed from prophetic and Gospel Scripture: after the praise and glory of the holy Trinity, thanksgiving is also added for the coming of the Savior. He, who is one within the Trinity and from the Trinity, became human and came into the world for our salvation; and he brought that same salvation to completion by dying and rising again. “For he died for our offenses, and rose again for our justification”—Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter fourteen, verse twenty-five. Therefore, giving thanks to him rightly, we say “Hosanna,” that is, salvation in the highest.
[Vigilius] For this is what is sung in the Psalm: “Salvation belongs to the Lord, and your blessing is upon your people”—Psalm three, verse nine. This is what the choir of the saints resounds in Revelation, in the devotion of great praise: “Salvation belongs to our God, who sits upon the throne, and to the Lamb”—the book of Revelation, chapter seven, verse ten. We also rightly add, “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord,” in keeping with what he said in the Gospel to the unbelieving Jews: “I came in the name of my Father, and you did not receive me”—the Gospel of John, chapter five, verse forty-five. The crowds take up this little verse of praise from Psalm one hundred seventeen, which is clearly sung about the Lord. When the crowds say “Hosanna,” it is the same as what is said there: “O Lord, save me! O Lord, give good success!” And immediately it adds: “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord”—Psalm one hundred seventeen, verse twenty-five.
And when, in the continuation of that same praise, “Hosanna” is added—that is, salvation, or saving power, in the highest—it openly teaches that the Lord’s coming in the flesh is salvation not only for the human race on earth, but also for the angels in heaven. For as we are brought upward toward the things above, the number of the angels, which had been diminished when Satan fell away, is surely filled up. Hence Paul says: “All things are restored in Christ, the things in heaven and the things on earth in him”—Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter one, verse ten. Therefore “Hosanna in the highest” is rightly sung in praise of him, since the whole saving plan of his incarnation appeared in order to complete the glory of the heavenly homeland.
After these praises and thanksgivings for the great grace of our redemption—which is enacted and commended in that divine mystery—the whole Church falls silent. All noise of words ceases, so that only the mind’s attention and the devotion of hearts may be directed to God. Then, joining to himself the prayers and desires of all, the priest begins to pour out the prayer by which the mystery itself of the Lord’s body and blood is consecrated. For this is fitting: at that hour of so sacred and divine an action, by God’s grace the whole mind is separated from earthly thoughts, and the Church with the priest, and the priest with the Church, enter by spiritual desire into the heavenly and eternal sanctuary of God. And because “God is spirit, and those who adore him must adore in spirit and truth”—the Gospel of John, chapter four, verse twenty-four—and because the Father seeks such people to adore him, the priest prays to that same God the Father in this way, and says:
