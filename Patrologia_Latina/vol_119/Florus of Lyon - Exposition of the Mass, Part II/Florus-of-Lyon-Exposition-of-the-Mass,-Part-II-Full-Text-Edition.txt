
by Florus of Lyon
Patrologia Latina Volume 


Exposition of the Mass

“Therefore, most merciful Father,” as far as, “these holy and spotless sacrifices.” So this prayer, like the other prayers, is directed according to the rule of faith and the custom of the Church: it is addressed to the most merciful Father. His mercy and compassion toward us, sinners and unworthy as we are, has been as great as the Apostle praises when he says, “He did not spare even his own Son, but handed him over for us all; how will he not also, along with him, give us everything?” This is in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verse thirty-two.

And again Scripture says, “But God, who is rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, brought us to life together with Christ; through this grace we have received the spirit of adoption.” This is cited as Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter two, verse four. The Apostle praises that same grace when he says, “But you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, in whom we cry: Abba, Father.” This is in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verse fifteen.

Therefore, in this Spirit of adoption, who pleads for us with sighs too deep for words, the priest cries out with the Church—not with his voice, but with his heart—saying, “Therefore, most merciful Father, we humbly pray and beseech.” The Spirit himself makes those whom he deigns to fill sigh and pray with an inexpressible longing.

This humble prayer, this bending of the heart toward God, must be filled with such reverence and humility that it can imitate what the Apostle says about his own prayer: “For this reason I bend my knees to the Father, from whom every fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named.” This is in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter three, verse fourteen. In that bending of the knees, we should understand not the prostration of the body, but a humble disposition and a contrite heart. About this the Psalmist says, “A sacrifice to God is a troubled spirit; a contrite and humbled heart, O God, you will not despise.” This is Psalm fifty, verse nineteen.

In this sense, then, the prayer says, “we humbly pray and beseech.” And it does this through Jesus Christ, your Son, our Lord. Through him every humble prayer and every request of ours must be directed to God, because he is the true mediator and the eternal priest, who sits at the right hand of the Father and intercedes for us.

But for what does the priest say to God the Father, “we pray and beseech”? He says it for what follows: that God would regard as accepted—that is, as pleasing to him—and would bless the gifts, holy offerings, and sacrifices that are offered to him. For it belongs to priests to offer and to call upon the majesty of God. But it belongs to God graciously to receive what is offered and to bless it. This is just as he commands the priests in the Law, saying, “In this way you shall bless the children of Israel, and you shall say to them,” and after the words of blessing he adds, “They shall invoke my name over the children of Israel, and I will bless them.” This is the Book of Numbers, chapter six, verses twenty-three and twenty-seven.

What could be clearer? They will invoke, and I will bless. And yet God had already said to them, “In this way you shall bless.” They bless by pleading; he blesses by giving. So too, in the offering of the divine sacrifice, priests carry out the ministry of offering and supplication. But God gives the gift of blessing through the one true priest: through him he sanctifies what is offered, and through him he accepts what has been sanctified. Therefore they say:
“That you would hold them as accepted and bless them.” It is as though they were humbly saying: We ask you to sanctify these things by your Spirit and bless them by your own word, so that what is carried out through the ministry of our humility may be fulfilled by the power of your strength.

Then the prayer adds, “these gifts, these offerings, these holy and spotless sacrifices.” This does not mean one thing after another, as if they were separate realities. Rather, one single reality is being praised with different names because of its greatness; and by being praised, it is commended.

For the things offered on the divine altars are called “offerings,” as the Lord says in the Gospel: “If you are presenting your offering at the altar.” This is the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five, verse twenty-three. They are also called “gifts” or “sacrifices,” as the Apostle shows when he says, “For every priest taken from among human beings is appointed for human beings in the things that pertain to God, so that he may offer gifts and sacrifices for sins.” This is the letter to the Hebrews, chapter five, verse one.

But, as was said, the very repetition of the words is a commendation of so great a sacrament and a stirring up of devout affection. For these are truly “holy” and “spotless”—that is, inviolate and undefiled—sacrifices. No words can praise them worthily. They are offered in the mystery of the Lord’s body. They were prefigured by those victims of the old Law, which had to be offered immaculate and without any defect. Such a victim was found in Christ alone, who was entirely without sin. The priest continues and says:
“Which we offer to you in the first place,” as far as, “your servant, our pope.” [Augustine]

It was entirely fitting that the holy offering should begin in this way: namely, for the holy catholic Church of God, spread throughout the whole world. For she is the whole people redeemed by the blood of the Lord. She is the inheritance promised and given to him by the Father, as the Father himself says in the Psalm: “I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession.” This is Psalm two, verse eight.

This unity of the body of Christ is the temple of God. To it the Apostle says, “For the temple of God is holy, and you are that temple.” This is Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter three, verse seventeen. This refers to all who believe in Christ, and who believe in such a way that they love. For to believe in Christ means to love Christ. They are like living stones from which the Lord’s temple is built.

This temple is the place where God is prayed to and where he hears. For whoever prays to God outside the temple is not heard for that heavenly peace; he is not heard for eternal life. Eternal life is granted only to the one who prays in the temple of God—that is, the one who prays in the peace of the Church, in the unity of the body of Christ. And that body of Christ is made up of the many believers throughout the whole world.

Therefore, the one who prays in the temple is heard, because he prays in spirit and in truth. Rightly, then, the divine offering begins from the Church and for the Church, and in the Church, as though it were said to God concerning her and within her: “Hear us, O God our Savior, the hope of all the ends of the earth and of those far away at sea.” This is Psalm sixty-four, verse six.

For she is that unity which cries out to the Lord from the ends of the earth—that is, from east and west, north and south—and says from the ends of the earth, “I cried to you when my heart was in anguish.” This is Psalm sixty, verse three.
[Isidore] This is also why the Church is called “Ecclesia” in Greek, a word translated into Latin as “convocation,” because she calls all people together to herself. She is also called “catholic,” meaning “of the whole,” or universal. For she is not confined to some regional corner, as the little gatherings of heretics are. Instead, she is spread abroad and extended throughout the whole world.

For this reason too, “universality” is named from “oneness,” because it is gathered into unity. Hence the Lord says in the Gospel, “Whoever does not gather with me scatters.” That is: whoever is not gathered into my unity is separated from me and is dispersed into the ruin of scattering.

Therefore, in that offering, prayer is made first for the Church: that God would graciously give her peace throughout the whole world. That means that her unity may not be torn apart by heresies and schisms, and that her tranquillity may not be disturbed by the hardship of persecutions. The Apostle himself prays that God would give this peace, both inwardly and outwardly, to his Church when he says, “May the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.” This is Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter four, verse seven.

And he urges the whole Church to pray when he says, “Therefore, I desire first of all that supplications, prayers, petitions, and thanksgivings be made.” And a little later he adds, “so that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all godliness.” This is Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse one.

Almighty God is also asked to deign to guard, unite, and govern the Church. For he is the true guardian of his people, that is, of the true Israel. About him the Psalm sings, “Behold, he who guards Israel has not slumbered and will not sleep.” This is Psalm one hundred twenty, verse three. And again, “Unless the Lord guards the city, the one who guards it keeps watch in vain.” This is Psalm one hundred twenty-six, verse one.

Now to unite seems to mean the same thing as to bring peace, and to guard seems to mean the same thing as to govern. For God unites his Church by giving her peace, and gives her peace by uniting her. He governs her by guarding her, and guards her by governing her.

He unites her by giving peace when he joins the hearts of all the faithful in his peace, and when, through the unity of faith, hope, and charity, he makes the minds of all the faithful share one will. Then, through the Holy Spirit, with charity poured out, what is written is fulfilled in her: “The multitude of believers had one heart and one soul.” This is the Acts of the Apostles, chapter four, verse thirty-two. And again: “Behold how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to dwell together in unity.” This is Psalm one hundred thirty-one, verse one.

He also governs that same Church by guarding her, when amid every danger he mercifully guides and directs her. Praying to him in the Psalm, we say, “Save your people, and bless your inheritance; govern them and lift them up forever.” This is Psalm twenty-seven, verse eight.
After the general commemoration of the Church, the prayer adds, “together with your servant, our pope.” By this, the unity of the Church’s body is emphasized even more strongly. For, as blessed Leo writes, our Lord Jesus Christ, the Savior of the human race, established the worship of the divine religion—which God’s grace willed to shine among all peoples and all nations—in such a way that he placed the sacrament of this gift chiefly in the most blessed Peter, the chief of the apostles. From Peter, as from a kind of head, Christ willed his gifts to flow into the whole body. Thus anyone who dared to withdraw from Peter’s firmness would understand that he had made himself a stranger to the divine mystery. For Christ took Peter into the fellowship of an undivided unity, and willed that Peter should be called by the very name that Christ himself was, saying, “You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my Church.” This is the Gospel of Matthew, chapter fifteen, verse eighteen. And so it is clear, as blessed Pope Pelagius also teaches, that those who, because of any division whatever, do not customarily mention the apostolic pontiff during the sacred mysteries are separated from the communion of the whole world. The mystery continues:
“And with our bishop, and with all who are orthodox and who worship according to the apostolic faith.” Just as the commemoration of the universal Church is joined to the commemoration of the apostolic pontiff, for the sake of unity in fellowship and peace, so too it is fitting and religiously right that individual Churches add the commemoration of their own bishops. By that prayer of the Lord’s offering, they show that they keep with their bishops the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. The Greek word orthodoxoi means, in Latin, “people of right glory,” because they have not been corrupted by any heretical teaching, but glorify God by confessing the true faith. These same people, then, are worshipers according to the catholic and apostolic faith: catholic, that is, universal, the faith that the whole Church everywhere preserves; and apostolic as well, because the teaching of the apostles established it throughout the whole world.
But people cannot truly be called worshipers according to this faith unless they sincerely love what they rightly believe. For each person worships what he loves most. That is why the Apostle says of certain lovers of gluttony and of the belly, “Their god is their belly.” This is Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter three, verse nineteen. So the worshiper of truth is the person who joins love to faith: by loving God, in whom he rightly believes, he worships him; and by worshiping him, he becomes worthy of reward. This is the faith the Apostle praises when he says, “For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision counts for anything, nor uncircumcision, but faith working through love.” This is Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter six, verse fifteen.
When this prayer is completed—the prayer in which, first of all, the universal Church, together with the apostolic pontiff and all the orthodox, is commended to God—the usual conclusion is added: “Through Christ our Lord.” For neither the Church’s prayer nor the Church’s offering can be presented to God in any other way; nor can the Church receive the things for which God is asked, except through the one mediator between God and human beings, who intercedes for us. Therefore, whatever the Church offers, she offers through him. And whatever God graciously grants—that he would pacify, guard, and unite that same Church—he grants through him. Through Christ our prayers rise up to God; through Christ God’s gifts come down to us. For Christ himself teaches this in the Gospel when he says, “Because I am going to the Father, and whatever you ask in my name, I will do this, so that the Father may be glorified in the Son.” This is the Gospel of John, chapter twelve, verse twenty-three. The Church preserves this pattern of petition in every one of her prayers. The mystery continues:
“Remember, Lord, your servants, men and women, and all who stand around, whose faith is known to you and whose devotion is evident to you.” In these words, first it says, “Remember, Lord, your servants, men and women,” and then it adds, “and all who stand around.” This makes clear that there is, as it were, a place in the prayer where certain people may be named individually, and then the commemoration of the others who are present in the Church is added. So at this point, either the priest is free to name particular people whom he wishes and to commend them to God by name, or else the practice observed by the ancients was that the names of those making offerings were recited there. Blessed Pope Innocent seems to indicate this in one of his letters, where he says, “It is unnecessary to mention beforehand the name of a person whose offering you have not yet presented to God, even though nothing is unknown to him. The offerings must first be commended, and only then should the names of those to whom they belong be announced, so that they may be named during the sacred mysteries—not among the other things we say beforehand—so that by the mysteries themselves we may open the way for the prayers that are to follow.” This prayer, then, is directed to God the Father: that he would graciously remember his servants, men and women—that is, people of both sexes who serve him—not only those who are named, but also all who stand around. And about all of them, and for all of them together, it is said, “whose faith is known to you and whose devotion is evident to you.” That is: how rightly they believe, and how devoutly they love you, you alone see in each person’s conscience. For you search hearts and innermost desires, and it is truly said to you, “You alone know the hearts of the children of humankind.” You seek above all, in those who worship you, the offering of faith and devotion: the offering of faith, when you say through your lawgiver, “Hear, Israel: the Lord your God is one God.” This is the book of Deuteronomy, chapter six, verse four. And the offering of devotion, when he immediately adds, “You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart.” This is the book of Deuteronomy, chapter six, verse five. To offer this faith and devotion to God—this is truly a saving sacrifice: it means paying attention to his commandments. The mystery continues:
“They offer to you this sacrifice of praise, and to you they render their vows, to the eternal God, living and true.” In these words we should notice that the whole Church offers that sacrifice of praise to God, because the prayer says of the entire multitude of faithful people standing around, “They offer to you.” What is carried out in the proper sense by the ministry of priests is, in a general sense, done by the faith and devotion of everyone. For blessed Peter the apostle says to the whole Church, “You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood.” This is Peter’s first letter, chapter two, verse nine. And he speaks of offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ. Therefore he calls the whole Church a holy priesthood—a name and office which, under the law, belonged only to the house of Aaron—because all the faithful are members of the high priest. All are signed with the oil of gladness and united to the body of the one who is the highest king and the true priest. As king, Christ gives his own people a kingdom; and as high priest, he cleanses their sins by the sacrifice of his own blood. Yet even in the law of the Old Testament, the mystery of this spiritual priesthood, by which the whole Church is consecrated, is clearly shown. For the lamb to be slaughtered at Passover—which is Christ, sacrificed for you—was commanded to be sacrificed not by the priests alone, but by the whole people in general. The Lord says, “The whole multitude of the children of Israel shall sacrifice it toward evening.” This is the book of Exodus, chapter twelve, verse six. The same law teaches that this lamb, sacrificed in the solemnity of Passover, is a victim, a sacrificial offering, and a sacrifice, when the Lord commands and says, “You shall not sacrifice the blood of my victim upon leaven, nor shall any of the victim of the solemnity of Passover remain until morning.” This is the book of Exodus, chapter thirty-four, verse twenty-five. And again he says, “Anyone who is unclean in his soul, or far away on a journey among your people, shall keep the Passover to the Lord in the second month.” This is the book of Numbers, chapter nine, verse ten. And a little later: “But if anyone is clean, and was not on a journey, and still did not keep the Passover, that soul shall be cut off from his people, because he did not offer the sacrifice to the Lord at its proper time.” This is the book of Numbers, chapter nine, verse thirteen. Therefore the priests of the Church offer; and through them and in them, the whole Church offers to God the sacrifice of praise—the sacrifice that God himself commanded to be offered to him by those who worship him, saying, “Offer to God the sacrifice of praise, and render your vows to the Most High.” This is Psalm forty-nine, verse fourteen.
[Augustine] God does not ask praise from us because he needs it. Rather, through our praise he provides for our salvation. As the same psalm says again: “The sacrifice of praise will honor me, and there is the way by which I will show my salvation.” This is cited as Psalm forty-nine, verse thirty-three.

And what is God’s salvation, if not the Son of God, the Savior of the world? He himself, both priest and victim, fulfilled the sacrifice of praise: he grants forgiveness for evil deeds, and gives the grace to do good. The Lord is offered this sacrifice of praise by those who worship him so that whoever boasts may boast in the Lord.

So this sacrifice of praise—that is, the offering of the Lord’s Passion—is the sacrifice in which we recognize no merits of our own, but praise only the grace of God. For in this the love of God appeared among us: God sent his only-begotten Son into the world so that we might live through him. It was not that we loved God first, but that he first loved us and sent his Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins.

The devotion of the faithful offers this sacrifice for themselves and for all who belong to them: those connected with them by birth, by household relationship, or by any other bond, so long as they share the same profession of faith. This includes both the living and the dead.

The prayer then adds the hope with which the faithful do this. They do not offer for some earthly desire or temporary gain, but only “for the redemption of their souls,” because, as the Apostle says, “in him we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins”; and “for the hope of their salvation and well-being”—that is, for the hope of eternal salvation. For we have been saved in hope. And if we hope for what we do not yet see, we wait for it with patience.

But they offer not only for eternal salvation; they also offer for temporal well-being, meaning bodily health. “Well-being” means the wholeness of health. Both kinds of salvation—the salvation of the soul and the health of the body—come from the one of whom the psalm sings, “Salvation belongs to the Lord.” And it belongs to true religious devotion to expect both from him, as the Apostle testifies when he says, “Godliness is useful for all things, holding the promise of the life that now is and of the life to come.”

“They render their vows to you,” he says, “the eternal God, living and true,” in keeping with the psalm’s words: “And render your vows to the Most High.” These are the vows of faith and reverent devotion, by which we vow and render our very selves to God. Anyone who thinks carefully about what he has vowed to God, and what vows he must fulfill, will see that this is what is required and owed: he must vow and render himself. Let Caesar’s image be rendered to Caesar; let God’s image be rendered to God.

But just as we must ask what the faithful offer, we must also consider where they offer it. For there is no place for the true sacrifice outside the catholic Church. For this reason, the mystery very fittingly adds the following words.
“In communion with, and honoring the memory, first of all,” and so on, as far as, “and of all your saints.”

[Augustine] Who are the ones said to be “in communion with” and “honoring the memory” of the blessed Mother of God, and of the apostles, martyrs, and all the saints? They are the priest who offers, and all who stand around him, who together offer one sacrifice of praise.

For this sacrifice to be acceptable and pleasing to God, the priests and the Church together with the priests offer it nowhere except in the communion and fellowship of the saints. In those saints, the same almighty God willed the foundation of his Church to stand firm. As the Apostle says, “You are fellow citizens of the saints and members of the household of God, built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets.” This is Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter two, verse nineteen.

Therefore, in communion with them, and in reverent remembrance of them, the devotion of the faithful offers its vows and gifts to God, as if in the true temple of God. In this way the faithful become sharers in their memory, like children of the fathers and followers of those who went before them.

For what does “in communion with” mean, if not “holding communion and fellowship”? And what does it mean to honor their memory, if not to follow their footsteps with reverence and humility?

The blessed apostle John invites us into this fellowship when he says, “What we have seen and heard, we proclaim to you.” And as if we had asked what benefit that proclamation brings us, he says, “so that you also may have fellowship with us.” Then, so that no one would think this fellowship could be despised as merely human, he immediately adds, “And our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son,” Jesus Christ.

By this he clearly shows that all who desire fellowship with God must first be united to the fellowship of the Church. They must learn that faith, and be formed by those sacraments, which the apostles received from Truth itself while he was present in the flesh. For that faith the holy martyrs suffered, and upon it the catholic Church is founded—the Church for whom the childbearing of the blessed and glorious Virgin became the beginning of salvation.
[Augustine] She is truly believed to be ever-virgin. For if her bodily integrity had been violated when the Lord was born, then he would no longer have been born of a virgin. In that case, which God forbid, the whole Church would falsely confess that he was born of the Virgin Mary.

For the salvation of the human race, he truly became man. Yet he was granted this unique gift: from the very moment when he began to be conceived in the Virgin’s womb and to become man, he was also true God. Therefore the same glorious ever-virgin Mary must truly be believed and confessed to be the Mother of God.

So she is placed first in this venerable commemoration of the saints, because through her both they and we were found worthy to receive the Author of life. Joined to her is the communion of the blessed apostles and martyrs—not only those who are named here one by one, but all those whose number and names we cannot know.

And yet, by the grace of faith and love, if we devoutly cling to those few and foremost saints who are named, we are joined, in their communion, even to those whom we cannot know. We keep the fellowship of the Spirit in the bond of peace, so that with them we may be one body and one spirit.

The mystery continues:
“By whose merits and prayers may you grant.”

For because of the merits and intercessions of those exalted righteous ones—whom we seek as intercessors and patrons—the Lord has mercy on our weakness and is gracious to us. Thus in every circumstance, whether adverse or favorable, we are fortified by the help of his protection: so that in hardships we do not collapse, and in prosperity we are not deceived.
[Vigilius] Finally, when Hezekiah was besieged by enemies and called upon God’s help, God said to him: “I will save this city and protect it, for my own sake and for the sake of my servant David.” This is cited as the Fourth Book of Kings, chapter thirty-one, verse six.

In the same way, when Moses interceded for the sinful people, he recalled the fathers and tried to appease the Lord by praying: “Let your anger rest,” he said, “and be appeased concerning the wickedness of your people. Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, your servants, to whom you swore by your own self, saying: I will multiply your offspring like the stars of heaven.” This is the Book of Exodus, chapter thirty-two, verse thirteen.

Nor should we doubt that the requests of the faithful are known to the spirits of the righteous. Those who behold the brightness of almighty God within cannot be thought ignorant of anything outside.

Therefore the Church asks for the support of the holy martyrs. Yet she does not set up altars on which she sacrifices to the martyrs, but to the one God who is both their God and ours.

[Augustine] In that sacrifice, the human beings of God who conquered the world by confessing him are named in their proper place and order. But they are not invoked by the priest who offers the sacrifice. For he sacrifices to God, not to them, even though he sacrifices at their memorials, because he is God’s priest, not theirs. The sacrifice itself is Christ’s sacrifice; it is not offered to them, because they themselves are also this sacrifice.

The mystery continues:
“Therefore this offering of our service,” and so on, as far as, “Through Christ our Lord.”

In these words the unity of the Church as she makes the offering is shown. For in that most holy offering, one common service is rendered to God both by the priests and by the whole family of God’s household. [Augustine]

This is the service owed to no one else: not to the holy angels, not to holy souls, but only to the one living and true God. About him we are commanded, as the Gospel of Matthew, chapter four, verse ten, says: “You shall worship the Lord your God, and serve him alone.”

For even if a sacrifice is offered by a human being, sacrifice is still a divine reality. That is why the divine law also commands elsewhere: “Whoever sacrifices to gods shall be rooted out, unless he sacrifices to the Lord alone.”

And so those immortal and blessed beings who dwell in the heavenly places are right to refuse sacrifices from us. In mercy they love us, who are mortal and miserable, so that we may become immortal and blessed. They do not want us to sacrifice to them, but to him whose sacrifice they know both they and we belong to. For together with them we are one city of God—as it says in the psalm, “Glorious things are said of you, city of God.” One part of this city is still on pilgrimage in us; another part already helps us in them.

So God is asked to be appeased and to accept this offering, which the Church brings to him alone in the service that is owed to him. For this is the true offering: in it the Son is offered, and the Father is reconciled. As the Apostle says, “Christ loved us and handed himself over for us, as an offering and a sacrifice to God, in the fragrance of sweetness.”

And, appeased by such an offering, may God order our days in his peace—the days in which we live in this mortal life amid many temptations and attacks. May he govern everything by his loving care, so that we can bear it, so that we do not fall away from his peace, and so that, even though we have distress in the world, we may have peace in him. And when the course of this mortal life is finished, may he rescue us from eternal damnation and graciously count us among the flock of his chosen ones. [Gregory]

For even the predestination of that everlasting kingdom has been arranged by the Almighty in such a way that the chosen reach it through labor. By asking, they are made worthy to receive what almighty God arranged before all ages to give them.

The mystery continues:
“Which offering you, O God, in all things,” and so on.

Almighty God is asked to make the offering placed upon his holy altars, and commended by so many prayers, into a lawful and perfect Eucharist by the power of the Spirit who descends. It is to be “ascribed” in all things—that is, received and counted among the things pleasing to him. It is also to be “ratified”—that is, established forever with unshakable firmness. For something is called ratified when it is firm and unmoved by an unchangeable principle.

And by the working power of the same Holy Spirit, this offering is to be “reasonable.” Through all these qualities it becomes uniquely pleasing and acceptable to God. Though taken from the simple fruits of the earth, by the unspeakable power of divine blessing it becomes the body and blood of the only-begotten Son of God. He himself bears witness to this when he says, in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse fifty-six: “My flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink.” Through this food and drink, what he promises is fulfilled in the minds of those who receive it. He says, in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse fifty-eight: “As the living Father sent me, and I live because of the Father, so the one who eats me will also live because of me.”

This, then, is the true and eternal sacrifice, because its power is true and eternal, and through it true and eternal salvation is accomplished. For it belongs to the new and eternal covenant. This is the reasonable sacrifice: full of reason, full of mystery. It is not celebrated by bodily slaughter, as happened in the sacrifices of old, but by spiritual meaning; and it is offered in the purity of faith, in keeping with the faithful Apostle’s command that our worship should be reasonable. For when the Apostle said this, he was certainly speaking about spiritual sacrifice.

That is why he says elsewhere, in Paul’s letter to the Hebrews, chapter nine, verse twenty-three: “It is necessary, then, for the copies of the heavenly things to be cleansed with these”; but the heavenly realities themselves must be cleansed with better sacrifices than these. The hearts of the faithful had to become heavenly. Just as they had borne the image of the earthly man, they had to bear also the image of the one who is from heaven; and as he is heavenly, they too had to become heavenly. Therefore they had to be cleansed, not by the blood of irrational animals, but by the reasonable blood of Christ. As the same Apostle testifies, Christ offered himself spotless to God through the Holy Spirit.

This is renewed for us every day in the sacrament of the body and blood of the Son of God. [Augustine]

This body and this blood are not gathered from ears of grain or from vine branches; rather, by a definite consecration they become mystical. They do not grow naturally for us, since the created reality of bread and wine is transferred into the sacrament of his flesh and blood by the ineffable sanctification of the Spirit. But what is not made in this way, even though it is bread and cup, is food for bodily refreshment, not the sacrament of religion with which Christ filled the divine altar.

So the Lord invited his servants, and he prepared food for them: himself. Who would dare to eat his Lord? And yet he says, in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse fifty-eight: “The one who eats me lives because of me.”

When Christ is eaten, life is eaten. He is not killed so that he may be eaten; rather, when he is eaten, he gives life to the dead. He refreshes, but he is not used up. Christ is eaten, and, having been eaten, he lives, because, having been killed, he rose again. Nor, when we eat, do we divide him into pieces. And yet in the sacrament it happens in this way: in the sacrament he is eaten in portions, and still he remains whole and entire in heaven, and whole and entire in his own heart.

The mystery continues:
“Who, on the day before he suffered, took bread,” and so on, as far as, “As often as you do these things, you will do them in memory of me.”

The Lord himself handed on these words to the apostles, and the apostles handed them on to the whole Church, so that the universal Church might celebrate the continual memorial of her Lord and Redeemer. Without these words, no language, no region, no city—that is, no part of the catholic Church—can perform, that is, consecrate, the sacrament of the body and blood of the Lord. The Apostle shows this clearly in his first letter to the Corinthians, where he says: “For I received from the Lord what I also handed on to you: that the Lord Jesus, on the night he was handed over, took bread,” and so on.

Therefore this bread and cup were consecrated from the beginning by the power and words of Christ. By the power and words of Christ they are always consecrated, and always will be consecrated. He speaks every day in his priests. It is his word that sanctifies the heavenly sacraments. They carry out the office; he works by the majesty of divine power.

For he is the true Melchizedek. The holy sacrifices that Melchizedek once prefigured in mystery, Christ fulfilled by the offering of his own body and his own blood. He himself, by the power of the Spirit, the Paraclete, and by heavenly blessing, makes them to be his holy body and blood.

When he says of the bread, “Take and eat from this, all of you,” and similarly of the cup, “Take and drink from it, all of you,” this is a commendation of unity and peace. Through this mystery, by sharing in Christ, all of us are to be one in him. As the Apostle affirms in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter three, verse twenty-eight: “There is no Jew and no Greek; there is no slave and no free person; there is no male and no female. For all of you are one in Christ Jesus.”

Elsewhere he commends this same unity in the sharing of this sacrament, saying in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter ten, verse sixteen: “The cup of blessing that we bless—is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ? And the bread that we break—is it not a participation in the body of Christ?” Because there is one bread, we, though many, are one body, all of us who share in the one bread.

So great is the unity of the Church in Christ that, just as there is one faith, one baptism, and one altar—about which Isaiah says, in the Book of Isaiah, chapter nineteen, verse nineteen, “There will be an altar of the Lord in the middle of the land of Egypt”—so too, everywhere, there is one bread of Christ’s body and one cup of his blood.

Therefore the Church, consecrating the mystery of the Lord’s holy body and blood by these words received from the tradition of the apostles, deliberately says that the Lord said to the apostles: “Take and eat from this, all of you; for this is my body. In the same way, after supper, taking also this glorious chalice.”

Let every believer pay attention to what is meant by the word “this.” The chalice that a catholic priest offers in sacrifice is no other than the very one the Lord handed on to the apostles. What is to be understood and held about the blood must also be understood and held about the body.

And when the prayer says “glorious chalice,” this is clearly taken from the psalm where it is sung, in the Book of Psalms, psalm twenty-two, verse five: “And your cup that intoxicates—how glorious it is!” And truly glorious is the chalice in which spotless blood is offered, touched by no cloud of sin. By this cup the love of the faithful is made drunk, so that it may put on the joy that comes from the forgiveness of sin. By this intoxication the soul is not confused, but consecrated.
Next, the words, “of the new and eternal covenant,” are easy to explain. In the Gospel we read only this: “the cup of my blood of the new covenant.” But the added word “eternal” is found both in the prophetic writings and in the apostolic writings. For in Isaiah we read God’s promise to the faithful: “I will make a covenant with you,” that is, an everlasting covenant. And in Ezekiel, where God promises the Church: “I will raise up for you an everlasting covenant,” in the book of Ezekiel, chapter sixteen, verse eighty. And the Apostle says: “By one offering he has perfected forever those who have been sanctified,” in the letter to the Hebrews, chapter ten, verse fourteen. And again: “The God of peace, who brought back from the dead the shepherd, the Lamb of the sheep, in the blood of the eternal covenant, our Lord Jesus Christ,” in the letter to the Hebrews, chapter thirteen, verse twenty. [Augustine] Therefore the blood of Christ is the new covenant because it is the new love by which, in the last ages of the world, he loved us even to death. May that love make us new, so that we may be new people, heirs of the New Covenant, just as the Apostle teaches when he says: “How much more has the blood of Christ, who through the Holy Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, cleansed our conscience from dead works, to serve the living God? And therefore he is the mediator of the New Covenant,” in the letter to the Hebrews, chapter nine, verse fourteen. So by this blood of the New Covenant, poured out for the forgiveness of sins, he cleansed—that is, he renewed—the conscience of the faithful. And those same faithful, according to the same Apostle, become heirs in hope of eternal life, and those who are called receive the promised eternal inheritance. And this is why that blood belongs to the new and eternal covenant: it renews people from oldness and leads them into an eternal inheritance. Hence the prophet Zechariah, speaking as if to the Lord himself, says: “You also, by the blood of your covenant, have sent out your prisoners from the pit in which there was now water,” in the book of Zechariah, chapter nine, verse ten. For through the pouring out of this blood, even the righteous people of old, held bound in hope of the redemption still to come, were brought out from the prison of the underworld; and through that same blood, all believers are rescued from the bonds of sins, from the pit of misery, and from the mud of filth.
Next, the phrase used here, “the mystery of faith,” is not found in the Lord’s own words when he handed on the mystery of his body and blood. It seems to have been taken from another place in the Gospel, where, according to John, he speaks about this same sacrament to some disciples who did not believe. He says: “The words that I have spoken to you are Spirit and life; but there are some of you who do not believe,” in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verses sixty-four through sixty-five. He shows that he had spoken a great mystery when he says: “The words that I have spoken are Spirit and life.” And when he adds, “But there are some of you who do not believe,” he shows that this mystery belongs only to faith and to the faithful, who hear those words faithfully. For that reason the words are Spirit and life for them: understood spiritually, they give them life. [Augustine] Or else these words come from the Apostle, where he says: “holding the mystery of faith in a pure conscience,” in Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter three, verse nine. For just as the sacrament of the Holy Trinity is necessary for salvation, so also the sacrament of the Lord’s passion and death, which is celebrated again and again in that offering, is equally necessary for salvation, and must equally be received and held in a pure conscience. This is also celebrated in baptism, as the same Apostle teaches when he says: “All of us who were baptized in Christ Jesus were baptized into his death.” Therefore everything that happens in this offering of the Lord’s body and blood is a mystery. One thing is seen; another thing is understood. What is seen has a bodily appearance; what is understood has a spiritual fruit. We must also know that, according to the practice of the Catholic Church handed down from the apostles, that same cup of the Lord’s blood may not be offered unless it is mixed with water. For wine was the mystery of our redemption when he said: “I will no longer drink of this fruit of the vine,” in the Gospel of Mark, chapter fourteen, verse twenty-five. And from his side, pierced by the lance, water came out together with blood; this showed wine pressed out, together with water, from the true vine of his flesh. These are the sacraments of the Church, without which no one enters into the life that is truly life. That blood was poured out for the forgiveness of sins. That water tempers the saving cup. It provides both washing and drink.
The Lord himself teaches us with what devotion and love this whole mystery of the Lord’s offering must be performed and received, when he says: “As often as you do these things, you will do them in memory of me,” in the Gospel of Luke, chapter twenty-two, verse nineteen. Explaining this, the Apostle says: “As often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you will proclaim the death of the Lord until he comes,” in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, verse twenty-six. So the offering of that bread and cup is the remembrance and proclamation of Christ’s death. This is carried out not so much by words as by the mysteries themselves; through them that precious death is commended to our minds more deeply and more powerfully. For what is the remembrance of Christ’s death, if not the commendation of his love? The blessed evangelist John commends that love to us when he says: “Jesus, knowing that his hour had come to pass from this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, loved them to the end,” in the Gospel of John, chapter thirteen, verse one. That means: this love led him all the way to death, because he loved them so much that he died for them. He himself bore witness to this when he said: “No one has greater love than this: that someone lay down his life for his friends,” in the Gospel of John, chapter fifteen, verse thirteen. Therefore, as he was about to go to his passion, and as he was about to depart from the world through the glory of his resurrection and ascension, he handed on this sacrament last of all to his disciples. He did this so that he might fix more firmly in their minds the memory of so great a love—the love by which alone we are saved. In this way we may always remember both what kind of people we were when he loved us, and how greatly he loved us: what kind of people, so that we may not boast about ourselves; how greatly, so that we may place our hope in him. The Apostle carefully teaches and presses both points when he says: “For why did Christ, when we were still weak, die at the appointed time for the ungodly?” in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter five, verse six. And a little later he says: “But God commends his love in us, because, if Christ died for us while we were still sinners, much more, now that we have been justified in his blood, shall we be saved from wrath through him. For if, when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son, much more, now reconciled, shall we be saved in his life,” in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verses nine through ten. This, then, is what he commanded to be done and repeated until he himself comes at the end of the age. Then the saints will have rest—not still in the sacrament of hope, by which the Church is joined together in this present time, as long as she drinks what flowed from the side of Christ, but already in the full perfection of eternal salvation. Then the kingdom will be handed over to God the Father, and in that clear contemplation of unchangeable truth we will need no bodily mysteries. [Gregory] For this victim, in a unique way, saves the soul from eternal destruction, because through the mystery it renews for us the death of the Only-Begotten Son. Although he has risen from the dead and now dies no more, and death will no longer have dominion over him, nevertheless he lives in himself immortally and incorruptibly, and yet for us he is again sacrificed in this mystery of the sacred offering. For there his body is received; there his flesh is divided for the salvation of the people. His blood is no longer poured into the hands of unbelievers. From this, then, let us consider what this sacrifice is for us: for our absolution, it always imitates the passion of the only-begotten Son. The mystery continues:
“Therefore, remembering, we your servants,” and so on, as far as, “the chalice of everlasting salvation.”

Our Lord and Savior entrusted to the memory of the faithful the mystery of his death, which he accepted for our salvation and for the salvation of all. He did this with such loving care that, through the offering and sharing of his body and blood, he wanted us to proclaim that same life-giving death until he himself comes in majesty from heaven.

So what could be more worthy, or more healing, than this: that by celebrating these venerable mysteries often, the priests and the people together—that is, the whole Church—remember his blessed passion? And it truly is blessed. Though he took it on without sin, it wiped away the sins of the whole world. For, as the Apostle says, not only “he died for our trespasses,” but also “he rose for our justification,” in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter four, verse twenty-five.

And so, by dying, he destroyed our death; by rising, he restored our life. Therefore we should also remember his resurrection from the dead.
Through that resurrection, once hell had been despoiled, he fulfilled in himself what he had foretold through the prophet: “I will be your death, O death; I will be your bite, O hell.” He also fulfilled what had earlier been prophesied: “For you will not abandon my soul in hell, nor let your holy one see corruption.”

And because, rising from the dead, he became the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep and the firstborn from the dead; because he no longer dies, and death will no longer have dominion over him; and because he was taken up into heaven and sits at the right hand of God—we should also remember his glorious ascension.

At that ascension, while the angels rejoiced and the whole army of heaven rang out his praises, he fulfilled what is sung in the psalm: “God has gone up with a shout of joy, and the Lord with the sound of a trumpet.” So too, in another psalm, the voices of the heavenly powers are brought in at his glorious ascension: they ask in wonder, and they answer in celebration. The questioners say, “Who is this King of glory?” And the answerers add, “The Lord, strong and mighty; the Lord, mighty in battle.” Again they ask, “Who is this King of glory?” And they answer, “The Lord of powers: he is the King of glory.”
For this is our whole salvation and our whole life: to remember him without ceasing, and to know that we belong to him alone. By his passion we have been strengthened; by his wound, healed; by his blood, cleansed; by his death, brought to life; by his resurrection, raised up with him; and by his ascension, glorified in heaven.

That is why we celebrate the price of our redemption in the sacrament of the Lord’s body and blood: so that we may always meditate faithfully on the Apostle’s warning, “You are not your own; for you were bought at a great price.”

Therefore, remembering the Lord’s passion, resurrection, and ascension, both the priests and the faithful people offer to the excellent majesty of God—that is, to his surpassing and glorious majesty—not anything of their own, but gifts that come from his own gifts and grants. As Scripture says: “What we have received from your hand, we have given back to you.” For he is the one who brings forth bread from the earth, and grants that wine should gladden the human heart.

So from these fruits of God’s creation—that is, from the gifts of his goodness and loving care—the Church offers him, in the mystery of the Lord’s body and blood, what is truly a pure victim, a holy victim, an unblemished victim. This threefold repetition praises so great a mystery; by praising it, it commends it to us; and by commending it, it stirs up loving devotion. For this victim is his body and blood, which, as blessed Peter says, belong to “Christ, the spotless and unblemished lamb.”
But what pure, holy, and unblemished victim does the Church offer, except the one she has received from the Lord? That is, the holy bread of eternal life, and the chalice of everlasting salvation.

For in this bread there is eternal life for those who receive it, as the Lord himself—the living bread and the bread of life—says about himself: “I am the living bread who came down from heaven,” in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse fifty-one. And again: “For the bread of God is the one who comes down from heaven and gives life to the world,” in the same Gospel, chapter six, verse thirty-three. And elsewhere he says: “Whoever eats this bread will live forever,” in the same Gospel, chapter six, verse fifty-nine.

And he explains that this bread is the bread of his body when he says: “And the bread that I will give is my flesh for the life of the world,” in the same Gospel, chapter six, verse fifty-two.

Therefore the chalice of his blood is also the chalice of eternal salvation, because that blood was poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins, so that he might save his people from their sins. About this it is sung in the psalm: “I will take up the chalice of salvation, and I will call upon the name of the Lord,” in the Book of Psalms, Psalm one hundred fifteen, verse thirteen.
This chalice brings salvation to the one who receives it: both in receiving the sacrament, and in imitating the passion, as the holy martyrs received it.

Manna signified this bread; the altar of God provides this bread. The faithful know the body of Christ: if a person is in the body of Christ, he will receive from the Spirit of Christ. Just as our own body cannot live without the spirit, so no one will have the Spirit of life unless he is found in Christ’s body.

Whoever wants to live should come near, believe, and live for God from God. Let him be incorporated into Christ, so that he may be made alive. For this is what it means to eat that food and drink that drink: to remain in Christ, and to have Christ remaining in oneself. As the Lord himself says: “Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood remains in me, and I in him,” in the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse fifty-seven.

The mystery continues:
“Look upon these with a gracious and serene countenance,” and so on, as far as “immaculate victim.” Here God is asked to look, with a gracious—that is, a favorable—and serene countenance, upon these gifts of life and salvation, which are being offered to his glorious majesty. This does not mean that his countenance is sometimes clouded over and sometimes made bright. He is, as blessed James says, “the Father of lights, with whom there is no change, nor shadow of variation,” in the letter of James, chapter one, verse seventeen. And blessed John testifies: “God is light, and in him there is no darkness at all,” in the first letter of John, chapter one, verse five.

Rather, the One who is always in light makes his face serene over us, and makes it shine upon us, when he shows us his mercy. So we pray in the Psalm: “May he make his face shine upon us, and have mercy on us,” Psalm sixty-six, verse two. By contrast, concerning certain people who are unworthy of his mercy, he himself says: “I will hide my face from them,” Deuteronomy, chapter thirty-one, verse seventeen. And again, to those toward whom he shows himself gracious, he promises: “I will not turn my face away from you,” the book of Jeremiah, chapter three, verse twelve. For this reason blessed Daniel prays in these very words: “Hear, Lord, the prayers of your servant; make your face shine upon your sanctuary, and look favorably upon this people, over whom your name has been invoked,” the book of Daniel, chapter nine, verse seventeen.

Of course, we must not think that God has a face or countenance in a human bodily form. As the Lord says in the Gospel: “God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and in truth,” the Gospel of John, chapter four, verse twenty-four. Instead, God’s face and countenance mean his presence. What is God’s face shining upon us, if not his presence made known, when we sense that his mercy and goodness are present with us? In the same way, for God to “look upon” us means the visitation of his loving-kindness, as we pray in the Psalm: “God of hosts, turn us back; look down from heaven, and see, and visit this vine,” Psalm seventy-nine, verse fifteen.
So almighty God is being asked to become favorable and gracious, and with a serene countenance—that is, with the revealed presence of his goodness and mercy—to look kindly upon the gifts of his Church, and to receive them as accepted and pleasing, just as he received the gifts of his righteous servant Abel. Abel’s faith, righteousness, and accepted offering are praised both in the Old Law, where it is written, “And God looked upon Abel and his gifts,” Genesis, chapter four, verse four; and in the Gospel, where the Lord says, “From the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah son of Barachiah,” the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-two, verse thirty-five; and also by the Apostle, when he writes to the Hebrews: “By faith Abel offered to God a greater sacrifice than Cain; through it he received testimony that he was righteous, God bearing witness to his gifts; and through it, though dead, he still speaks,” the letter to the Hebrews, chapter eleven, verse four. Abel is called God’s “puer”—his child or servant—not because of his age, but because of his simplicity and purity. In the same way David, who died old and full of days, is nevertheless spoken of in the Gospel: “He has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of David his servant,” the Gospel of Luke, chapter one, verse sixty-nine.

And the prayer says, “as the sacrifice of our patriarch Abraham.” Surely this refers to the sacrifice he offered when he presented his only-begotten son Isaac as a holocaust to God. That offering was accepted so fully that Abraham at once deserved to hear: “By myself I have sworn, says the Lord, because you have done this thing and have not spared your only-begotten son for my sake, I will bless you,” Genesis, chapter twenty-two, verse twelve, and so on. Abraham is patriarch not only of the Israelite people according to the flesh, but also of us according to faith, just as it was promised to him: “For I have made you the father of many nations.”

The prayer then adds: “and the offering which your high priest Melchizedek offered to you.” Scripture says plainly about him: “And Melchizedek, king of Salem, brought forth bread and wine; for he was priest of God Most High, and he blessed Abraham,” Genesis, chapter fourteen, verse eighteen. His priesthood and sacrifice are magnified so greatly that the Apostle says of him: “First, Melchizedek, which is interpreted king of righteousness; then also king of Salem, which means king of peace; without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end, made like in every way to the Son of God, who remains a priest forever,” the letter to the Hebrews, chapter six, verse three.
What is added after all this—“a holy sacrifice, an immaculate victim”—seems to refer back to the earlier words, “Look upon these with a gracious and serene countenance,” and to have been added to complete the petition. In this whole prayer, then, almighty God is asked to accept the sacrifice of the Church as he accepted the sacrifice of righteous Abel, the sacrifice of the patriarch Abraham, and the sacrifice of the high priest Melchizedek.

For in those ancient sacrifices there was already an image of this true sacrifice. At that time, by the perfectly ordered arrangement of the ages, the one God—the author of both Testaments and of both kinds of sacrifice—was pleased by what prefigured the truth that was to come; and now he is pleased by the fulfillment of that same truth.

In Abel’s sacrifice, offered from the firstborn of the sheep and from their fat, and preferred over Cain’s sacrifice, which was offered from the fruits of the earth, the faith of the New Testament was prefigured: that faith which, praising God in the innocence of grace, is placed before the earthly works of the Old Testament. And in the sacrifice of Abraham’s only and beloved son, there was prefigured the sacrifice of the one about whom the Father says: “This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased,” the Gospel of Matthew, chapter seventeen, verse five. Therefore the Apostle says: “He did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us all,” Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight, verse thirty-two. And the sacrifice of Melchizedek was so true a sign of Christ’s sacrifice that it was foretold about Christ: “You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek,” Psalm one hundred nine, verse four.

The mystery continues:
“Humbly we beseech you, almighty God,” and so on. Who could ever fully grasp words of a mystery so deep, so wonderful, so astonishing? Who could say anything worthy about them? They are more to be reverenced and feared than analyzed. Still, blessed Gregory, a fitting interpreter of so great a mystery, speaks about them in one place almost unspeakably, as one speaks of something beyond words. [Gregory] He says: “What believer could doubt that, at the very hour of the sacrifice, at the priest’s voice, the heavens are opened; that in this mystery of Jesus Christ the choirs of angels are present; that the lowest things are joined to the highest; that earth is united with heaven; and that visible and invisible realities become one?”
The same teacher also explains, with care, how we should understand “the altar of God on high in the sight of his divine majesty.” He is commenting on the verse of the Psalm: “Establish a solemn day in crowded gatherings, as far as the horn of the altar,” Psalm one hundred seventeen, verse twenty-seven. He says: “A solemn day for the Lord is the joining of our heart to him. But that solemn day is established ‘in crowded gatherings’ when the mind, moved continually by devout love for him, is stirred to tears. And as if we were asking, ‘How long will we keep doing this? How long will we be afflicted by suffering?’ the psalm immediately adds the limit to which this must continue, saying: ‘as far as the horn of the altar.’ For the horn of the altar is the exaltation of the inner sacrifice. Once we have reached it, there will no longer be any need for us to make a solemn day for the Lord out of our lamentation.” Blessed Augustine also speaks about this same altar on high. Commenting on the verse of the Psalm, “I will wash my hands among the innocent, and I will go around your altar, Lord,” Psalm twenty-five, verse six, he briefly explains it this way: “I will make my works clean among the innocent, and by those works I will embrace your heights.” Again, elsewhere, explaining the verse, “And I will go in to the altar of God, to God who gives joy to my youth,” he says about the same reality: “There is an invisible altar to which no unjust person comes. Only the one who comes safely to this visible altar can come to that one. There he will find his life, if here he examines his cause. As for the kind of sacrifice offered there: the one who enters will himself be taken up as a whole burnt offering.” And Augustine repeats, in other words, what this altar is, saying to the Lord: “To God who gives joy to my youth.”
Again, in another place, the same teacher explains the same verse that blessed Gregory explained. He says: “In that eternal house there is a sacrifice of praise; there is an eternal priest; and there is an eternal altar: the peaceful mind of the just. So it is not enough that on earth there should be that solemn day on which the Lamb was slain. Establish it as eternal in dense assemblies, searching out the high and hidden things, until, with your minds lifted up by God, you reach all the way to his divinity. And what will we do there except sing praises? What else will we say there except, ‘You are my God,’ and, ‘I will confess you; you are my God, and I will exalt you,’ Psalm one hundred seventeen, verse twenty-eight? We will not say this with the noise of words. Rather, love clinging to him cries out this voice by itself. Love itself is the voice.” These statements about the altar of God on high in the sight of his divine majesty had to be placed here from the words of the holy Fathers, so that no one, thinking in a fleshly way, should imagine that there is a bodily altar in heaven, made out of some heavenly or super-heavenly material. Instead, following the understanding of the spiritual Fathers, we should understand the altar of God on high to be rational and intelligible, existing in the chosen rational creature—that is, in angels and in human beings. In the holy angels, from the moment that creature was made, it has been lifted up in contemplation of its Creator and most closely united to itself through the spirit of peace. Thus it is the true and exalted altar of God, from which God receives the eternal sacrifice of praise and the offering of jubilation. To the unity of that altar is now joined, through faith, the whole multitude of elect human beings; and in the future they will be joined to it through the direct vision of divine contemplation.
But as long as the elect live in this mortal condition, before they actually enter that altar of God, and before they reach the horn of the altar in their prayers and supplications, they are still, as it were, down below. So they are helped by the powers above, in order that through their intercession they may be heard and, so to speak, received from the height of the altar. Thus we read that, when Daniel was praying and pleading, the angel Gabriel appeared to him and said: “From the beginning of your prayers the word went out, and I have come because of your words; and there is no one who helps me in all these things except Michael the archangel, your prince,” the book of Daniel, chapter nine, verse twenty-three. And in the prophet Zechariah we read that, for the consolation and liberation of God’s people, an angel prayed and said: “Lord of hosts, how long will you not have mercy on Jerusalem and the cities of Judah, with which you have been angry? This is now the seventieth year,” the book of Zechariah, chapter two, verse twelve. And the Lord answered the angel with good words, words of consolation. Likewise, the angel Raphael says to Tobias: “When you prayed with tears, and buried the dead, I offered your prayer to the Lord; and now the Lord has sent me to heal you,” and so on, the book of Tobit, chapter twelve, verse twelve. Therefore, in this prayer and offering of the sacred consecration, something incomprehensible and beyond speech takes place—something far more wonderful than all these examples: through angelic ministries and supplications, the offerings are presented, as if from the altar on high, before the sight of the divine majesty at that hour of sacrifice. For at that hour Christ must be believed to be present, with his heavenly ministers standing by him, so that he may consecrate the gifts set before him. On this point it seems useful to quote some words of blessed Ambrose from his Exposition of the Gospel according to Luke: “Do not doubt that an angel is present when Christ is present, when Christ is sacrificed. For Christ our Passover has been sacrificed. And so it was not without reason that an angel appeared to Zechariah as he prayed, at the right side of the altar of incense: the coming of the true priest was already being announced, and the heavenly sacrifice was being prepared, in which the angels would minister.” So we should think about these things in such a way that, by that very thought, we try to reach something than which nothing is better or higher.
The same applies to what follows: “So that all of us, as many as receive from this sharing in the altar the most holy body and blood of the Son, may be filled with every heavenly blessing and grace.” These are the gifts, these are the offerings, set above every glory. By them almighty God refreshes his faithful people; by the glorious body of his Son he gives them life, sanctifies them, and prepares them for eternity. Through these gifts he fills the hearts of the faithful with every heavenly blessing and grace, giving chastity to their bodies, faith to their minds, and purity to their thoughts. Through these gifts he protects his own with mercy, strengthens them with hope, and fortifies them with love, so that, by so great a sharing in the Lord and receiving God within themselves, they may deserve to become his temple.
So what does it mean to be filled with every heavenly blessing and grace through sharing in the body and blood of the Son of God? And how greatly should we hunger and thirst for this good? Let us listen to blessed Ambrose, who teaches and exhorts us so sweetly: “Christ is the food of the Church; Christ is its drink. The flesh of God is food, and the blood of God is drink. Christ is ministered to us every day. This food does not fatten the body; it strengthens the human heart. This is the bread of life. Therefore, the one who eats life cannot die. Come to him and be satisfied, because he is bread. Come to him and drink, because he is a spring. Come to him and be enlightened, because he is light. Come to him and be set free, because where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom. Come to him and be absolved, because he is the forgiveness of sins. The body of the Lord Jesus is the plea for divine reconciliation and eternal protection. Take this defense for yourself. Receive the Lord Jesus as the guest of your mind. Where his body is, there Jesus is. When the adversary sees that your guest-room is occupied, he will understand, by the brightness of heavenly wisdom, that through Christ the place is closed to his temptations. Do you ask who this is? Listen to him saying: ‘I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will not hunger; and whoever believes in me will never thirst,’ the Gospel of John, chapter six, verse thirty-five.” The mystery continues.
“Remember also, Lord, your servants, men and women, who have gone before us with the sign of faith,” and so on, as far as, “of light and peace, we pray that you grant.” The loving mother, the Church, also prays for her dead. She commends them through the intercession of the sacred offering, firmly believing that the precious blood poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins has power not only for the salvation of the living, but also for the absolution of the dead. As blessed John testifies: “And the blood of his Son Jesus Christ cleanses us from every sin,” John’s second letter, chapter one, verse seven.

For the faithful, when they leave the body, go forward to the Lord. Yet they are not cut off from the Church, because they have gone before us “with the sign of faith” and “sleep in the sleep of peace.” They go with the sign of faith because they have been reborn from water and the Holy Spirit, and because they have been marked by Christ’s cross and passion. They sleep because they are truly to be awakened in the resurrection. So the Lord said of the girl whom he was about to raise: “The girl is not dead, but sleeping.” And of Lazarus he said: “Our friend Lazarus is sleeping; but I am going to awaken him from sleep.” Therefore the Apostle says about the faithful who have died: “We do not want you to be ignorant, brothers, about those who are sleeping, so that you may not grieve like the others, who have no hope.”

So they sleep in the sleep of peace because they have not been separated from the unity and fellowship of Christ and the Church, either by heresies, or by schisms, or by deadly sins. And even if they were once caught in such things, they were healed by repentance and reconciled through the prayer of the Church, to whom it was said in Peter: “Whatever you loose on earth will be loosed also in heaven.” [Augustine] Joined to the mystery of redemption, they certainly died in peace, through the mercy of him who does not want the death of the sinner, but wants the sinner to turn back and live.

For the souls of the devout dead are not separated from the Church. Otherwise, their memorial would not be made at the altar in the commemoration of Christ’s body. Nor would it do any good to hurry to his baptism in times of danger, lest this life end without it; nor to hurry to reconciliation, if someone has been separated from that same body by a corrupt and evil conscience. Why are these things done, unless the faithful, even when dead, are still his members? So although their souls do not yet reign with Christ together with their bodies, still their souls already reign with him.

Hence we read in the Apocalypse: “Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord. From now on, the Spirit says that they may rest from their labors, for their works follow them,” the Book of Revelation, chapter fourteen, verse thirteen. This is the true sabbath-rest of the faithful—that is, their rest—about which the Apostle says: “And so there remains a sabbath-rest for the people of God,” the letter to the Hebrews, chapter four, verse nine. For whoever has entered his rest has rested from his own works, just as God did from his.

Therefore the Church now reigns with Christ in both the living and the dead. For this reason, as the Apostle says: “Christ died, so that he might be Lord of both the living and the dead,” Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter fourteen, verse nine. And elsewhere he says: “For God has not appointed us for wrath, but for obtaining salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ, who died for us, so that whether we are awake or asleep, we may live with him,” Paul’s first letter to the Thessalonians, chapter five, verse nine. By these words he intended “the awake” to mean the living, and “the sleeping” to mean the dead, just as he says again: “So whether we live or whether we die, we belong to the Lord,” Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter fourteen, verse eight.
[Gregory] It is clearer than daylight that the souls of the perfectly righteous, as soon as they leave the enclosure of this flesh, are received into the heavenly dwellings. Truth himself bears witness to this when he says: “Where the body is, there the eagles will gather,” the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-four, verse twenty-eight. For where our Redeemer himself is in body, there, without any doubt, the souls of the righteous will also be gathered.

And Paul longs to be dissolved and to be with Christ. So whoever does not doubt that Christ is in heaven must not deny that Paul’s soul is in heaven. For Paul also speaks of the dissolution of his body and of his dwelling in the heavenly homeland when he says: “We know that if our earthly house of this dwelling is dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made by hands, eternal in the heavens,” Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter five, verse five.
[Vigilius] Yet this should be believed, as we have already said, about the apostles, martyrs, confessors, and the other men of more perfect life. For there are souls of certain righteous people who, although they are in blessed rest, are still delayed from the kingdom of heaven in certain dwelling places. In the harm caused by this delay, what else is shown except that they lacked something of perfect righteousness?

But some people, because of their good works of faith, have indeed been appointed beforehand to the lot of the elect. Yet because of certain evils by which they were stained when they left the body, after death they are received for severe correction into the flames of purifying fire. Either they are cleansed from the stain of their faults by a long testing up to the day of judgment, or else, before that day, they are released from their punishments through the prayers, alms, fasts, tears, and offerings of the saving Victim made by their faithful friends. Then they too reach the rest of the blessed.

So the sacred sacrificial offerings benefit those dead persons who, while living here, gained the grace that after death they would be helped by the good works done for them here by others. But in all this we must consider that “the safer path” is this: whatever good a person hopes others will do for him after death, he should do for himself while he is alive. For it is more blessed to go out free than to seek freedom after being bound.

After the words, “who have gone before us with the sign of faith and sleep in the sleep of peace,” the custom of the ancients was—and the Roman Church still acts this way—that the names of the dead were immediately read from the diptychs, that is, from the tablets. And after the reading of the names, these following words were added: “For these, and for all who rest in Christ, we pray that you grant them a place of refreshment, light, and peace.” That means: for those whose names are remembered, and for all the others who rest in Christ, about whom the Apostle says, “And the dead who are in Christ will rise first.”

“A place of refreshment”: where the burning heat of punishments is not felt, the heat of which the rich man buried in hell spoke when he said, “For I am tormented in this flame.” “And of light”: the light the Lord promises to the faithful when he says, “I am the light of the world; whoever follows me will not walk in darkness, but will have the light of life.” The Psalmist also speaks of it: “That I may be pleasing before God in the light of the living,” the Psalms, Psalm fifty-five, verse fourteen. “And of peace”: the peace into which righteous Simeon desired to pass when the labors of this life were finished, saying, “Now you dismiss your servant, Lord, according to your word, in peace,” the Gospel of Luke, chapter two, verse twenty-nine. And it is the peace in which the souls of the holy martyrs rest, as Scripture says: “In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to die, but they are in peace,” the Wisdom of Solomon, chapter three, verses two through three.
This devout and religious practice, which the whole Church throughout the world holds uniformly and solemnly—having without doubt received it from apostolic tradition—is also very carefully commended by the Scripture of the Old Testament and by the custom preserved by the Fathers. For we read in the books of the Maccabees that the most courageous man and high priest Judas sent twelve thousand drachmas of silver to Jerusalem to be offered for the sins of the dead who had fallen in battle. He considered that those who had accepted their death in godliness had a most excellent grace stored up for them. And in praise of his faith, this too is added: that with holy and saving thought he prayed for the dead, so that they might be released from sin.

The mystery continues:
“To us sinners also, your servants,” and so on, as far as “Through Christ our Lord.” This is a commendation of the spirits of the dead through the offering of mercy, so that, among the blessed dwellings of the elect, they may be worthy to receive a place of refreshment, light, and peace. Next, almighty God is prayed to bring the rest of the faithful as well—the ones who still remain alive, that is, the priests and the people—to that same blessedness, by his power and grace, and with the help of the venerable sacrament.

For this is the voice of the whole Church: the voice of priests and people saying to God with humility and a contrite heart, “To us sinners also, your servants.” Although we should always recognize from the heart that we are sinners, still this must be done and confessed with special attention when, in that holy mystery, the grace of remission and the forgiveness of sins are being celebrated. With humility and contrition of heart we must say to God, “To us sinners also, your servants.” For, as it is written, even if we have sinned, we are yours, because we know your greatness. We do not hope in any merits of our own, but “in the multitude of your mercies.” So we sing in our hearts with the psalm: “And according to the multitude of your mercies, blot out my iniquity”—Psalm fifty, verse three.

At this point we must recall, and carefully impress on our minds, the words of the Apostle: “Every priest, taken from among human beings, is appointed on behalf of human beings in the things that concern God, so that he may offer gifts and sacrifices for sins. He is able to have compassion on those who are ignorant and going astray, since he himself is surrounded by weakness. And because of this, he must offer for sins for himself, just as he does for the people”—the letter to the Hebrews, chapter five, verse one.

In connection with this, blessed Gregory says in one place, exhorting us: “When we do these things, we must sacrifice ourselves to God in contrition of heart. For we who celebrate the mysteries of the Lord’s Passion ought to imitate what we do. Then the sacrificial victim will truly be for us before God, when it has made us ourselves into a sacrifice.”

So almighty God is prayed to grant, not only to the dead whose commemoration was made above, but also to us sinners who hope in the multitude of his mercies, some share and fellowship with his holy apostles and martyrs and all the saints. In this way, according to our measure, since we are now joined to them by communion in faith, since we venerate their memory, and since we perform these divine mysteries in communion and unity with them, we may also receive with them in the future the fellowship of life and eternal blessedness.

We ask that God’s mercy bring us into their company; that he not judge us by what we deserve, but grant us pardon; and that, because we cannot be joined to the fellowship of the saints by merit, we may be joined to it by forgiveness. For if we are assessed and weighed by divine judgment, no one will rise up to the fellowship of the apostles and martyrs.

At the end of this prayer, as in the preceding prayers, the words “Through Christ our Lord” are added. And immediately the conclusion of the whole consecration follows, saying:
“Through whom you always create all good things,” and so on. For through him almighty God not only created, at the beginning of the world, all these good things that are consecrated on the holy altars, bringing into being what did not exist. Through the same only-begotten Word he said, and it was done: “Let the earth sprout green plants bearing seed, and a fruit tree making fruit”—Genesis, chapter one, verse eleven. In that creation, of course, both the grain-bearing plant and the vine from which wine comes had their beginning.

But God also always creates these same good things by causing them to grow and by renewing them—just as in Cana of Galilee, to show that he was the author of this creation, he changed water into wine by a wondrous sign—so that every year new harvests and new wines may come forth. About this work of governing, propagating, and renewing creation, the only-begotten Son himself says in the Gospel to the Jews who were indignant because he healed on the Sabbath: “My Father is working until now, and I am working.” It is as though he were saying: The Father did not work only during the first six days, as you think. He is working until now—not by establishing a new kind of creature, but by causing the things he created in the beginning to continue, so that they do not fail.

[Vigilius] “And I,” he says, “am working”—understand, “until now”—arranging all things with the Father, governing them, and bringing them to increase. In these words he declared himself, with complete clarity, to be equal to God the Father. For those whose work is one and the same undoubtedly have equal majesty.

Through him, then, as has been said, God the Father always creates all these good things. And once they have been created and offered in his sight, he sanctifies them, so that what was simple created matter becomes sacraments. And so, by sanctifying, he gives them life, so that they become mysteries of life, so that life may be received in them, and so that they may supply a life-giving substance to those who receive them. By sanctifying and giving life, he blesses them in an ineffable way, because he fills them with every heavenly blessing and grace. And once they have been sanctified, given life, and blessed in this way, he grants them to us through that same Son, who together with him sanctifies, gives life, and blesses, and who has given us such a saving refreshment from his body and blood.

[Augustine] So let those who eat, eat; and let those who drink, drink. Let them hunger and thirst. Let them eat life; let them drink life. To eat that food is to be refreshed. But you are refreshed in such a way that the source of your refreshment is not used up. What is it to drink that drink, if not to live? Eat life, drink life, and you will have life—and life remains whole. This will be so—that is, the body and blood of Christ will be life for each person—if what is visibly received in the sacrament is, in the reality itself, eaten spiritually and drunk spiritually.
“Through him, and with him, and in him, there is to God the almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, all honor and glory.”

“Through him,” that is, through the true mediator between God and human beings, the man Jesus Christ. “With him,” because he is coequal with the Father, and one honor and glory are owed to him together with the Father; for, although he was in the form of God, he did not regard equality with God as something seized by robbery. “In him,” because he is truly of the same substance, as he himself says: “That the Father may be glorified in the Son.”

So “all honor and glory” belong to God the Father through the Son, and with the Son, and in the Son, in the unity of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, possesses unity of deity with the Father and the Son, and therefore is adored and glorified together with them. Thus, in the Father, authority is indicated to us; in the Son, being begotten; in the Holy Spirit, the communion of the Father and the Son; and in the three, equality.

But what does it mean that we give honor and glory to God the Father through the Son, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, “unto all ages of ages”? What does “unto all ages of ages” mean, if not what the blessed apostle Jude, at the end of his letter, explains almost point by point and expressly, and by explaining it gives a general conclusion? He says: “To the only God our Savior, through our Lord Jesus Christ, be glory, majesty, dominion, and power before every age, and now, and unto all ages of ages”—the letter of Jude, verse twenty-five.

For the age began—that is, temporal changeability began—with the creation of creatures. The present ages are running their course, slipping away with the movement of changeable things. But future ages will follow, remaining eternally in an unchangeable state of things. Yet because all these things are fixed in the eternal wisdom of God, without any beginning at all and without change—as we read in the Gospel, “What was made was life in him,” the Gospel of John, chapter one, verse three—the phrase “ages of ages” is rightly understood in this way: as ages that remain in the wisdom of God with unshaken stability, and that bring into being these ages that pass away with time.

Or certainly, “ages of ages” may be taken simply as ages that follow the ones before them, joined together in an unbroken connection. They are called “ages of ages” until this whole present age, which is woven together out of the ages of different times, reaches its end; and then the future age succeeds it, the age that has no end, where God will be seen and praised without end.

“Amen,” which is answered by the whole Church, means “true”—not just anywhere or in any ordinary way, but in a mystical and sacred sense. [Augustine] Therefore the faithful respond this to the consecration of so great a mystery, just as they do in every proper prayer; and by responding, they give their assent.

Then the priest adds further and says: “Let us pray.”
“Admonished by saving precepts,” and so on. It was entirely fitting that this whole, most holy action should be concluded with the Lord’s Prayer, and that the petitions of the faithful—the petitions the Lord taught us for the sake of both the future life and the present life—should be more powerfully commended through this same commemoration of his Passion.

So the whole Church is summoned, and the priest says, “Let us pray.” And the Church prays with the priest—not with the voice, but with the heart. The lips are closed, but the conscience is open. There is silence, yet the breast cries out; but the one who shows mercy hears it with his ears.

So also Susanna, when she was being condemned by unjust judges, was silent and prayed. Her mouth was not heard by human beings, but her heart cried out to God. Surely she did not fail to deserve being heard just because no sound came forth from her bodily mouth. She was heard—but when she prayed, no human being knew it.

Therefore the priest prays together with the Church, turned toward the Lord, and says: “Admonished by saving precepts,” and so on.
“Our Father, who are in heaven,” and so on. This is the saving command given to us—that is, a command that serves our salvation. Through the washing of rebirth and the Spirit of adoption, the only-begotten Son has made us children of God, and he taught us, indeed commanded us, to say this. He himself gave us the pattern for prayer, not by human teaching, but by divine instruction. And so that we might dare to say to God, “Our Father,” he taught us by his command and formed us by his instruction, saying: “This, then, is how you will pray: Our Father, who are in heaven.”

So this is saving for us above all else: that God the Father has given us so great a grace through his only-begotten Son. Thinking about such goodness, and loving it, we should live in such a way that we are no longer children of this age, but children of God. Therefore, commanded by saving precepts and formed by divine instruction, we presume to say to God the Father, “Our Father, who are in heaven”—not with reckless presumption, but with the reverence of obedience. In this way every believer, who in the old humanity was a child of wrath, now in heavenly rebirth calls upon the Father with devoted love.

[Augustine] We do believe that God is everywhere by the presence of his divinity, but he is not everywhere by the grace of indwelling. So because of this indwelling, where the grace of his love is recognized, we do not say, “Our Father, who are everywhere”—although that too is true. Rather, we say, “Our Father, who are in heaven,” so that in the Prayer we may remember above all his temple, which we ourselves ought to be.

[Severian] And to the extent that we are that temple, to that extent we belong to his fellowship and to the household of adoption. Nor did the Son leave himself out of this Prayer. Instead, he joined himself to the Father’s unity, because he commanded that, in the Father, he too should be prayed to. For when you ask the Father, you call upon the name of the Son.
“May your name be made holy.” By asking this, we are taught to desire that his name, which is always holy in itself, may also be regarded as holy among human beings—that is, that it may not be treated with contempt. This benefits not God, but human beings.

“May your kingdom come.” With this request, we stir up our desire for that kingdom: that it may come to us, and that we may be worthy to reign in it.

“May your will be done, as in heaven, so also on earth.” With this request, we ask him for obedience itself: that his will may be done in us just as it is done in the heavenly realm, among his angels.

“Give us today our daily bread.” When we say “today,” we mean this present time. In this request, either we ask for enough daily food—using the word “bread” to stand for the whole of our sustenance—or we ask for the sacrament of the faithful. That sacrament is necessary in this present time, yet not for gaining the happiness of this present time, but for reaching that eternal happiness.
“And forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.” Here we are taught both what we should ask for and what we should do, so that we may be worthy to receive it.

“And do not lead us into temptation.” We ask that we may not be left without his help, and so be deceived into consenting to some temptation, or be overcome by affliction and give way to it.

“But deliver us from evil.” Here we are taught to remember that we are not yet in that good state where we will suffer no evil at all. This request is so wide-ranging that a Christian, whatever trouble he may be placed in, should pour out his groans in it, shed his tears in it, begin his prayer with it, dwell on it, and bring his prayer to its end with it.

“Amen” is the seal of the Lord’s Prayer. It means “truly,” or “faithfully”; it is an attestation of truth. By apostolic tradition the Church customarily gives this same response to the other prayers as well, just as the Apostle writes to the Corinthians: “If you bless in the spirit, how will the person who fills the place of the uninstructed say Amen to your blessing, since he does not know what you are saying?”—Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter fourteen, verse sixteen. From this he shows that an uninstructed person cannot answer that what is being said is true unless he has first understood it.

After the Lord’s Prayer, which the whole Church pours out to God for the completion of the perfect mystery, the priest continues with the conclusion of that Prayer and says:
“Deliver us, we ask you, Lord, from all evils,” and so on. In these words, the past evils from which we ask to be delivered should be understood as our sins already committed. Even if the act itself has now ceased in us, the guilt remains unless it is wiped away by divine pardon. So divine Scripture warns us, saying: “Son, you have sinned; do not add to it again. But also pray about your past sins, so that they may be forgiven you”—the Book of Sirach, chapter twenty, verse one.

Present evils are understood in this way: because we sin every day, every day we have to say, “Forgive us our debts.” These present evils can include our daily sins in the soul; and also the various illnesses and sufferings that often befall the body; and, from outside, the countless disasters that rush upon us—famine, plague, the sword, and other dangers of this kind. Whenever we suffer any of these, we must implore the help of the one who alone, in his mercy, heals both souls and bodies. He knows when to apply to his children, whom he is preparing for the eternal inheritance, both the scourge of correction and the sweetness of consolation.

Future evils should be understood as whatever can happen to us that is opposed to our salvation and peace, whether through the temptations of the devil or through the adversities and miseries of this world. And above all, future evils mean those things from which we must especially pray to escape: that we may be delivered from the coming wrath, from eternal condemnation, which without doubt is prepared for the wicked.

So the priest prays for these things—or rather, the whole Church prays with the priest—that what was said at the end of the Lord’s Prayer, “But deliver us from evil,” may be granted to us by God’s mercy in such a way that we are delivered from all evils: those that have happened to us in the past, those that happen in the present, and those that we fear may happen in the future. For this reason the Lord, knowing that our frailty is set amid such great dangers, mercifully warns us, saying: “Watch and pray, so that you may not enter into temptation”—the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-six, verse forty-one. And again: “Watch, therefore, at every time, praying that you may be counted worthy to escape all these things that are to come, and to stand before the Son of Man”—the Gospel of Luke, chapter twenty-one, verse thirty-five.

The priest continues and says:
“And with the blessed and glorious one interceding for us,” and so on. When the people of Israel were held captive in Babylonian servitude, the Lord said to them, among other things, through the prophet Jeremiah: “And pray for the peace of the city to which I have caused you to be carried away, because in its peace there will be peace for you”—the Book of Jeremiah, chapter twenty-nine, verse seven. In the same way, the Apostle exhorts the people of the Church, saying: “I wish, therefore, first of all, that supplications, prayers, petitions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and for those who are in high position, so that we may lead a quiet and tranquil life in all godliness”—Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter two, verse one.

So, following this pattern established by God through the prophet and the Apostle, the faithful people, while on pilgrimage in this world—as if captive in Babylon, and sighing for the homeland above—also pray for temporal peace, so that they may not be hindered from spiritual and eternal peace. They pray that, by God’s loving mercy, persecutions, rebellions, and hostilities may be removed, and that the Church may live a quiet and tranquil life in complete peace.

And the Church prays for this with universal observance and without interruption in every offering of the Lord. In that offering, according to the apostolic command, she offers to God her supplications, prayers, petitions, and thanksgivings. She asks this from God by naming only a very few saints, but the most eminent ones: namely, that through the intercession first of all of the Mother of God, through whom salvation arose for the human race, and of the blessed apostles Peter and Paul and Andrew, she may be worthy to obtain it.

In this petition, the intercession of the other saints is not being left out. Rather, by commemorating these saints, the help of all the saints is being sought. For just as all the saints are joined and united with one another in God, so they all desire one thing and pray for the salvation of human beings. And they wish that in one of them all may be equally honored, and in one of them all equally slighted. The saints do not love those who choose them if they find that those same people have abandoned their companions. Indeed, anyone who thinks he should act in that way will offend the saints to whom he turns even more than those from whom he turns away. For the saints rejoice in unity, and they are one in Christ.
So when the Church says, “Graciously grant peace in our days,” she is clearly acting according to the example found in Isaiah. For when that same prophet had foretold to King Hezekiah the evils of the captivity that was to come, Hezekiah was struck to the heart and answered: “The word of the Lord that he has spoken is good. Only let there be peace and truth in my days”—the Book of Isaiah, chapter thirty-nine, verse eight.

In other words, he humbly left the future to God’s judgment. But concerning the present—what belonged to his own salvation and the salvation of his people—he prayed: “Let there be peace and truth in my days.” Peace, so that the tranquility of the kingdom would not be disturbed by enemies; and truth, so that within that very peace the worship of God, the truth of religion, would be preserved.

This is why, when the Apostle commanded that these things be prayed for, he placed both together: “That we may lead,” he says, “a quiet and tranquil life in all godliness.” For both quiet and tranquility belong to peace, while godliness belongs to the religion of divine worship. In the same way, then, the Church prays that peace may be granted in our days; and after us others will pray the same, and after them still others, all the way to the end of the age.
When the prayer asks for this peace, it adds: “that with the help”—that is, with the assistance and protection—“of God’s mercy, we may be aided.” So, as far as the inward devotion of religion is concerned, it asks that “we may always be free from sins.” And as far as outward peace is concerned, it asks that we may also be “safe from every disturbance.” For a peace in which people serve not divine worship but worldly desire is not the peace of the righteous, but of the wicked. About such people the Psalmist says: “For I was jealous of sinners, when I saw the peace of sinners,” Psalm seventy-two, verse three. Then there follows what is usually added at the conclusion of this prayer, and of nearly all the Church’s prayers:
“Through our Lord Jesus Christ, your Son,” and so on. [Fulgentius] As has often been said, the catholic Church uses this ending for a definite reason: because of that sacrament by which Christ Jesus, himself truly human, became the mediator between God and humanity, and a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek. Through his own blood he entered once for all into the holy places—not places made by human hands, but heaven itself—where he is at the right hand of God and intercedes for us.

So through him we offer the sacrifice of praise and prayer: through him, by whose death we were reconciled when we were enemies. For Christ, remaining in the form of God as the only-begotten God, to whom, together with the Father, we offer sacrifices, took the form of a servant and became a priest. Through him we are able to offer a living, holy, and pleasing sacrifice to God. Yet no sacrifice could have been offered by us, if Christ had not first become the sacrifice for us.

And when we say, “your Son,” and add, “who lives and reigns with you as God in the unity of the Holy Spirit,” we are recalling that unity which the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit possess by nature. This shows that the very same Christ who fulfilled the priestly office for us also has natural unity with the Father and the Holy Spirit. And when we say, “in the unity of the Holy Spirit,” we show that the Holy Spirit has one nature with the Father and the Son. What else is unity of nature, except that the Trinity is one God, and that the kingdom too is shown to be one? And where there is a natural unity of kingdom, there remains one power of reigning.

Therefore Christ our Lord lives and reigns with the Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit, forever and ever, just as he himself says in the Book of Revelation: “I am the first and the last; I was alive, and I was dead; and behold, I am living forever and ever,” the Book of Revelation, chapter one, verse seventeen. For to him it is said in the Psalm: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.” And again: “Your kingdom is a kingdom of all ages,” Psalm one hundred forty-four, verse thirteen. And about him the angel says to blessed Mary in the Gospel: “And of his kingdom there will be no end,” the Gospel of Luke, chapter one, verse thirty-three.
When this prayer is finished, the priest commingles the Lord’s offering, so that the Lord’s chalice may contain the full completeness of the sacrament. Then, as though through the joining together of this same mystery, he prays peace upon the Church, saying: “The peace of the Lord be with you always.” The Church, in turn, prays this great good upon him, answering: “And with your spirit.” [Augustine]

But what is the peace of the Lord, if not the peace of Christ? And the peace of Christ has no end in time. It is the perfection of every holy action and every holy intention. For the sake of this peace we receive his Spirit as a pledge. For the sake of this peace we believe and hope in him. By love for him, as far as he grants it, we are set on fire. And for the sake of this peace we bravely endure every trouble, so that in that peace we may reign happily, with no trouble at all. True peace creates unity, because whoever clings to the Lord is one spirit with him.

So, after peace has been prayed for, the whole Church, beginning with the priest, gives one another the kiss of peace. In this way, with everyone united by true peace, a place for God is made among them, as it says in the Psalm: “And his place was made in peace,” Psalm seventy-five, verse thirteen. And may what the Psalmist says elsewhere be fulfilled in the Church: “God is in his holy place; God makes people of one mind dwell in a house,” Psalm sixty-seven, verse seventeen. [Vigilius]

The Church keeps this practice from apostolic tradition. For through the apostles it is often said to the Church: “Greet one another with a holy kiss,” Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter sixteen, verse sixteen. That means with a true kiss, a peaceful kiss, a dove-like kiss—not a false and treacherous kiss, like the one Joab used to kill Amasa, or the one Judas used to betray the Savior, or the one used by those who speak peace with their neighbor while evil is in their hearts. So those people greet one another with a holy kiss who do not love merely in words or with the tongue, but in deed and in truth.
During all this, everyone sings, and in singing they pray, saying: “Lamb of God, who take away the sins of the world,” and so on. [Augustine] For no one takes away sins except the one about whom it was said: “Behold the Lamb of God; behold him who takes away the sins of the world.” For him, no human good is impossible, and no evil is incurable. [Vigilius]

The apostle Peter shows how he takes away the sins of the world when he says: “You were redeemed from your empty way of life handed down from your fathers, not with perishable silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of an unblemished and spotless lamb,” Peter’s first letter, chapter one, verse eighteen. And in the Book of Revelation, the apostle John says: “He loved us and washed us from our sins in his own blood,” the Book of Revelation, chapter one, verse five.

He washed us from our sins in his blood not only when he gave his blood for us on the cross, or when each one of us was washed in the waters of baptism in the mystery of his most holy passion. He also takes away the sins of the world every day, and washes us from our daily sins in his blood, when the memorial of that same blessed passion is renewed at the altar.
After this, the Eucharist is received—that is, the “good grace,” since by the grace of God he tasted death for everyone. Then, when the thanksgiving has been completed, everyone answers, “Amen.” [Augustine] For this is the clear voice of the blood of Christ: a voice that the blood itself brings forth from the mouths of the faithful who have been redeemed by that same blood.

When everything is finished, the people standing there and keeping the solemn observance are given their release, as the deacon cries out: “Go, it is the dismissal.” So here the word missa means nothing other than a sending away—that is, a release. The deacon announces that this release has come when all the rites have been celebrated and the people are dismissed from the solemn observance.

This is why the canons also speak of the missa of the catechumens: after the reading of the Gospel, the sacred mysteries begin to be celebrated, and no one may be present for them unless reborn in the font of baptism. At that moment, when the deacon cried out, those catechumens were sent away—that is, dismissed outside. So the missa of the catechumens took place before the action of the sacraments. The missa of the faithful takes place after the consecration and reception of those same sacraments.
