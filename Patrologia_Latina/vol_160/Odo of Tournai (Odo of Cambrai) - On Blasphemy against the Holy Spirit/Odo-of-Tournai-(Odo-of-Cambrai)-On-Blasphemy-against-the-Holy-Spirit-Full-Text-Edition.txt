
by Odo of Tournai (Odo of Cambrai)
Patrologia Latina Volume 


On Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit


Prologue.

In his Enchiridion, blessed Augustine says that he wrote a little book on blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. But that book has never come into my hands, and I do not know that I have ever met anyone who had read it.

For this reason, a venerable man named Amandus, a monk of Anchin, used to speak with me anxiously and complain that no solution could be found to so great a question. At that time Anchin was a sweet refuge for me in my exile. I had been driven by royal power from the see of Cambrai, because I would not agree to receive again, as a gift from the emperor, the staff and ring that I had already received from the Church when I was consecrated.

This brother often spoke with me about spiritual matters, and he earnestly kept asking me for a solution to the question just mentioned. I referred him to the explanations of the orthodox Fathers. But he said that they did not address the precise forms his doubt took, and that their explanations could not remove his uncertainty.

So it seemed better to me to take up the burden, even if I should stumble under it, than by refusing outright to make him think I was acting from pride or contempt. I would rather have him see me sweating and exhausted because I cannot manage the task, than have him feel ashamed of being rejected while I avoid the work. I was also greatly encouraged by the little book On Original Sin, which I had recently written; while I was struggling with that subject, many things came to me that I had not known before.

In Which Canon and Which Chapters Blasphemy against the Spirit Is Found in the Gospel

First, we must set out the very words of the Gospel that create the question. Then we must show why the question is difficult, before we prepare ourselves to solve it.

These passages are found in the second canon, at section twelve of the Gospel of Matthew, section thirty-four of the Gospel of Mark, and section forty-seven of the Gospel of Luke.

Matthew says: “Every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven human beings, but blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven. And whoever speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven; but whoever speaks against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven, either in this age or in the age to come.”

Mark says: “Amen, I tell you, all sins will be forgiven the children of men, and all the blasphemies with which they blaspheme. But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit has no forgiveness forever, but will be guilty of an eternal offense.” “Because they were saying, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’”

Luke says: “Everyone who speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven. But the one who blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven.”

That Some Sins Can Be Forgiven, and Some Cannot

These three Gospel passages in the second canon say that every sin can be forgiven except one. One sin alone can in no way obtain pardon; every other sin can, in some way, receive the mercy of forgiveness. One sin alone is completely unable to be loosed; every other sin can be absolved.

Over that one sin, the pit presses its mouth shut, so that it is closed up in eternal oblivion. Over every other sin, the mouth of the pit stands open, so that somehow it may escape.

But first, let us consider how God forgives sins that can be forgiven.

That Sin Cannot Be Forgiven to the Unrepentant

God forgives either a repentant person or an unrepentant one. But God cannot forgive sin in an unrepentant person, because God cannot be unjust.

A human being sins against another human being, and also sins against God. A person can remain unrepentant toward another human being, and also unrepentant toward God. A human being ought to forgive even an unrepentant person; God ought not to do so. Why? Because a sinner ought to forgive another sinner, but God does not owe forgiveness to the unrepentant.

A sinner forgives even the unrepentant because he fears judgment from above, fearing that he himself may not be forgiven even when he repents. God does not forgive the unrepentant, because God has no fear that forgiveness will be withheld from him. A human being ought to forgive an unrepentant sinner because he too is a sinner. God does not owe that forgiveness, because God is not a sinner.

Furthermore, all impenitence is pride, and all pride is directly opposed to God. So if God forgives the unrepentant, he forgives pride directed against himself; he joins to himself the very thing that cuts every good off from him, and unites himself to that which every good resists.

But it is impossible for the highest good to be allied in any way with the highest evil. The supreme good cannot give support to any evil at all, much less to the evil that is directly opposed to it. As Paul says in his second letter to the Corinthians, chapter fourteen: “What fellowship has light with darkness?” And again: “What agreement has the temple of God with idols?”

That God Possesses Some Goods Which He Himself Cannot Do

If, then, a human being can forgive the unrepentant, while God cannot forgive the unrepentant, it follows that a human being can do a good thing that God cannot do.

But where does a human being get any good, except from the one who is the highest good? A human being has a great good: the ability to forgive an unrepentant person. It is a great good, and one suited only to the perfect. But where does a person get this good, if God himself cannot do it?

Certainly, a human being does not possess so great a good from himself, but from God, from whom every good comes. God possesses even this good, although he does not perform it. God has it in himself, and from himself he gives to human beings what he has. God has it in order to command it; the human being receives it in order to do it.

God does not have it in himself for his own sake, but for the sake of humanity. He does not have it in himself as something for himself, but as something for us. He has it in himself, and we perform it. He has it in himself, and it belongs to us.

Therefore there are good things that God possesses, even though God cannot perform them. He possesses them because we would not do them unless we had them from him. So there are goods fitting for the majesty of the Creator, and there are goods fitting for the service of the creature. Yet both kinds of good come from the one from whom and in whom all good things exist.

That Even Impenitence Can Be Forgiven If One Repents

Although, as we have shown, no sin is forgiven in an unrepentant person, every sin is forgiven in a repentant person.

Perhaps, then, impenitence itself is blasphemy against the Spirit, since impenitence is unforgivable. But what if a sinner repents of that very impenitence? What if he finally lets go of the stubbornness he held for so long? Does he not deserve pardon? Is not the impenitence of a repentant person forgiven?

He was unrepentant for a long time, but now he repents. He has now softened the hardness of heart that lasted so long. We see his tears; we hear his groans; and God sees inwardly the compunction of his heart. If the door of mercy is closed to this person, to whom is it opened?

Far be it from God to reject such a person. Far be it from the most merciful one to push him away, since Scripture says in Ezekiel, chapter thirty-three: “At whatever hour the sinner groans, I will receive him.” Far be it that any guilt in a repentant person should be incurable before the supreme physician.

So if impenitence, which seems unforgivable, is itself forgiven through repentance, then what is the guilt that is not forgiven at all? What sin is called blasphemy against the Spirit?

Inquiry: Which Sin Is Blasphemy against the Spirit?

So here is the problem. On one side, the Gospel cries out that blasphemy against the Spirit is absolutely unforgivable. On the other side, the Church, that firmest pillar of truth, preaches repentance in response to every sin. On one side, the Gospel confirms in three passages that a certain sin cannot be loosed at all. On the other side, the Church promises sure pardon for every sin through repentance.

It would be wicked to contradict the Gospel; and who would dare accuse the Church of falsehood? The Gospel denies forgiveness for one particular sin; the Church affirms forgiveness for every sin. These statements are contradictory, and they cannot both be true if they are understood in exactly the same sense. So they must be understood in different senses, so that both can be true.

We must find the distinction: in one sense, the statements separate truth from falsehood; in another sense, they allow both statements to stand as true. The Church’s universal affirmation—when it says that every sin can be forgiven—is true if it is understood as speaking about forgiveness through repentance. But if the Gospel’s particular denial—when it says that blasphemy against the Spirit cannot be forgiven—were understood in the same way, as meaning that it cannot be forgiven through repentance, then it would be false. For it to be true, the qualification “through repentance” must not be understood in it; for blasphemy against the Spirit cannot be forgiven without repentance.

So, if the Gospel is to be true together with the Church, we must understand a different relation to repentance in each statement. When the Church affirms forgiveness, it includes repentance: every sin is forgiven through repentance. When the Gospel denies forgiveness, it excludes repentance: blasphemy against the Spirit is not forgiven without repentance. In that way, both are true: blasphemy against the Spirit is not forgiven without repentance, and every sin is wiped away through repentance. The same idea of repentance separates true from false; the different application of that idea lets both statements be true.

Understood in this way, the Gospel does not differ from the Church. When it says that blasphemy against the Spirit cannot be forgiven, we understand, “without repentance.” And the Church says the same thing about every sin.

But then another puzzle remains. Why does the Gospel single out blasphemy from all other sins, when, like every other sin, it can be forgiven through repentance? I think this seems puzzling because the door has not yet been opened to me. Open it, Christ, to the one who knocks. Reveal the mysteries of your most holy word.

You say that every sin can be forgiven except blasphemy against the Spirit, and yet no sin can be forgiven except through repentance. Does repentance not follow blasphemy too? Perhaps someone will say, “Blasphemy against the Spirit is such a great sin that no repentance can ever follow it.” And even if some blasphemer seems to beat his breast, and is heard sighing, perhaps it will be said that he is not doing this from the heart.

But then we are sent off into endless uncertainty. We start judging what cannot be known, and we presumptuously claim knowledge of the unsearchable abyss of the human heart. We shout that we can penetrate a hidden depth which only God can know. When we see blasphemers beating their breasts and shedding tears, and when we hear deep groans and long sighs, who would dare say that the repentance is deceitful and not from the heart?

I do not believe that supreme Wisdom wanted something written in the Gospel which a human being cannot grasp, or wanted to teach a human being something that a human being cannot know. And therefore I think I have not yet understood what the unforgiven blasphemy against the Spirit is; otherwise I would be forced to invent an unknowable impenitence of the heart.

That the Sin unto Death Is Blasphemy against the Spirit

But we should consult the first letter of John the Evangelist, one of the canonical letters, where something similar to the Gospel is said. In that letter, John seems to agree with the three evangelists on this saying, even though he had not included it in his own Gospel. It is as though, in the letter, he supplies what he had left unsaid in the Gospel, so that the teaching we are investigating may be known as more firmly established because it is confirmed by all four evangelists.

We have heard what the three evangelists said, and from their words the question arises. Now let us hear what John, the fourth evangelist, says, and from his words the solution will arise. The three entangle us by the obscurity of their words; the fourth disentangles us by the clear revelation of his own speech.

For he says, in John’s first letter, chapter five: “There is a sin leading to death; I do not say that anyone should pray for that.” Why is prayer for that sin forbidden, unless because it is in no way to be forgiven? Certainly one should not pray for something that cannot be forgiven at all.

And the Evangelist calls this a sin “unto death”: the person who closes his final day while still in sin has an unforgivable guilt, and God admits no one to pray for it. This is the blasphemy against the Spirit which is not forgiven: a person is held by sin right up to the end, so that eternal punishment receives the one whom this life leaves behind still willing to sin.

Such a sinner is not absolved in this age, because he remained in the will to sin. Nor is he absolved in the age to come, because he passed out of this life with the will to sin. And it is just that he be punished forever, because he would have wanted to sin forever if he had lived forever in this age. An everlasting punishment follows an everlasting will to sin, so that the necessity of just punishment does not fail in the person whose will to sin does not fail.

How the Sin unto Death Is One Single Sin, Just as Blasphemy against the Spirit Is

Here a very difficult question arises. The three evangelists say in the Gospel that only one sin is unforgivable, namely blasphemy against the Spirit, and they separate it from all other sins. But John the Evangelist, in his letter, does not name any one sin separately as unforgivable. Instead, he speaks of any sin at all, if it continues up to death.

How, then, do John’s letter and the Gospel agree? The Gospel denies this of every sin except one named sin; John affirms it of any sin whatever, and not of one sin separately. The Gospel says, “Only blasphemy against the Spirit is unforgivable.” John says, “Any sin that continues unto death deserves no prayer.”

The Gospel names blasphemy against the Spirit in a singular and definite way. The letter names nothing singularly, but speaks indefinitely of a sin unto death. When “sin unto death” is spoken of indefinitely, we understand it as any one sin, without specifying which. But “blasphemy against the Spirit” is understood not as just any sin, but as one definite sin.

In this way, John’s letter seems to disagree with the Gospel: the letter says that no one should pray for any sin that leads to death, while the Gospel says that blasphemy against the Spirit alone is the unforgivable sin.
But the problem is tangled only when we confuse things without thinking carefully. It is untangled when we distinguish them with care.

Beside every sin there stands either repentance or obstinacy. Repentance is good. The sin itself is evil. Obstinacy in sin for a time is worse. Obstinacy all the way to death is worst of all; it is the final evil.

So among evils, one comes first, another later, and another last. The first evils are things like fornication, theft, robbery, hatred, envy, and countless others. The later evil is impenitence for a time. The last evil is impenitence to the very end.

The first evils come before the others, like primary sins, and with respect to them a person may have either repentance or impenitence. For unless the first sin is committed, there is nothing for which someone may repent, or in which someone may remain impenitent.

From any evil, a person is first directed toward the first good, that is, repentance; forgiveness follows it. But from the first evil, a person may fall into the later evil, and from there into the last evil, which is impenitence to the end; unforgiveness follows that. When the sinner comes into those two evils—the later and the last—he becomes contemptuous, as Scripture says in the Book of Proverbs, chapter eighteen.
Here we should consider four opposites: two goods set against two evils. To make the relation clearer, imagine them arranged as a square. The upper line contains two things facing each other; the lower line likewise contains two things facing each other. In both lines, the direct opposition is clear. Along the two sides, the terms are related as things that follow one another.

On one side are the two goods that come through mercy: below, the good of virtue; above, the good of reward. On the other side are the two evils that come through justice: below, the evil of guilt; above, the evil of punishment. Diagonally, the terms oppose each other in such a way that they cannot coexist: two by the unchangeable character of justice, and two by the certainty of mercy, as the diagram below is meant to show.

So, when these distinctions are carefully considered, the Gospel’s statement that every sin is forgiven refers to the sins that come first, and also to impenitence for a time, which comes later. But blasphemy against the Spirit, which cannot be forgiven, is impenitence to the end, for which one should not pray.

Thus, although the Gospel speaks of many, indeed countless, sins that can be forgiven, there is one blasphemy against the Spirit that cannot be forgiven. This is the same as what John’s letter calls “the sin leading to death,” that is, impenitence to the end, for which alone one should not pray. Therefore, both in the three evangelists and in John, there is only one unforgivable sin: in the Gospel it is called blasphemy against the Spirit; in the letter it is called the sin leading to death, meaning impenitence to the end.

Why the Unforgivable Blasphemy Belongs to the Spirit Rather than to the Father or the Son

But since John marks this out and calls it, in a special sense, “a sin leading to death,” the question arises in the Gospel: why is it called blasphemy not against the Father, or against the Son, but only against the Spirit? The answer is this. The majesty of the supreme Trinity is wholly love, and each person in the supreme Trinity is most certainly love. Yet the Holy Spirit is love in a special and proper sense, because he is the true communion of the Father and the Word, and the sweet love by which they love one another and all things. And because love itself cannot admit its own opposite, it cannot hate anything that has been created. Rather, it loves all things — that love which, in the whole of what it is, is true love. For unless it loved every being, not even one thing could exist as it is. Some beings, however, have fallen into guilt. But true love loves even those who have sinned, and is ready to pardon them if they repent. For the love that truly loves gladly forgives an offence.
For the forgiveness of sins is the work that true love desires. And just as, within the supreme Trinity, love is properly related to the Spirit, so too the forgiveness of sins is properly ascribed to the Spirit, because forgiveness is the work of love. Even so, the power to forgive sins belongs in common to the whole Trinity.
So if the Holy Spirit is the person to whom the forgiveness of sins is properly attributed, then whatever most decisively shuts out the forgiveness of sins rises up most directly against the Holy Spirit. And whatever repels the pardon of sins with greater wickedness offends the Holy Spirit with greater injury. That is why this sin is rightly called blasphemy against the Spirit: it refuses the sweet work of love and does not care to receive the gift of pardon, which love desires to give to everyone. This is the sin leading to death: final impenitence, the ultimate evil, the only sin that cannot be forgiven, while every other sin can be forgiven. And this is what it means to speak against the Holy Spirit: by slandering divine works and twisting them into an evil interpretation, a person persists, enviously and proudly, without repentance all the way to the end. That is what the malicious Jews were doing at that time, when they attributed Christ’s divine powers to an unclean spirit. Therefore, seeing by divine knowledge their impenitence to the end, Christ declared that they were to be condemned forever, and he instructed later generations to beware so great an evil. We do find the orthodox Fathers speaking in other ways about these Gospel passages. Anyone who wants to know their interpretations should consult their writings.
