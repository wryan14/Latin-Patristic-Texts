
by Geoffroy Babion
Patrologia Latina Volume 


On the Two Cities of God and the Devil

The author begins with Scripture: “Come out of Babylon, my people, so that you do not share in her sins, and so that you do not receive any of her plagues. For her sins have reached all the way to heaven, and the Lord has remembered her injustices.” This is cited as the Book of Revelation, chapter eighteen, verse four.
So then, dearly beloved brothers, there are in this world two kingdoms, two rulers, and two households. One is the kingdom of God; the other is the kingdom of the devil. From the beginning of the world, the households of these two kingdoms have always opposed one another.

The kingdom of the devil is called Babylon. The kingdom of God is called Jerusalem. Babylon means “confusion.” Jerusalem means “vision of peace.”

But let us speak first about the city of God. In that city there is a strong wall. The prophet says of it: “Sion is our city of strength; the Savior will be placed in it as a wall.” This is cited as the book of Isaiah, chapter twenty-six, verse one. And this city has twelve gates, as was shown to John in his vision. John says: “The angel showed me the holy city, Jerusalem, which had a great wall and twelve gates.” This is cited as the Book of Revelation, chapter twenty-one, verse twelve.

This city is founded upon mountains. Thus David says: “Its foundations are on the holy mountains.” This is cited as Psalm eighty-six, verse one. There is also in it a very great tower, to which everyone may flee for safety. Solomon says of this tower: “The tower of David was built with defenses; a thousand shields hang upon it.” This is cited as the Song of Songs, chapter four, verse four. That tower reaches up to the heavens.

There are many other towers as well, though they are lower. David says of them: “May there be peace in your strength, Jerusalem, and abundance in your towers.” This is cited as Psalm one hundred twenty-one, verse seven.

There is never night in that city; it is always day. For John says: “There will be no night there.” This is cited as the Book of Revelation, chapter twenty-one, verse twenty-five. Yet there are always watchful guards there. The Church speaks of them in the Song of Songs: “The watchmen who guard the city found me.” This is cited as the Song of Songs, chapter three, verse three.

The Lord himself builds the city and keeps watch over it. As Scripture says: “Unless the Lord guards the city, the one who guards it keeps watch in vain.” This is cited as Psalm one hundred twenty-six, verse two.

There is no filth in this city. Everything is clean and bright. Everything blossoms; everything gives off fragrance. There is no anger, no discord, no disgrace, but rather a shared life of love. The citizens of that city care for what belongs to others just as carefully as they care for their own. They provide for one another and warn one another. Another person’s losses are their own losses. They feel another person’s pain, and they help relieve it.

There are constant heralds there, continually urging the citizens to look to their own safety. They reject no one who runs to them for refuge; instead, they protect such a person. They draw to themselves everyone they can. They do not know how to take or desire what belongs to another. Instead, they freely distribute what is their own.

They suffer nothing except at the hands of the citizens of Babylon, with whom they are always at war. Among the people with whom they live, however, the peace of God is always present.

Their food is this: to obey the will of their Lord and to seek to know what his will is. Their banquet dishes are the Lord’s commandments, more desirable to them than gold and topaz; and they remember his commandments in order to carry them out. They burn with longing for those who are absent, and they love them. They want to imitate the virtue of the good, not envy it.

No one dwells in this city unless that person is a soldier, because human life on earth is military service. All are always armed, because they must always fight. Their enemies do not know how to stop; they are always either laying traps or openly making war.
You have heard, brothers, the description of the city. You have heard the character of its citizens. But for a deeper understanding, that description must be explained.

The wall of the city is the guardianship of the angels, assigned to protect us. The city itself is the Church. The twelve gates are the twelve apostles, who by preaching and by writing opened the way to God for believers. The city is founded upon mountains because it is founded upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets.

The tower of refuge is God himself. David says of him: “Be for me a protecting God, and a house of refuge, a fortified place.” This is cited as Psalm thirty, verse three.

The other towers are the saints who stand out in virtue and who move others by their word and example. There is no night of unbelief in that city. The Apostle says of that night: “The night has passed; the day has drawn near.” This is cited as Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter thirteen, verse twelve.

The watchmen are bishops, priests, and other leaders, who keep watch over the Lord’s sheep. The heralds are preachers, to whom it is said: “Cry out; do not stop. Lift up your voice like a trumpet, and announce to my people their crimes.” This is cited as Psalm fifty-eight, verse one.
Babylon, by contrast, also has a wall: a legion of angels, that is, evil spirits. And it has five gates: the five senses of the body, through which one enters into confusion. David speaks of these gates when he says: “You lift me up from the gates of death.” This is cited as Psalm nine, verse fifteen.

Babylon has no tower. Instead, it has a dark pit, which is despair. From that pit the citizens of Jerusalem cannot rescue anyone. Scripture says of it: “When the sinner has come into the depth of sins, he despises.” This is cited as the book of Proverbs, chapter eighteen, verse three.

The devil first fell into this depth. Judas fell into it, and so did the Jews who are completely blinded and hardened.

In Babylon it is always night; that is, there is blindness of heart. The character of its citizens is like this: they love and acquire the riches of this world by every means—by theft, robbery, usury, bloodshed, and deceit. They devote themselves to feasting, drunkenness, and sexual indulgence.

There is no trust among them. They take pleasure in deceiving not only the citizens of Jerusalem, but also their own people, and even themselves. They envy everyone richer than they are. They rejoice in other people’s losses. They do not know how to show mercy.

They speak peace with their neighbor, but evil is in their hearts. The sinner borrows and does not repay. There is no truth in their mouth; their heart is empty and false. They swear harm against their neighbor and deceive him. They accept bribes against the innocent.

Some trust in chariots, and some in horses. All have turned aside; together they have become useless. There is no one who does good, not even one.

All the wicked dwell in this Babylon. They are always working to capture the citizens of Jerusalem, and they drag many people over to themselves.
To these people the Lord cries out, saying: “Come out of Babylon, my people.” But because the two households are mixed together in this world—because the good and the evil live side by side in bodily life—someone may be unsure how they are to come out. They do not come out by leaving bodily, but by becoming different in mind and heart.

That is what the Lord means when he says: “So that you do not share in her sins.” If you avoid the sins, you will also avoid the torments that come from the sins. And that is what he means when he says: “So that you do not receive any of her plagues.” For many are the scourges of the sinner: if not in the present life, then certainly in the future. Some punishments are for correction; others are for vengeance.
Bodily sicknesses and the loss of possessions are punishments meant to correct us. Eternal death, however, is punishment by way of vengeance. As the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter six, verse twenty-three: “The payment for sin is death”—that is, eternal death. So when Scripture says, “You will not receive any of her plagues,” we should understand this as referring to the punishments of hell, because temporary sufferings are common to everyone. But this Babylon really does receive plagues, because her sins have reached all the way to heaven—that is, they have come to God’s knowledge. God is said to “ignore” sins when he does not punish them. He is said to “remember” them when punishment is given for guilt. That is why Scripture says, “God remembered her injustices.” So then, dearly beloved brothers, you who have been deceived by the rulers of Babylon and by her citizens, and who have been led there into captivity: come out through repentance. Break the chains from your neck, captive daughter of Zion. And why, house of Israel, will you die? The price of your redemption has already been paid. The blood of Christ has been poured out. And yet you remain in prison! The Babylonians show you false riches, so that you will love them and abandon your Creator. Listen to how Moses rebukes you in the book of Deuteronomy, chapter thirty-two, verse eighteen: “You have abandoned the God who gave you birth, and you have forgotten the Lord, your Creator.”
Let the wicked abandon his way, and the unjust person abandon his thoughts. Let him return to the Lord, and the Lord will have mercy on him. Remember what Scripture says in the book of Isaiah, chapter forty, verse six: “All flesh is grass, and all its glory is like the flower of the field.” And David explains how flesh is like grass when he says in the Book of Psalms, Psalm eighty-nine, verse six: “In the morning it blooms and passes away; in the evening it falls, grows stiff, and dries up.” This means that a human being blooms in the morning—that is, in childhood and youth. But by evening he falls. He grows stiff as a corpse and clings to the dust. For after the living person comes the corpse; after the corpse, the worm; after the worm, ashes. That is said about the human person. But what about all the labor we spend on earthly things? Scripture says in the Book of Psalms, Psalm eighty-nine, verse ten: “Our years will be spent like the spider.” That is, as long as we live, our years are compared to a spider’s work. What does a spider do? It consumes its own insides to make a web, and it works so hard on that web only to catch cheap prey—flies. In the same way, the people of this world wear out their bodies by working, passing through many dangers, and earning a worthless reward: things that pass away.
How miserable they are! They will leave their riches to strangers, and their tombs will be their homes forever. For when the rich person dies, he will not take everything with him, and his glory will not go down with him. Brothers, we have shown that human beings, and all human affairs, are passing away. So do not let the Babylonians deceive you with false riches. Come out from them through repentance. And do not repent of only one sin, but of all of them. If someone has many sins and gives up all but one, he is like a man bound with many chains who breaks every chain except one—and that one chain is still enough to hold him. He is also like a sailor whose ship has many holes: if he stops up every hole except one, water still comes in through that one hole and drags the ship down to the bottom. He is also like someone with many arrows in his heart: if all the others are pulled out but one remains, that one arrow alone will cause his death. In the same way, if someone has many deadly sins and gives up all but one, that one sin alone is enough to condemn him. Yes, giving up some sins has value, because it may bring a lighter punishment in hell. But it does not bring a person to salvation. So anyone who gives up some sins, but not all, has not yet come out of Babylon. Still, he is near the gate, so to speak, and more ready to leave.
Mary had sinned much, and she wept much; and many sins were forgiven her, because she loved much, as we read in the Gospel of Luke, chapter seven. Peter sinned in one thing, because he denied the Lord; and he wept over that one thing. So weep in proportion to your guilt. If your sin is one, weep for one. If your sins are many, weep for many. Do you want to be set free? Then break your chains. After that, redeem yourself by almsgiving. For the Lord willed that some people should be rich and others poor. If everyone were rich, people would have no one through whom they could redeem their sins. Lazarus was placed at the rich man’s gate so that the rich man could see in whom he might redeem his sins. Therefore he remains without excuse, since he gave nothing to the poor man. Wash yourself, then, in the river of mercy, so that, after coming out of Babylon, you may be worthy to be received into Jerusalem. And may the Lord who dwells in Jerusalem send us help from his holy place and protect us from Zion: he who lives and reigns as God forever and ever. Amen.
