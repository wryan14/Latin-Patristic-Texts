
by Justus of Urgel
Patrologia Latina Volume 


Letter of Saint Justus of Urgel to Pope Sergius.

To my truly devoted lord, especially rich in the grace of God, and always blessed in Christ, the lord Pope Sergius: from Justus, bishop.

I know how diligently you have worked to bring relief to the poor. You have gained temporary comfort for them, and eternal reward for yourself. And judging that you have now turned yourself sufficiently and wholly toward the words of God, from which you have never been empty, for the cultivation of your soul.

For that reason, I have taken care to send you a spiritual gift under a physical form. If Christ makes it very pleasing to you—not because of any merit of ours, but by his own gift—then perhaps this will happen: even during the days of fasting, you will come to the table already refreshed in some way, and before receiving food you will feel that you have tasted something in advance.

By saying this, I am not arrogantly trying to recommend the value of what I am sending before you have approved it. Rather, because love stands between us—and in that love I possess you wholly—I presume upon your mind as I would upon my own, and I trust without any doubt that it will be acceptable to you.

Therefore, knowing that you are a brother of my heart in Christ Jesus, I decided that I should offer first to you this little book, a treatment of the canonical book called the Song of Songs, which we have recently produced by Christ’s illumination. In receiving it, please understand that you are not so much receiving what is ours, but what is yours, since you love such things.

I ask you to read it over often, with the careful attention by which you flourish in Christ. If you notice anything there that needs correction, please point it out with complete freedom. And then offer it to the other brothers in Christ to read over, or, if it should perhaps please you, to copy.

Now, because it happened that parchment was lacking, the same work was written in rather small letters, gathered into equal quires, and not surrounded with carefully prepared margins or outer presentation. So, if you judge the work to have any value—and as I said, by the Lord’s gift this very thing is yours—please see as soon as possible that it is copied with greater care and arranged in a more useful form.

If this work turns out to deserve praise, I will tell Christ that you are its author. But if it lies open to criticism, then, since it belongs to both of us, I will immediately take you yourself as my partner. For it is fitting to your dignity that the things God has given should be properly arranged, so that you receive not blame for negligence, but the fruit of love’s diligent work.

Then our brothers, who feast on the writings of the earlier Fathers, may also take pleasure in being nourished, in some way, by these more recent works that we offer them out of love—not because of our merit, but because of God’s gift and their own goodwill.

Farewell now in Christ, most devoted father, and support our littleness in the Lord with your holy prayers.

Saint Justus of Urgel. A Mystical Explanation of Solomon’s Song of Songs.


Chapter 1: The Church Longs for Christ’s Kiss.

The first verse says: “Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth.”

This is the voice of the Church as she comes to Christ for a kiss. She wants the offense removed—the offense that human beings had incurred before God by sinning. She longs to find peace: the peace in which, while we were enemies of God the Father, we are reconciled through his Son.

For this reason the prophet says to God, in the book of Isaiah, chapter twenty-six: “Lord, give us your peace, for you have given everything back to us.”

And in the Gospel of Luke, chapter fifteen, the father kissed the son who returned from a far country. In a certain sense, God gave this kiss to those who came to him when he willed that his only Son should be born as a human being. Through that Son, God also granted that we might cling to him in the bond of peace.

Finally, when Christ was born, the angels cried out together, as the Gospel of Luke, chapter eight, is cited here: “Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace to people of good will.” They cried this because, by those words, they clearly taught us that Christ himself is our peace.
The text continues: “For your breasts are better than wine, fragrant with the finest ointments.”

By Christ’s breasts, we may fittingly understand the apostles and evangelists. Through them we are nourished in faith and refreshed with spiritual food.

They are shown to be better than wine because the teaching of the Gospel is recognized as higher than the law given long ago. There, in the old law, was figure; here, in the Gospel, is truth. There was something like the sharpness of wine: it showed that one should love only one’s neighbor and hate one’s enemy. Here there is the sweetness of grace: it tells us to pray for our enemies and to love those who hate us.

The text also says that Christ’s breasts—that is, the apostles—are fragrant with the finest ointments. This means that they shone with such great virtues and such great miracles that the scent of their righteousness, spread far and wide, delighted those who saw them then and refreshes all who hear them even now.

As one of the apostles declares, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter two: “Thanks be to God, who always leads us in triumph in Christ Jesus, and for our sake makes known in every place the fragrance of the knowledge of him.”
The text says: “Your name is oil poured out; therefore the young women have loved you.”

God anointed this one—that is, Christ—with the oil of gladness above his companions, as Psalm forty-four says. Therefore his name also is like oil poured out. All who are called Christians take their name from Christ. They are anointed with chrism, baptized in his name, and filled with the Holy Spirit.

This is like the ointment that descends from the head onto Aaron’s beard, as Psalm one hundred thirty-two says. That ointment signifies what is true, perfect, and most powerful: the Holy Spirit. The Spirit, poured out by God upon Christ, descends even to the edge of his garment and fills the whole Church to the very end. And because the Church clings to Christ, she is without doubt his garment.

For at the transfiguration, as we read in the Gospel, Christ’s face shone like the sun, and his clothing became white as snow. The printed source cites the Gospel of Matthew, chapter seventeen, and the Gospel of Luke, chapter nine. No fuller on earth could make clothing like that. For no teacher has ever led his disciples to such brightness as Christ has, nor has anyone else so cleansed those who believe in him from stains and made them white like snow.

Therefore the young women loved him. These young women are the new churches, or uncorrupted souls. Filled with this fragrant ointment, they say with the Apostle, in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter five: “The love of God has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us.”
The text says: “Draw me after you; we will run after your fragrance.”

The Lord says in the Gospel of John, chapter six: “No one comes to me unless the Father who sent me draws him.”

A person is drawn to Christ when that person believes in Christ. But those who run after him are those who imitate him, or those who, by Christ’s own gift, make progress by following him.
The text says: “The king has brought me into his storerooms.”

Christ brought the Church into his storerooms when he revealed to her the hidden mysteries of the Scriptures. As the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter two, God did this “so that in the ages to come he might show the abundant riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus.”

Or, in another sense, the saints are brought completely into Christ’s storerooms when at the end it will be said to them, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-five: “Come, you blessed of my Father; receive the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.”
The text says: “We will rejoice and be glad in you, remembering your breasts more than wine.”

The saints rejoice and are glad in the Lord when they remember how they were nourished with spiritual milk through the breasts of Christ—that is, through the teachings of the apostles.

And when they perceive that Christ’s teaching is brighter than the commandments of the ancient law, they understand it, beholding Christ in that teaching and from it. To them he says in the Gospel: “Blessed are your eyes, because they see, and your ears, because they hear. Amen, I say to you: many prophets and kings desired to see what you see, and did not see it; and to hear what you hear, and did not hear it.” The printed source cites the Gospel of Matthew, chapter thirteen, and the Gospel of Luke, chapter ten.
The text says: “The upright love you.”

The crooked cannot love righteousness. Those are truly upright, and truly love God, who are not held captive by love for created things. Instead, as is fitting for upright people, they set the precious gift first and cling with the strongest love to God, the maker of all things.

Those also are proved to love God rightly who give thanks in the same way not only when they receive his benefits, but also when they are placed under his chastisements. One such person said in Psalm thirty-three: “I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall always be in my mouth.” That means: I bless him both when I receive joy from his gift, and when I receive pain as he corrects me.

Likewise another man, tested by an extremely violent suffering, with his flesh diseased but his heart sincere, said: “If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord, why should we not endure evil things? The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it pleased the Lord, so it has happened. Blessed be the name of the Lord.”
The bride says: “I am dark, but beautiful, daughter of Jerusalem, like the tents of Kedar.” She is dark through the confession of sins, but beautiful through the grace of the sacrament. She is a daughter of Jerusalem because she was born through the Gospel from that first Church, the Church of the first believers in Jerusalem. Or, as the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter four: “The Jerusalem above is free, and she is our mother.” And rightly so, because she compares herself to the tents of Kedar; Kedar means “darkness.” To Kedar’s children the Apostle says, in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter five: “You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord.” She can also be called dark when she is handed over to sufferings, insults, abuse, and contempt. Look at how she is both dark and beautiful in Paul’s words, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter four: “We suffer tribulation in every way, but we are not crushed; we are at a loss, but not abandoned; we are struck down, but we do not perish.” She is “like the tents of Kedar” because, as Scripture says, “we have become like the refuse of this world, the scum of everyone.” And she is like “the curtains of Solomon” because, in the Apostle’s words, we are “always carrying around in our body the death of Jesus Christ, so that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in our mortal flesh.”
She says: “Do not stare at me because I am dark, because the sun has discolored me.” That means: do not despise me because I have been placed in persecution. I have endured the burning heat of tribulations, and I have, as it were, lost the beauty of temporary happiness. As Scripture says in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter fourteen: “Through many tribulations I must enter the kingdom of God.” I belong among those of whom it is written in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five: “Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.” And again, in the Gospel of John, chapter sixteen: “You will indeed have sorrow, and the world will rejoice; but your sorrow will be turned into joy.”
She says: “The sons of my mother fought against me.” That is, the sons of the Synagogue fought against her: those who stoned the martyr Stephen while he was teaching and rebuking them. As Scripture says in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter seven: “A great persecution arose against the Church in Jerusalem, and all the brothers were scattered throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria.” Or this may also refer to those who fought against the Church by pursuing Paul from city to city while he was preaching among the Gentiles. Likewise, it includes those who thought the burdens of the Law should be imposed on believers who came from the Gentiles—burdens which blessed James, together with the other apostles, wisely decided should be removed, as the Acts of the Apostles, chapter five, records. And those who persuaded the Galatians who believed in Christ to be circumcised are also shown to have fought against the Church, because they disturbed the evangelical tradition. About them the Apostle Paul says, in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter five: “Even those who are circumcised do not keep the Law; they only want you to be circumcised so that they may boast in your flesh.” And about the same people he also says, in the same passage: “I wish those who are troubling you would cut themselves off.”
She says: “They made me keeper of the vineyards.” These vineyards are the synagogues. Saul had received authority and letters from the chief priests so that, if he found any men or women who believed in Christ, he could hand them over to prison and punishment, as the Acts of the Apostles, chapter eleven and chapter twenty-two, relate. While he was doing this, he was, in a sense, guarding the vineyard of the Synagogue. Then the bride adds: “But my own vineyard I did not keep.” For Saul became Paul, and the persecutor became a preacher. When he preached the word of God to the Jews, and they contradicted him and utterly rejected him, he said to those unbelievers, as told in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter thirteen: “It was necessary that the word of God should be preached to you first. But since you have rejected it, and judged yourselves unworthy of so great a salvation, behold, we turn to the Gentiles.” For the Lord commanded us through Scripture, in the book of Isaiah, chapter forty-nine: “I have made you a light for the Gentiles, so that you may be salvation to the ends of the earth.” Or, as Paul himself wrote in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter one: “You have heard of my former way of life in Judaism: how I persecuted the Church of God and tried to destroy it, and how I advanced in Judaism beyond many of my own age among my people, being far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers.” And again, in Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter three: “But whatever things were gains for me, these I have considered losses.” In this way Paul speaks, as if under a figure: “They made me keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I did not keep.”
She says: “Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where you pasture your flock, where you lie down at noon.” We read in the Gospel: “If they say, ‘Look, here is Christ,’ or, ‘Look, there he is,’ do not go out.” That warning appears in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-four, and in the Gospel of Mark, chapter thirteen. For “here” and “there” indicate divided parties. But Christ rests in the catholic Church as at noon: he has flooded the whole world with his brightness, he finds rest in those who are fervent in spirit, and from the love by which he illumines the entire world, he daily feeds the peoples who believe in him.
She says: “Do not let me begin to wander among the flocks of your companions.” That is: if I fail to recognize that you dwell in the brightness where the whole community of your faithful people stands, then I will begin to stray among the flocks of your companions—meaning, among the gatherings of heretics. Heretics can, in one respect, be called companions of Christ, because they confess his birth, his passion, and his resurrection. But because the Apostle says of them, in Paul’s second letter to Timothy, chapter five, “They have the outward form of godliness, but deny its power,” they are proven to be not companions any longer, but opponents.
Christ speaks to the Church: “If you do not know yourself, O most beautiful among women, go out and follow the tracks.” This is the voice of Christ to the Church: “If you do not know yourself, O beautiful among women”—if you do not understand that you are the one to whom it is said in the Psalms, psalm forty-four, “Listen, daughter, and see; incline your ear, and forget your people and your father’s house, because the king desires your beauty,” since you alone have received the beauty of the incorruptible sacrament. And he rightly says, “among women,” meaning: glorious among the heresies. For the heresies are fittingly called “women,” because they have lost the virginity that exists in Christ. So do not wander by looking for me anywhere outside yourself. I rest within you, where until now you were not looking for me, as though at noonday. Now understand where you should look for me as the one who feeds and governs you, so that the imitation practiced by others does not carry you away as if into the night. Since sure sincerity endures only in you, because I reign within you, know that you belong among those to whom, after my resurrection, I truly promised in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-eight: “Behold, I am with you until the completion of the age.” But if you waver in doubt about my promises, and recognize neither my presence nor your own beauty, then “go out and follow the tracks of your flocks.” Such a person rightly follows the tracks of the flocks, because she has abandoned the presence of the shepherd. And “feed your young goats beside the tents of the shepherds”—that is, delight unjust people with the traditions and little assemblies of the heretics. These rebukes are deserved by those who do not recognize Christ in the catholic Church, or who do not recognize that they themselves are the catholic Church.
He says: “I have compared you to my cavalry among the chariots of Pharaoh.” The prophet Habakkuk says, in the book of Habakkuk, chapter one: “Were you angry with the rivers, Lord? Was your wrath against the rivers, or your indignation against the sea, when you mounted your horses, and your chariots were salvation?” We understand these horses to be the apostles and evangelists. Therefore Christ says to the Church: I compared you to my cavalry among the chariots of Pharaoh when I turned the wise people of this world, calling them to my faith. I did this so that, once they had laid aside the swelling pride of the age, they might make progress by running under me, for I am true salvation. By carrying me through devout preaching, they would make me visible to the eyes of believers through faith. And just as the ruler of this world, who has now been cast out, used false philosophers like splendid chariots for destruction, so I, through apostles filled with my wisdom, will train believers for salvation.
He says: “Your cheeks are beautiful, like those of a turtledove.” He is praising chastity as the companion of modesty. For it is said that turtledoves, once they have lost their bodily mate, go about alone and do not seek another physical union. So the bride of Christ possesses exceptional beauty when she preserves chastity together with modesty.
He says: “Your neck is like necklaces.” The neck signifies those who never cease to meditate on the law of God day and night, and who, like clean animals, continually chew over and bring forth saving doctrine. Because such people are splendid ornaments of the Church, they are described as the necklaces of the bride.
The speakers say: “Let us make little golden chains for you.” This is the voice of the teachers. They arrange ornaments for the Church, mixed with gold and silver, when they explain spiritual meanings with the brilliance of eloquence. These little chains flow down from the head and are normally fitted to adorn the neck. For that reason, those teachings are truly excellent which proceed from the head—that is, from Christ—and serve in such a spiritual way that they teach our neck to submit to the yoke of Christ. To such people the Apostle says, in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eleven: “Do not set your mind on high things,” and so on.
“While the king was on his couch.” The Gospels tell us—in the Gospel of Mark, chapter fourteen, and the Gospel of John, chapter twelve—that two days before the Passover, while the Lord was reclining at table, a woman who had an alabaster jar of pure spikenard ointment poured it over his head. And here the prophecy says, “While the king was on his couch, my spikenard gave forth its fragrance.” So both the prophetic words and the Gospel event are understood figuratively. For when our King was in his own place of rest—either on the cross, where he bowed his head and gave up his spirit, or in the tomb, where his body was laid and rested—the Church anointed him with the fragrant ointment of a faithful confession. She did this so that she might never cease to proclaim, for the salvation of believers, that he suffered willingly and rose from the dead on the third day by his own power.
“My beloved is a bundle of myrrh.” Myrrh is known as a spice suitable for embalming the bodies of the dead. So Christ is the bundle of myrrh: Christ who suffered, died, and was buried. “He will remain between my breasts.” Those who never stop proclaiming the Lord’s death in every way, as they have been commanded, until he comes, are without doubt proved to be the breasts of the Church: learned teachers who overflow with spiritual milk and nourish the peoples being born in the faith.
“My beloved is a cluster of Cyprus.” The cluster in the Cyprus tree is Christ hanging on the wood of the cross. That kind of wood is always fragrant and incorruptible; and indeed, with the fragrance of chrism, we mark the sign of the cross on the forehead. He rightly says, “My beloved is a cluster of Cyprus to me,” because the sweetness of this cluster was granted not to those who crucified him, but to those who believed in the Crucified One. “In the vineyards of Engaddi” means among the peoples of the Synagogue, where the Lord’s passion took place. “Engaddi” is interpreted as “the eye of my testing.” Read the Gospel, and you will find that the Pharisees and Sadducees aimed at nothing else with their questions except to test the Lord at every point and to find grounds for accusing him. To them he says, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-two, “Why do you test me, hypocrites?” Yet the immense blessing of Christ’s passion, though carried out among them, is clearly granted to us who have believed.
“Behold, you are beautiful, my friend.” Christ calls the Church beautiful twice, because by the kiss of his peace he has made her his friend. In the same way, the Apostle teaches, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter seven, that she is holy in body and in spirit. Christ then adds, “Your eyes are like doves.” Nothing adorns the face of a body more than the proper placement of the eyes. By these eyes we rightly understand the holy apostles, who were even able to look ahead to future things. Describing their privilege, the blessed apostle Paul writes to the Corinthians, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter twelve, “You are the body of Christ.” And in another place, in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter four, he says that God has appointed certain people in the Church, first of all apostles, and so on. So when Christ says, “Your eyes are like doves,” he is clearly pointing to the apostles themselves. They were commanded, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter ten, to be wise as serpents and innocent as doves. And by comparing them to this bird, Christ also shows that they were filled with the Holy Spirit.
“Behold, you are beautiful, my beloved.” The Church here calls Christ beautiful and lovely, and the Psalmist proclaims the same thing when he says, in Psalm forty-four, “You are beautiful in form beyond the sons of men; grace has been poured out on your lips.” But your beauty, Christ, comes from this: as the Gospel of John, chapter one, says, “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God”; and again, “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.” Then the Church adds, “Our little bed is flowering.” By this she points to the Lord’s own body, in which the Godhead rested: pure, fragrant, and born from the Virgin.
“The roofs of our houses.” These houses in which Christ dwells are the saints, as the prophet says, in Leviticus, chapter twenty-six, and as Paul repeats in his second letter to the Corinthians, chapter six: “I will dwell in them.” The roofs and ceilings of these houses are made of cedar and cypress, woods known to be incorruptible and fragrant. They represent the love of God and of neighbor, granted from above through the Holy Spirit—that is, so that the saints may be holy within themselves.

Chapter 2: Christ the Lily and the Beloved’s Coming

“I am the flower of the field and the lily of the valleys.” That is: I am the beauty of the world, born from the virgin purity of the humble.
“Like a lily among thorns.” When the human being was driven out of Paradise, Scripture says in the book of Genesis, chapter three: “Cursed is the earth because of your works; it will bring forth thorns and thistles for you.” By that earth we should understand human flesh, which is accustomed to produce the sharp points of lust and the thorns of disordered desires.

So when the text says, “Like a lily among thorns, so is my beloved among the daughters,” by “daughters” he means heresies. Because they have in a sense been reborn through baptism, they are called by the name of daughters. Yet, since they are compared to thorns, they are shown to be corrupting and corruptible. The Church knows nothing of that corruption. Compared to the purity of lilies, she gives off fragrance and shines on every side.
“Like an apple tree among the trees of the forest.” The psalmist says in the Book of Psalms, Psalm eighty-eight: “Who in the clouds can be compared to the Lord? Or who is like God among the sons of God?” In that passage, the others are sons by adoption; here, Christ is Son by nature. Therefore no one among the saints, and no one in the whole human race, however much he may be called by the name of son, is found to be like Christ. Christ surpasses the elect with incomparable beauty, because of the sweetness and nourishment of his fruit.
“Under the shadow of the one I had desired.” Protected by the very one whose presence I had always sought, I remained humble and secure.
“And his fruit was sweet to my throat.” In the Gospel of John, chapter fifteen, the Lord says: “I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever remains in me, and I in him, bears much fruit, because without me you can do nothing.”

So even though fruit appears on the branch, it must still be credited to the vine, because the branch and its fruit come from the vine’s root. In the same way, whatever fruit the righteous produce in good works must be referred entirely to Christ. In his fruits there is incomparable sweetness. And those saints who, under the image of the throat, are accustomed to chew over what they have tasted—that is, to meditate on the things of Christ—also receive a very great sweetness from his fruit.
“He brought me into the wine cellar.” When the Lord was about to ascend into heaven, he commanded the apostles not to leave Jerusalem. He said to them, “Rejoice, because I have conquered the world and I am going to the Father. But you, remain in the city until you are clothed with power from on high.”

And, as it is written in the Acts of the Apostles, chapters one and two: “Then they returned to Jerusalem from the mount called Olivet, which is near Jerusalem, a Sabbath day’s journey away. And when they had entered the upper room, they went up to where Peter and John were staying.” A little later it says: “All these were gathered together with one mind, with the women, and with Mary the mother of Jesus, and with his brothers.” And the crowd of people in that place was about one hundred twenty. When the Holy Spirit came upon them, they were filled; and when they began to speak in different languages, the people thought they were full of new wine.

So the Church is brought into the wine cellar when this apostolic gathering, remaining in unity, is filled with the Holy Spirit. As the Apostle also says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter twelve: “In one Spirit we were all baptized, whether slaves or free, and we were all made to drink of one Spirit.”

Because love is poured into the hearts of the faithful through the Holy Spirit, the text immediately adds: “He ordered love in me.” This is ordered love: that a person loves his neighbor as himself, but loves God in such a way that he even denies himself for God’s sake. Even in wicked people, what is loved is not the wrongdoing they commit, but the creature in them, because they were made good by God.

This too is ordered love: that servants should not experience the Lord as if they were criminals, nor love one another with a shameful, indulgent kind of love. Instead, when necessary, they should freely correct one another, after superstition has been cast out. As Scripture says in the Book of Proverbs, chapter twenty-seven: “The wounds of one who loves are better than the deceitful kisses of one who flatters.” And again, in the Book of Proverbs, chapter twenty-four: “Those who give correction will be praised, and blessing will come upon them.”
“Support me with flowers; surround me with apples.” She longs to be joined to, and surrounded by, the assemblies of the saints, because she is faint with a holy and chaste love. This faintness is not the weakness of sickness, but the weakness of an inexpressible desire. As it says in the Book of Psalms, Psalm one hundred eighteen: “My soul has fainted for your salvation, and I have hoped beyond hope in your word. My eyes have failed as they look for your promise, saying, ‘When will you comfort me?’”

Blessed are those who advance by such progress. For, in a certain sense, the person who longs intensely grows faint and fails until he finds what he hopes for and seeks.
“His left hand is under my head.” Solomon himself says in the Book of Proverbs: “Blessed is the person who finds wisdom and overflows with prudence. Length of days is in her right hand, and in her left hand are riches and glory.”

So the chosen Church of Christ, by having his left hand under her head, shows that temporal things are placed beneath her. And by saying that his right hand embraces her, she teaches that she desires eternal things.
“I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles.” By “daughters of Jerusalem” he means the holy and peaceful souls of teachers. He adjures them by clean and swift animals, which never stop chewing the cud and which feed in the fields of the holy Scriptures, not to make the beloved awaken until she herself wishes.

We know that the apostle Paul acted in this way when, regarding virgins preserving holiness, he gave not a command but counsel, as in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter seven. And in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter nineteen, the rich man is told: “If you wish to be perfect, go, sell everything you have, and give to the poor.” In another place, those eunuchs are praised who have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven, and immediately it is added: “Let the one who can accept this accept it.”

Again, when the Apostle urges continence, he says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter seven: “I say this for your benefit, not to throw a noose around you, but to encourage what is honorable, and what gives freedom to pray to God without hindrance.” And again, in Paul’s letter to Philemon: “I did not want to do anything without your consent, so that your good deed would not be as if forced by necessity, but voluntary.”

So the beloved is in no way awakened until she wishes, when the vows of the saints are offered to the Lord by their own free will.
“The voice of my beloved.” This voice of Christ, which is only mentioned in this prophecy, may rightly be understood as the voice by which, in the Gospel, the race of dead humanity is raised. Christ himself declares in the Gospel of John, chapter five: “Amen, amen, I say to you, the hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice, and those who have done good will come forth to the resurrection of life.”

And in the Book of Revelation, chapter twenty, it says: “Blessed is the one who has a share in the first resurrection.” A person has a share in the first resurrection when he is rescued from an evil habit and, as if from death, rises again because Christ gives him life.
“Look, here he comes, leaping over the mountains.” Christ comes leaping over the mountains when he appears, more perfect than the perfect. He comes bounding over the hills when, by the excellence of his majesty, he surpasses every height of the saints.
“My beloved is like a gazelle.” He is like a gazelle because, as Paul says in his letter to the Romans, chapter eight, he appeared to us “in the likeness of sinful flesh.” He is also like a young stag, because according to the flesh he took his origin from the saints who conquered the serpent.
“See, he stands behind our wall.” He stands behind our wall when he is clothed with the covering of our body.
“Looking through the windows.” Since he concealed something of himself and revealed something, he looked out, as it were, through windows and latticework. He did not show himself completely, especially according to his divinity. This is why he said to Philip, who wanted to see the Father, in the Gospel of John, chapter fourteen: “Have I been with you so long, and you have not known me?” And in another place in the same chapter: “The one who loves me keeps my commandments, and I will love him and reveal myself to him.”

Or we may say that he looked through the windows and latticework when he taught the people in parables. It can also be understood that he looked through the windows at the time of his Passion, when his side was pierced by the lance and blood and water flowed out, bringing forth the sacraments of redemption and washing.
“My beloved speaks to me.” The beloved is described often in this book. For who is so beloved to the Church as the one for whom the martyrs laid down their lives?

“Rise, hurry, and come.” Rise—that is, believe. Hurry—that is, after completing the course of devoted labor, receive the reward. “My beloved,” because you have been reconciled to me through death. “My beautiful one,” because you have been made clean through the washing of baptism.
“For now the winter has passed.” That is, temporary tribulation has come to an end.
“The rain is gone and has withdrawn.” That is, frequent and many-sided temptation, or the persecution by pagans, has ceased.
“Flowers have appeared on the earth.” The saints have abounded in the world, springing up in the month of new things, when Easter is celebrated. To them the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Colossians, chapter three: “If you have risen with Christ, set your mind on the things above, not on the things that are on the earth.”
“The time of pruning has arrived.” Those who are planted in the house of the Lord are purified from every superstition by the pruning hook of discipline, and so they are prepared for the fruits that are to come.
“The voice of the turtledove has been heard in the land.” In the Gospel, Anna, a most chaste widow eighty-four years old, never left the temple. With fasting and prayers she recognized Christ, and she spoke about him to everyone who was waiting for the redemption of Israel, as told in the Gospel of Luke, chapter two. God also commanded that a pair of these birds be offered in sacrifice, because nothing is truly excellent in an offering to God unless the faithful themselves are offered to the Lord, sanctified in body and in spirit.
“The fig tree has brought out its early fruit.” In the end, even the Synagogue brought forth Israelites who believed in Christ. As the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eleven: “I do not want you to be ignorant of this mystery: a blindness has come upon part of Israel until the fullness of the nations comes in, and so all Israel will be saved.”
“The vineyards are in bloom and have given their fragrance.” Communities of clergy, monks, and virgins grow and flourish within the Church, and they become known through their good reputation and their holy way of life.
“Rise up, my friend, my beautiful one.” With these loving names, Christ continually urges the Church to leave behind temporal things and to long for eternal things.
“My dove in the clefts of the rock.” The Church is called a dove for no other reason than that she is without deceit and is filled with the Holy Spirit. She lives in the clefts of the rock because she always finds refuge in the very wounds of Christ, the wounds by which she has been healed. She is also received into the hollow of the wall when she is warmed and strengthened by the holy consolation of the Scriptures, or when she is completely protected by the merits and prayers of the saints who have gone before her. In these clefts of the rock, the apostle Thomas found protection like a dove. After the resurrection, when he touched the places of Christ’s wounds, all doubt was removed, and he cried out in faith, as recorded in the Gospel of John, chapter twenty: “My God and my Lord.”
“Show me your face, Zion.” The name Zion is interpreted as “contemplation,” and rightly refers to those who say with the Apostle, in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter three: “We, contemplating the glory of God, are being transformed into the same image, from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord.” And again: “We look not at the things that are seen, but at the things that are unseen. For the things that are seen are temporary, but the things that are unseen are eternal.” Zion, then, is invited always to stand before God, like Elijah, who said in the Fourth Book of Kings, chapter three: “The Lord lives, in whose sight I stand today.” She must not be among those whom the prophet rebukes in the book of Jeremiah, chapter two: “They have turned their back to me, and not their face.” And, it says, “your voice is in my ears”—cited in the printed text as the Third Book of Kings, chapter seventeen. And in Psalm thirty-three: “The righteous cried out, and the Lord heard them, and delivered them from all their troubles.”
“For your voice is sweet.” The prayers of the saints are always pleasing to the Lord and always present before his grace, because they have received from the light of Christ the power to remain in the beauty of righteousness.
“Catch the little foxes for us.” In the Gospel of Matthew, chapter thirteen, the Lord says in a parable that when the grass had grown and produced fruit, then the weeds appeared. In other words, as the Church advanced, many heresies also arose; or else, while that faith endured, the heresies were found out in what they wrongly believed. So too Samson, whose name is interpreted as “sun,” caught three hundred foxes, set fire to their tails, and burned the crops of his enemies, as told in the book of Judges, chapter fifteen. Heretics always appear impressive at the beginning, as though distinguished by a religion that is only feigned; but at their end they drag fire behind them, being destined for the fire of Gehenna. Therefore, “Catch the foxes for us” means: refute the heretics, and trap their tricks with the testimonies of the holy Scriptures. He calls them “little foxes” because, according to the rule of right faith, there is nothing great in their understanding. Whatever wisdom they claim, they put it all on display. Though they are small, they destroy the vineyards; they overthrow peoples by corrupting them with evil traditions. “Our vineyard has flourished”: the Catholic Church advances with growth that cannot be turned aside.
“My beloved is mine, and I am his.” The Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter five: “The two will be one flesh. This is a great mystery; but I am speaking of Christ and the Church.” Speaking of Christ, the same Church says in Psalm fifteen: “The Lord is the portion of my inheritance.” And again, the Father says to the Son in Psalm two: “Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession.” Therefore Christ has the Church in his body, and the Church has Christ as her head. Because this bond cannot be broken apart, she rightly says: “My beloved is mine.”
“He pastures among the lilies.” Christ is said to pasture among the lilies when his body, which is the Church, is adorned with communities of saints and virgins.
“Until the day breathes and the shadows turn away.” This means: until the gospel tradition advances in a clearer light, and the figures of the Old Testament—which had gone before like shadows in the ritual of sacrifices and in symbolic observances—are driven away.
“Return, my beloved; be like a gazelle or a young stag.” When the text suggests that the beloved goes back, and when it compares him to a gazelle and a young stag, it shows that Christ’s ascension into heaven is being compared to clean and swift animals. These animals leap quickly over the higher places of the mountains. Christ, however, has surpassed everything that appears exalted, because the Apostle says of him in Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter two: “Therefore God exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bend, in heaven, on earth, and under the earth, and every tongue should confess that the Lord Jesus is in the glory of God the Father.” Now Bethel means “house of God.” So the phrase “upon the mountains of Bethel” proclaims that Christ is more powerful than all the saints who are in heaven and on earth.

Chapter 3: The Bride Searches for Christ and Beholds His Church

“On my bed, through the nights, I sought him.” Among those who sleep in the faith, and who are blotted out by the darkness of ignorance, the Church sought Christ, the one she loves. But notice what follows: “I sought him and did not find him.” The Lord says to Adam in Genesis, chapter three: “By the sweat of your face you shall eat your bread.” For those who collapse through some vice, or through the drowsiness of laziness, do not quickly come to eat that bread which came down from heaven. Rather, the people who keep watch through holy labor are the ones who find him.
“I will rise and go around the city.” I considered all the glory of the present age. I looked into the gatherings of unbelievers and the various schools of the philosophers. But even there I did not find the one whom my soul loves.
“The watchmen found me.” The wise people of this world and the worshipers of idols argued against me. They investigated many things in vain and produced many books with fruitless labor. To them I said: Why are you exhausting yourselves in useless effort? Why are you wearing out your minds so fiercely, and for nothing? Have you seen the one whom my soul loves? Since you boast under the false name of wisdom, God has made your wisdom foolish, as Paul says in his first letter to the Corinthians, chapter two. For you were not able to find Christ there.
“When I had passed on a little way.” When I had passed beyond people of this sort—people who are considered famous in the world because of empty learning—and when I had resisted their superstitious inventions and judged their wisdom to be foolish, then my soul found the one she loves. That is why the Apostle says about such things in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter one: “We speak wisdom among the mature, but not the wisdom of this age. Rather, we speak God’s wisdom in a mystery, the hidden wisdom which God predestined before the ages for our glory.” And because that wisdom itself, which is Christ, is not found among such people, Paul continues: “None of the rulers of this age knew it. For if they had known it, they would never have crucified the Lord of glory.” And again: “As it is written: What no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has not entered the human heart—these are the things God has prepared for those who love him.” But because the bride finds the one she loves among spiritual people, listen to what follows: “God has revealed it to us through his Spirit; for the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God.”
“I held him, and I will not let him go.” In this way I will cling to Christ and persevere in faith in him, until, by preaching him among the people of the synagogue, as in the house of my mother, I bring him into the chamber of the one who bore me. Then the Jews, who until now have denied him with their mouths, may receive him in their hearts. And when the eyes of their minds have been enlightened, they may at last find him in that inner chamber: that is, in the hidden meaning of the holy Scriptures, where he has stood all along, though he was revealed to us earlier. Or else Christ is brought into the house of his mother when the Christian people, who are his body, are joined to the heavenly Jerusalem. There is the reality of which Scripture says: “What no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and what has not entered the human heart,” and so on.
“I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem.” He has already set down this saying earlier, and, as God heard us, we remember that we examined it then with supporting testimonies. But I think it is repeated again in this book for this reason: no teacher should entice future believers with gifts or frighten them with threats. Instead, he should simply preach what is holy and bring people to faith and religion willingly, whether they are already coming to believe or are being converted.
“Who is this who comes up through the desert?” The Church comes up through the desert when the people of the nations advance in faith and fill up the multitude of believers. For of the Church it is sung in the Psalms, in psalm one hundred six and psalm one hundred thirteen: “He turned the desert into pools of water, and the waterless land into springs of water.” The “little columns of smoke from spices” mean the prayers that rise up to the Lord from the saints, prayers born from a pierced and repentant heart. Smoke, after all, often brings tears to the eyes. Myrrh, frankincense, and every powdered perfume point to the signs of mortification, or to the different gifts of grace that overflow among the chosen.
“Look at Solomon’s bed.” Solomon’s bed is the resting place of Christ. This resting place is rightly understood as the Church, which we read was chosen, according to its number, in the Psalms, in Psalm seventy-five: “The Lord has chosen Zion; he has chosen her as a dwelling for himself. This is my resting place forever and ever; here I will dwell, because I have chosen her.” Now the number sixty is fitting here, because sixty is complete in all its parts and rises by way of the number ten. So it is fitting that the strongest men of Israel are described as those who, while the Lord is present, are appointed to guard the Church. To the Church herself the prophet says, in Isaiah, chapter sixty-two: “Upon your walls, Jerusalem, I have appointed watchmen; all day and all night they will never keep silent.” These are “all holding swords, most skilled in battle,” and “each one’s sword is upon his thigh.” For to these people the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter six: “Stand, therefore, with your loins girded in truth. Put on the breastplate of righteousness. Let your feet be shod with readiness for the Gospel of peace. In all things take up the shield of faith, with which you can extinguish all the flaming arrows of the evil one. Take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God.” Therefore, those are the people most skilled in battle who know how to fight against the desires of their own flesh. And the person usefully carries a sword upon his thigh who puts to death bodily impulses and lusts by spiritual nourishment. So whoever is found in the Church living in this way is known to be one of the strong, from the strongest of Israel, because he contemplates God.
“King Solomon made himself a carrying couch.” Where a serving of food is described, we may understand the faith of believers, by which Christ is perceived. For when the Lord was speaking with the Samaritan woman at the well, and she went back and announced his majesty to the others who lived in the city, his disciples arrived and urged him, saying, “Rabbi, eat.” But he said to them, in the Gospel of John, chapter four: “I have food to eat that you do not know about.” And a little later the Gospel says: “Many Samaritans from that city believed in him.” He showed that their faith would become food for him, when shortly before he had told the disciples that he would eat something they did not know about. But in this passage, as the words that follow make clear, the term “carrying couch” refers not so much to the food itself as to the object in which something is carried—or, as another edition has it, a portable litter. “From the wood of Lebanon” means: from the most excellent saints in the Church.
“He made its columns of silver.” Christ prepared the supports of the Church from his learned teachers and apostles. As Paul, the chosen vessel, wrote in his letter to the Galatians, chapter two: “When James, Cephas, and John, who seemed to be pillars, recognized the grace of God, they gave me and Barnabas the right hand of fellowship.”
“Its reclining place was gold.” Christ finds a place to recline, and to feast well, in those who are gifted with sincere understanding and shine like gold in the holy congregation.
“Its ascent was purple.” The purple ascent teaches us about the outstanding perfection of the martyrs in the shedding of their blood.
“Its middle was built with love.” There is no other reason why Christ built all these adornments of the Church except this: he loved us and handed himself over for us, as Paul says in his letter to the Ephesians, chapter five, “an offering and sacrifice to God, a sweet-smelling fragrance.”
“Go out and see, daughters of Zion.” Holy souls, always keep before your eyes King Solomon—our peace-maker, who is Christ. See how he endured his passion, as the Gospel of Mark, chapter fifteen, and the Gospel of John, chapter nineteen, proclaim, so that he might free the human race from destruction. Look at him “in the diadem with which his mother crowned him”: that is, in the crown of thorns which the synagogue placed upon his head on the day of his betrothal. For he betrothed the whole Church to himself at the moment when he ascended the gibbet of the cross and, from that same cross, taught all who believe in him the sign to place upon their foreheads. As he himself says in the Gospel of John, chapter twelve: “When I have been lifted up from the earth, I will draw all things to myself.” That day of his passion was also the day of his heart’s joy, because through his suffering Christ granted good things to the faithful as his own possession, for generations and for many ages.

Chapter 4: Christ Praises the Church's Beauty and Gifts

“How beautiful you are, my friend.” By repeating the phrase, Christ emphasizes the beauty he is praising. Or else, by using these images, he is signaling that we should carefully consider the different kinds of holy people that he is about to introduce under these comparisons.
“Your eyes are those of doves, apart from what lies within.” The simplicity of spiritual people deserves the deepest reverence, because they do not know how to exalt themselves. Conscious of their purity, they strive to please God more than human beings.
“Your hair is like flocks of goats.” The Apostle teaches, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven, that hair was given to a woman as an adornment. So the Church especially counts as her adornment those peoples who first believed from among the circumcised. They sold their possessions, distributed the proceeds for the use of the poor, and had one heart and one soul, as Scripture says in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter four. These came up from Mount Gilead. Gilead means “heap of testimony.” In other words, their ascent—that is, their spiritual progress—came from the testimonies of the holy Scriptures, because in those Scriptures they recognized the Christ in whom they later believed.
“Your teeth are like flocks of shorn sheep.” By teeth he means preachers. They take what they have received, break it down in a saving way, and pass it into the body of Christ. These preachers are compared to flocks of shorn sheep because they have put off the old self and have moved forward in the newness of the Spirit. They have also come up from the washing, because, after receiving forgiveness of sins through baptism, they made progress in Christ.
“All of them bear twins.” All are filled with love for Christ and for their neighbor. And because they are ready for mercy, none among them is barren, as the Book of Joshua, chapter six, is understood to suggest.
“Your lips are like a scarlet ribbon.” Rahab, the prostitute who was found worthy to be saved and joined to the people of Israel, hung a scarlet cord from the window of her house as a sign of salvation. Therefore their lips are beautiful, because they preach the passion and announce the redemption given through his blood. They rejoice that they have found nothing sweeter to speak about. As the Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter one: “I judge that I know nothing among you except Jesus Christ, and him crucified.”
“Your cheeks are like a slice of pomegranate.” Those who preserve chastity, with modesty as its companion, create beauty in the face of the Church. So do the holy martyrs: by imitating Christ’s passion, they are covered with the rosy beauty of their own blood. And this is only the visible beauty; besides the merit of chastity and martyrdom, there is also what lies hidden within. The saints possess many other good things, known to Christ, because he himself granted them in their hearts.
“Your neck is like the tower of David.” The tower means the perfection or the teaching of the saints. It was not built by them, but by Christ. It has its defenses: the testimonies of the holy Scriptures, by which unbelievers and heretics are defeated.
“A thousand shields hang from it.” This tower can fittingly be understood as the canonical Scripture of the Old and New Testaments, constructed by the Holy Spirit like a lofty tower. “A thousand shields hang from it.” This number is taken as a sign of complete fullness. So the Psalm says, in Psalm one hundred four, “the word that he commanded for a thousand generations,” meaning, as it were, for all generations. Therefore every weapon of the strong is found in holy Scripture. From it one fights powerfully against the devil and against the devil’s servants. Indeed, when the Lord himself was tempted by the devil in the desert, as the Gospel of Matthew, chapter four, recounts, he drew weapons from this tower: by bringing forward testimonies from sacred Scripture, he defeated the devil completely.
“Your two breasts are like two young fawns, twins of a gazelle.” The two breasts of the Church are the two Testaments. They are fitted to the two twin fawns—that is, to the two peoples who are nourished from you: the people of the circumcision and the people of the foreskin. Both were born from the inheritance of sin. Yet they feed among the lilies when they grow together through the examples and teachings of the saints.
“Until the day breathes, and turns.” This means: until, as the Apostle says in Paul’s second letter to the Corinthians, chapter five, what is mortal is swallowed up.
“I will go to the mountain of myrrh.” That is: I will be joined to those who have grown by putting themselves to death for God’s sake, and who never cease, by offering themselves to him, to pour out the fragrance of grace.
“You are wholly beautiful, my friend.” As the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, cited here as chapter three, Christ desired to present the Church to himself without spot or wrinkle. Nor is it meaningless that he says, “You are wholly beautiful.” He means: in all her members, small and great. Not only in martyrs, confessors, and learned virgins, but also in the continent, in those who give alms, and in penitents.
“Come from Lebanon, my bride, come.” Lebanon is interpreted as “whiteness.” Unless a person receives that whiteness through holy baptism, where forgiveness of sins is given, that person cannot come to Christ.
“You will be crowned from the summit of Amana.” Seir is interpreted as “lamp,” and Hermon as “consecration.” All these meanings refer to Christ. Those whom he enlightens, he consecrates; and those whom he enlightens and consecrates, he also crowns.
“From the dens of lions.” The Church is called out from the dens of lions and from the mountains of leopards when, leaving behind proud self-exaltation and the traditions of various sects, she comes to Christ simple and humble.
“You have wounded my heart, my sister.” This wound of the heart is love. Therefore, with that one eye of immense love, the Church pleased Christ, because she looked ahead not to temporal things, but to eternal things. So too with the one lock of hair on her neck: she sought to be adorned not with visible things, but with things to come. Or perhaps the one eye and the lock of hair on the neck mean the believing people of the Church, whom Christ loved so much that he laid down his life for them.
“How beautiful are your breasts.” How worthy of admiration are those teachers who nourish people newly born in faith. Like devoted nurses, they endure the troubles caused by chattering, fleshly crowds, until they lead to maturity those whom they have raised on spiritual milk.
“Your breasts are more beautiful than wine.” As mentioned earlier, these breasts are the apostles, or apostolic men. Their teaching surpasses the wine—that is, the tradition and commandment of the old law. “And the fragrance of your ointments is beyond all spices.” This means the distribution of graces granted through the Holy Spirit. The Apostle explains it in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter twelve, when he says, “To one is given a word of wisdom, to another a word of knowledge,” and so on. This grace is so excellent that it surpasses all the vanities of the world.
“Your lips, my bride, are a dripping honeycomb.” The dripping honeycomb of your lips is the exposition of the sacred Scriptures. “Honey and milk are under your tongue,” that is, in your teaching: with milk you nourish the little ones, and with honey you remove the rottenness of various wounds, that is, of sins.
“And the fragrance of your garments.” Through the prophet, the Lord speaks to Jerusalem about the saints: “As I live, says the Lord, you will be clothed with all these as with an ornament, and you will wrap them around yourself like a bride.” Therefore the garments of the Church are all the saints, resplendent in words and works; and they give off a sweet fragrance.
“A garden enclosed is my sister, my bride.” This bride is enclosed like a garden; that is, by the grace of Christ she is surrounded with such an unbreakable enclosure that the prophet Isaiah says of her, in Isaiah, chapter fifty-two: “No uncircumcised or unclean person will ever again pass through you.” The enclosed garden and sealed fountain can also be understood as the Lord’s own mother, Saint Mary. Conceiving as a virgin and giving birth as a virgin, she displayed in herself the undefiled beauty of an enclosed garden and a sealed fountain.
“Your shoots are a paradise of apple trees.” Your growth means the gathering of the holy martyrs, together with the fruits of the trees; that is, together with the rest of the faithful multitude, adorned with the sweetness of righteousness.
“Henna with nard, nard and saffron.” The incorruption and strong virtue of the martyrs, along with teaching and good reputation, are found among all peoples reborn in Christ through baptism.
“Myrrh and aloes, with all the finest spices.” These are the signs of Christ’s death, together with the chief gifts of the Holy Spirit.
“A fountain of gardens, a well of waters.” The open fountain is the instruction of peoples. The well is the depth of doctrines. These waters flow with force from Lebanon when they are sent out by Christ with the fervor of the Spirit.
“Rise up, North Wind, and come, South Wind.” There is one Spirit. Although North Wind and South Wind are different names, the text is pointing to the gifts of the one Holy Spirit. He is called the North Wind when he gives coolness to those who are burning with heat; he is called the South Wind when he softens those who are hard, so that they may believe and do good. The Psalmist teaches that the North Wind can be understood in a good sense when he says, in Psalm forty-seven: “Mount Zion, the sides of the north, the city of the great King.” And again the prophet Ezekiel says, in Ezekiel, chapter one: “The hand of the Lord was upon me, and I looked, and behold, a shining Spirit was coming from the north.” And because one Spirit is recognized in the two winds, the text does not say in the plural, “Blow, you winds,” but in the singular, “Breathe upon my garden, and let its spices flow.” In other words: inspire the believing people, and let their good works abound.

Chapter 5: Christ Enters the Church and Sustains His Bride

“Let my beloved come into his garden.” That is: let him enter his Church, and take delight in the works of his saints. Or again: let him be fed by the faithful in the persons of the poor, since he will repay these temporary acts of mercy with the eternal kingdom.
“Come into my garden, my sister.” In other words: hurry toward my kingdom, people enriched by my name. You are my sister, because you come from my blood; you are my bride, because you belong to my faith. As the prophet Hosea says, in Hosea, chapter two: “I will betroth you to myself in faith.”
“I have harvested my myrrh with my spices.” That means: receive my martyrs, those who suffered or died for my sake, together with the merits that belong to them.
“Eat the honeycomb with my honey.” That is: receive into my body those who have been formed by the gentler teaching and by the stronger teaching of the Old and New Testaments.
“Eat, friends, and drink.” In the Gospel, the Lord says to the apostles, in the Gospel of John, chapter fifteen: “I no longer call you servants, but friends.” And to the apostle Peter, when that vessel was sent down from heaven and many kinds of animals were shown to him, it was said in the Acts of the Apostles, chapter ten: “Peter, kill and eat.” In other words: put to death what they are, and make them what you yourself are. So then, “Eat, friends, and drink.” Let there be joy for you over the way of life of the peoples as they pass into your unity.
“I sleep, but my heart keeps watch.” I am ready to die for Christ, but I believe that after death I will reign with him. I also rest from temporary pleasures, as though my senses had been lulled to sleep; but I long for what is to come, and I seek what is eternal.
“The voice of my beloved knocking.” The Lord himself says in the book of Revelation, chapter three: “I stand at the door and knock. If anyone opens to me, I will come in to him, and I will dine with him, and he with me.” The Lord knocks when he teaches what is good through doctrine, or when, by a hidden inspiration, he urges the hearts of the saints to do good. Or this may be the voice of the one knocking in the Gospel: “Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you,” as it says in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter eleven.
“Open to me, my sister, my friend.” Christ asks to be opened to: either through the obedience of unbelievers brought to faith, or through the faith of the nations who have been called. The door is opened to him when the things he commanded are fulfilled, or when an entrance is unlocked for him through faith in those who have believed in him. The Church is Christ’s sister because she comes from his blood; she is his friend because she has been reconciled through his death; she is his dove because she comes from the Holy Spirit; and she is spotless through the sacrament of baptism.
“For my head is full.” Those who seem to hold the highest honor in the Church are understood as a kind of head in the body of Christ. And those who administer the visible sacraments within the assembly of the faithful are described as the locks of hair. But because mortal human beings are often stained by vices and by works of darkness, such ministers seem to cling to Christ the head while weighed down, as it were, with the drops of night. About certain people like this the Apostle says, in Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter one: “They proclaim Christ insincerely.” And so the bride’s voice follows, groaning as it were for such people.
“I have taken off my tunic.” Coming to faith, I put off the old self, with its former way of life, and I put on the new self, which was created according to God, as Paul says in his letter to the Ephesians, chapter four. And if any stain clung to my feet while I walked through earthly things, it was washed away by Christ. Yet then I see, and not without sorrow, that the ministers of the sacraments, by whom I ought to be adorned, are infected with fleshly vices. Because, through the wrongdoing of certain people, I sense myself being darkened again by the very evils I had renounced, I say: “I have taken off my tunic; how can I put it on again? I have washed my feet; how can I dirty them?” With a grieving heart I speak, as if saying more plainly: Is it right for me to put on again the garments of sin from which I was stripped when I believed? Or should I allow myself to be stained again by living badly, with the filth from which I have been washed? Should I turn back, through evil conduct, from progress to decline, from the brightness of righteousness to the blackness of sins?
“My beloved put his hand through the opening.” He put his hand through the opening when he taught me to pass through the narrow straits of suffering. “And my inner being was disturbed at his touch”: this happened when persecution was close at hand, and my weaker members were shaken and trembled at the bitterness of the punishments inflicted on me with his permission. This “touch” is understood as the pain of a blow that is coming, according to what Satan says to the Lord in the book of Job, chapter one: “But touch his bones and his flesh, and see whether he does not curse you to your face.”
“I rose to open to my beloved.” Awakened in heart, I confessed Christ. I was not frightened by bodily torments; instead, in my sufferings and in my deeds, the self-offering of the confessors appeared, and it appeared among the elect. As the Psalm says, in Psalm one hundred twenty-five: “Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints.”
“I opened the bolt of my door.” With every doubt removed, I believed in Christ. I loved him with an inexpressible love, proclaimed him with a clear voice among the nations, and called upon him to be with me in my tribulations.
“But he had turned aside.” He seemed to turn aside and pass by when he handed me over to persecutors and wicked people, and allowed them to rage against my body.
“My soul melted.” Hearing his promises, I burned with longing when I learned from his own promise that those who suffer persecution for righteousness are blessed, because the kingdom of heaven belongs to them, as it says in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter five.
“I sought him and did not find him.” I was left so abandoned beneath the trampling of the wicked that it seemed as though there were no helper, and as though the one who could defend me from my enemies was unable to come to my aid. So also blessed Job said while he was suffering, in the book of Job, chapter thirty: “I cry to you, and you do not hear me; you do not look upon me. You have changed toward me into someone cruel, and in the fury of your hand you oppose me.”
“The watchmen found me.” The powers of this world seized me: those who are known to govern, as it were, the commonwealth.
“They struck me; they wounded me with blows.” They raged against my body with punishments, loaded me with chains, and shut me in prisons. By using carefully devised tortures, they prolonged my life and did not bring death quickly, so that I could not easily be released from the sufferings they inflicted.
“They took away my cloak.” For when they demolished churches down to the ground; when they burned the altars, together with the Gospels and the other canonical books; when they imprisoned priests and condemned them to the mines; when no place was allowed for offering sacrifice, no freedom for baptizing, and no permission for giving or receiving communion—when the persecutors did all these things, then in a sense they took away a kind of cloak from the bride of Christ, that is, from the Church.
“I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem.” She solemnly charges all the saints: if, through these tribulations and by despising the life of this world, they come to him, they are to make known that the Church, even while placed in these persecutions, burns with an indescribable love for God and is in no way cooled by such great and horrifying distress.
“What is your beloved?” This is the voice of the elect who ask the question and seek to learn the faith of the Church from her answer. “What is your beloved, from the Beloved?” That is: what is God from God, or the Son from the Father? By this likeness he declares the equality of all the persons. For those who are not distinguished from one another are believed to be of one nature and to remain in the same glory of majesty.
“My beloved is radiant and ruddy.” He is radiant because, as the Gospel of John says in chapter one: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was God.” He is ruddy because, born from the Virgin, “the Word became flesh and lived among us,” as the Gospel of John says in chapter one. He is radiant in virginity and ruddy in his Passion, just as the prophet says of him in Isaiah, chapter sixty-three: “Who is this who comes from Edom, with garments dyed from Bozrah?” Edom is interpreted here as “blood,” and so he is ruddy from the shedding of blood. “He is beautiful in his robe, walking in the greatness of his strength.” He is “chosen from thousands.” He is the one of whom the Psalms say, in Psalm forty-four: “Who will be like God among the sons of God?” And again: “You are beautiful in form beyond the sons of men; grace has been poured upon your lips.”
“His head is the finest gold.” We read in the Apostle, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter one: “The head of the man is Christ, the head of the woman is the man, and the head of Christ is God.” Therefore, just as among metals nothing is like gold, so among created things nothing can be compared with the Creator. God is the head of Christ according to what we recalled shortly before from the Gospel: “In the beginning was the Word,” and so on.
“His hair is like the tall branches of palm trees.” These saints are known to cling to their head, that is, to Christ, because, like the raven, they recognize that they are sinners. One of them says, as Scripture says in the book of Job, chapter nine: “If I wanted to make myself righteous before you, my own mouth would condemn me.” And another says, in Psalm fifty: “I know my iniquity.” John, speaking with the elect in one harmonious voice, declares in his first letter, chapter one: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. But if we confess our sins, he is faithful and just, so that he forgives us our sins and cleanses us from every injustice.” Paul says the same in his first letter to Timothy, chapter one: “Christ Jesus came into this world to save sinners, and I am the first of them.” And because, according to the Gospel—in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-three, and the Gospel of Luke, chapter four and chapter eighteen—everyone who humbles himself will be exalted, these people who lower themselves by confessing their sins are rightly compared, as this book says, to the height of palm trees.
“His eyes are like doves.” His chosen ones, filled with the Holy Spirit, remain free from every bitterness of resentment, and they nourish the people with the flowing streams of Scripture.
“They have been washed in milk.” They are made purer because, with unhesitating faith, they believe that Christ was born in the flesh. They also recognize that through the visible sacrament they have been reborn, washed, and nourished. So they “sit beside the fullest streams,” that is, among the most abundant gifts of the Holy Spirit.
“His cheeks are like beds of spices.” In the dove-like eyes, we rightly understand the apostles, filled with the Holy Spirit. So too, in the cheeks, which are close to the eyes, we understand the churches that came to faith through the apostles. Like beds of spices, these churches give off a most pleasant fragrance through holy conduct. They were planted by perfumers, that is, by devout preachers. To such people the Apostle says, in his letter to the Romans, chapter eleven: “You, although you were a wild olive shoot, were grafted in among them, and became a partner in the root and richness of the olive tree.”
“His lips drip myrrh.” His lips are his teachers, who proclaim him born of the Spirit and also of the flesh of the Virgin, and who in this myrrh preach his passion, so that, for the sake of the one who died for us, they themselves will not refuse death.
“His hands are golden and rounded.” Those who never cease doing what is good are counted as the hands of Christ. They are called rounded and golden because they are always ready for every good work. They are “full of jacinths,” in keeping with the Apostle’s words in his first letter to Timothy, chapter six: “Be rich in good works, generous in giving, storing up for themselves a good foundation for the future, so that they may take hold of true life.”
“His belly is ivory.” Those who bring believers to birth in faith are counted as his belly. And the ivory belly may also quite fittingly be understood as the fountain of baptism, from which we are reborn. Ivory, after all, is known to be the beautiful and incorruptible bone of a dead animal. For this reason the Apostle says to us, in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter three: “Do you not know that all of us who were baptized in Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? For we were buried with him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ rose through the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life.” “Set with sapphires” means that he is adorned with the saints born from him, saints who shine most brightly in their confession of faith.
“His legs are marble columns.” The legs that carry the whole body are the saints themselves. They are described as supporting the house of the Church, like marble columns set upon golden bases. These are the righteous who bear up others through love and strengthen them with the encouragement of the Scriptures. As the Apostle says in his letter to the Romans, chapter fifteen: “We who are stronger ought to bear the weakness of the weak, and not please ourselves.” A little later, he points to the holy Scriptures as the golden bases, saying: “Whatever was written was written for our instruction, so that through patience and the consolation of the Scriptures we might have hope.”
“His appearance is like Lebanon, choice as the cedars.” All the saints who have given off the sweetest fragrance through good conduct, and who remain whole and uncorrupted, have received the grace of such great blessings from God as their author.
“His throat is most sweet.” Those who explain his mysteries and meditate daily on his law provide, in sacred teaching, a most delightful taste. “And he is altogether desirable.” Then will be fulfilled what blessed John says in his first letter, chapter three: “Beloved, now we are children of God, and it has not yet appeared what we shall be. We know that when he appears, we shall be like him, and we shall see him as he is.”
“Such is my beloved.” She urges the righteous to give their attention to this variety of graces and to these adornments of the mysteries, when she suggests that the bridegroom’s beauty should be contemplated in his members.
“Where has your beloved gone?” They speak as people searching, as if saying: “You have described such great beauty in him that our hearts have been set on fire to seek him. Now show us where he has gone, or where he has turned aside.” The Church is rightly called a virgin, “the most beautiful of women,” because Paul speaks to the faithful about her in this way, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter eleven: “I betrothed you to one husband, so that I might present you as a chaste virgin to Christ.” And they fittingly say of him: “Where has your beloved gone? Where has he turned aside?” For in a sense he did turn aside: although he was in the form of God and did not think equality with God something to be seized, he emptied himself, taking the form of a servant. He was made in the likeness of human beings, and, being found in appearance as a man, he humbled himself even to death, death on a cross, as Paul’s letter to the Philippians, chapter two, says. Therefore, whoever seeks the Exalted One in the form of God will not find him unless he believes in the form of a servant. He humbled himself in that form precisely so that he might raise us up as we lay fallen.

Chapter 6: Christ Visits His Garden, the Church

“My beloved has gone down to his garden.” He went down to his garden when, at his coming, he visited Israel, his own people. As he himself says in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter ten: “I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” And by the little bed of spices we understand the holy Virgin Mary herself. For from her there arose for us the pattern of righteousness: the Lord Jesus Christ.
“That he may feed in the gardens and gather lilies.” That is, that he may reign among the nations, or take delight in the churches, and from them gather to himself virgins and all the holy ones.
“I belong to my beloved, and my beloved belongs to me.” I am Christ’s kingdom, and he is my king. I am his body, and he is my head. He feeds among the lilies when he rests in the hearts of virgins, or when, by the grace he himself gives, he daily grows and flourishes there.
“You are beautiful, my friend; beautiful,” and so on. She is beautiful through righteousness. She is gentle and beloved because she is always peaceful. She is lovely like Jerusalem. The Lord says in the Gospel, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twelve, and the Gospel of Luke, chapter ten: “In the resurrection they will neither marry nor be given in marriage, but they will be like the angels in heaven.” So the Church is rightly described as lovely like Jerusalem, since the Lord grants her the likeness of the angels forever.
“Terrible like an army drawn up for battle.” How many tyrants and persecutors, together with their author, the devil, have tried to shatter the Church’s strength—and they have never been able to prevail. For the holy Church has an ordered battle line when Christ stands over her: persecution does not conquer the martyrs, bodily pleasure does not conquer the virgins, greed does not conquer the merciful, and ambition for worldly glory does not conquer the humble.
“Turn your eyes away from me, for…” Do not keep asking to see me bodily, because you see me better in spirit, through faith. This is why I ascended into heaven: so that I would not always appear to you as though confined to one place. In fact, by the presence of my divinity, I fill all things in such a way that I am present everywhere, contain everything, and am contained by no place.
“Your hair is like flocks of goats”; “your teeth are like a flock of sheep, all bearing twins, with none barren”; and, “like the rind of a pomegranate.” It is clear that these four verses are written here a second time. With God’s help we have already discussed them earlier. In them, however, he foretold that both Jews and Gentiles would be converted with the fruit of the twofold love, and he revealed the glory of the martyrs together with their merits.
“There are sixty queens and eighty concubines.” In the number sixty, which is completed when six rises through ten, we recognize the full perfection of good things, the perfection brought to completion by the commandments of the Law. Now notice briefly how the number six is made up of all its own parts: its sixth part is one, its third part is two, and its half is three. And everyone knows that one plus two plus three makes six. It is hard to find this same result in another number, when its parts are divided up and then brought back together into one. So the “queens” are the perfect souls of the saints, souls that are not slaves of sins. As Scripture says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter three: “Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is freedom.” The “concubines,” however, are those who, like Hagar, give birth into slavery, as Paul says in his letter to the Galatians, chapter four. They are rightly understood as people who have received the seed of the word of God, yet set their minds not on spiritual things but on fleshly things. And the “young women without number” are the multitude of those who are always ready to hear holy teachings, but do not make any effort to fulfill what they hear by their actions. About them the Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to Timothy, chapter three: “Always learning, and never arriving at knowledge of the truth.” And although these are different kinds of people who are joined to Christ in some way through his teaching, nevertheless, because unity rather than diversity is praised among the elect, listen to what follows.
“My dove, my perfect one, is one.” She is a dove because she is simple and thinks spiritually. She is perfect because, leaving the world behind, she loves me more than temporal things and more than herself. This agrees with the Lord’s words in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter nineteen: “If you wish to be perfect, go, sell everything you have and give it to the poor; then come and follow me.”
“She is the one chosen by her mother.” Her mother is the heavenly Jerusalem, about which the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Galatians, chapter four: “The Jerusalem above is our mother.” She is one to that mother because, in the blessedness to come, only she will be joined to her.
“The daughters saw her and proclaimed her most blessed.” What holy souls would not lift up the Church with marvelous praise? Even the concubines—that is, fleshly people—are not silent in praising her, when they admire her as unconquered in the martyrs and victorious in the virgins.
“Who is this who comes forth like…” She rises like the dawn when, among the married, she despises the works of darkness and lives chastely and soberly. She is beautiful as the moon when, among the continent, she shines with bright radiance. She is chosen as the sun when, among virgins, she gleams with the beauty of perfection. She is like the good soil in the Gospel, which received the seed of the word of God and produced fruit thirtyfold, sixtyfold, and a hundredfold. She is “terrible as an army drawn up in order.” Is she not terrible—she who overthrew the rites of the nations and the worship of idols, and, persevering in the faith of Christ, could not be overcome by the nations? She is also very terrible in this: in Peter, she is given the power described in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter sixteen, and again in chapter eighteen: “Whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven; and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven.”
“I went down to the garden of nuts, to see.” The garden of nuts signifies the canon of the Old and New Testaments. A nut shows one thing in its shell and keeps another thing in its kernel. So if someone does not make this distinction in the holy Scriptures—if, so to speak, he does not look for the inner meaning beneath the nut’s shell—he cannot arrive at the birth of spiritual understanding. And when he says, “to see the fruits of the valley,” he means: to see the fruit of the humblest people in the Church. As is sung in the Book of Psalms, Psalm seventeen: “You will save a humble people, and you will humble the eyes of the proud.”
“To see whether the vines had flowered.” That means: to test whether the Church had advanced so far that there would spring up within her people who could reach the perfection of martyrdom—people who would be red and beautiful because of the shedding of the blood of their Christ.
“I did not know; my soul troubled me.” What did Christ not know, except sin? He also did not know those people who say in the Gospel: “Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?” Then, he says in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter seven: “I will say to them: Amen, I tell you, I do not know you.” This does not mean that he did not know what kind of people they were. It means that he recognized nothing good in them. “My soul troubled me because of the chariots of Amminadab.” We also find this Amminadab in Christ’s genealogy. Therefore we understand these chariots in a good sense, and it is fitting to take them as the Evangelists. Now when he says, “My soul troubled me,” this disturbance is not opposed to calm; rather, it is found to be in keeping with solicitude. This is like what is said to Martha in the Gospel of Luke, chapter ten: “Why are you anxious and troubled about many things?” And in the Book of Psalms, Psalm fifty-nine: “You shook the earth and troubled it; heal its fractures, for it has been shaken.” And from what source do all the narratives of the Gospels tell their story, if not through the Holy Spirit? This agrees with the prophet Ezekiel, in chapters three and ten, where it says: “Wherever the Spirit went, there the wheels were carried along with it.”
“Return, return, O Shunammite.” We find this Shunammite twice in the books of Kings, and in both places she is praiseworthy. One is the woman who served King David in his old age and, though held in his embrace, remained a most beautiful virgin, as told in the fourth book of Kings, chapter four. The other is the woman whose son was born by promise and later died, and whom Elisha raised up, as told in the third book of Kings, chapter one. So who could doubt that, under this name, we should understand the holy Church? She is often told, “Return,” because once, in the person of the younger son, she had gone away into a distant country and turned aside, as the Gospel of Luke, chapter fifteen, says. She truly returned when she was converted to God from the heart. Or perhaps she is told so often to return so that, even if she sins after conversion, she will not fall into despair. Instead, by hope in forgiveness, she may come back to her senses and leave behind the habit of sinning. This call clearly belongs to the saints, who long to behold the beauty of the one who has returned.

Chapter 7: The Church as Spiritual Army and Bride

What will you see in the Shunammite, if not this? What should be looked for in the Church, if not the ordered ranks of those who are always armed and ready, stationed as if in a camp, and never stop fighting against the devil? They fight so that believers may not lose the faith; so that those vowed to religious life may not grow proud and become apostates; so that the merciful may not be hardened by greed; so that penitents may not return to their vomit; so that virgins may not lose the beauty of integrity; and so that the continent may not lose the brightness of chastity. These are the battle formations of the camp within the Church, always engaged in struggle, and, with God defending them, always unconquered.
How beautiful are your steps! How wonderful is the progress made by those who proclaim the word of God in the Church of Christ. As the Apostle recalls what is written in the prophet: “How beautiful are the feet of those who proclaim good news.” And again he says, in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter ten: “Have your feet shod with readiness for the Gospel of peace.”
“The joints of your thighs.” By the joining of your thighs we understand the fellowship of peoples who come both from circumcision and from uncircumcision. That is, those who were born according to the flesh from the thigh of Abraham, and those who were counted worthy in Abraham’s seed, that is, in Christ, are joined together in the unity of faith. So they are described as ornaments fashioned by the hand of a craftsman. And who is the craftsman who joined these peoples in faith like ornaments, if not the one of whom the Apostle says, in Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter six: “He is our peace, who made both peoples one”?
“Your navel is a turned bowl.” Those who are changed, not at the far edges, but in the middle of the Church’s body, are called the navel. The turned bowl represents useful vessels—turned and shaped because they are ready to obey and prepared for every good work, as Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, chapter two, says. They never lack drink, because they are always filled with the grace of the Holy Spirit.
“Your belly is like a heap of wheat.” Those who are always receiving the holy nourishment of the Scriptures, and who store up within themselves what leads to salvation, are compared to the belly. They are rightly called a piled-up heap of wheat, because from the sound teaching with which they are filled each day, they refresh many people in abundance. This teaching is so fruitful that, by devout persuasion, it even leads many to preserve virginity.
“Your two breasts are like two young fawns.” The two Testaments are fitted to two young fawns of a doe—that is, to the peoples who come from the stock of sin. For those who are subject to original sin are nourished by these works, and in Christ they are reborn like young fawns.
“Your neck is like an ivory tower.” Knowledge of the Scriptures, gained through constant meditation, is indicated by the beauty of the neck, like an ivory tower. It is called an ivory tower because those who serve the word of God, through daily growth and through the brightness of righteousness, advance to higher things.
“Your eyes are like pools in Heshbon.” Your spiritual teachers, stationed at the gate, are in Christ like irrigating waters for the instruction of the peoples. They are called pools because they are filled with the knowledge of the Holy Spirit. Solomon himself says in another book, in Ecclesiastes, chapter two: “I built pools of water for myself, so that I might water a forest of growing trees.”
“Your nose is like the tower of Lebanon.” The nose of the Church means those who perceive the fragrance of righteousness that is in Christ, and carry it into the inner places of the heart. These people, who stand firm in great virtue, are arranged like the tower of Lebanon. As mentioned earlier, Lebanon means whiteness. The bright height of these people faces toward Damascus, because their wisdom, which comes from God, enlightens the world.
“Your head is like Carmel, and your hair.” On Mount Carmel, the holy prophets Elijah and Elisha often had a place of refuge, as the third book of Kings, chapter eighteen, records. Therefore, in the head of the Church, our Lord Jesus Christ, who is most exalted—as on Mount Carmel—both the righteous find a dwelling place, and the multitude of peoples, like many kinds of living creatures, receive the food of wisdom. The hair of this head is like royal purple fastened to channels. That is, the more eminent saints, especially the blessed martyrs of Christ, are compared through the image of purple to royal ornaments, and are understood as always joined to teachings that bring salvation.
“Your stature is like a palm tree.” Your perfection has reached its reward, and your teachers have been made sweet through the Holy Spirit.
“I said: I will climb the palm tree.” Those climb the palm tree who make progress in the cross of Christ. Of them the Apostle says: “Those who belong to Christ have crucified their flesh with its vices and desires.” And those receive the fruits of this palm tree—the rewards of the holy cross—who say with the same Apostle, in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight: “If we suffer with him, we shall also be glorified with him.”
“And your breasts will be like clusters of the vine.” For no others are found to nourish those being born in the faith except the teachers of the Church.
“And your fragrance will be like apples.” The sweetness of your instruction will be spread through those who live soberly and justly, and who never stop offering the fragrance of a good way of life.
“Your throat is like the finest wine.” The throat can also be understood as those holy people through whom the Law, the Prophets, and the teaching of the Gospel were proclaimed. This holy Scripture, endowed with an excellent taste, gives off fragrance and shines like the finest wine, and through the Holy Spirit it makes those who drink it spiritually intoxicated.
“Worthy for my beloved to drink.” Christians drink from the sacred Scripture of Christ when, from it, they—the members of his body—are satisfied in marvelous ways and with incomparable sweetness.
“And for his lips and teeth to chew over.” The lips and teeth of Christ are the teachers of the Church. They never stop chewing over what is holy, because they meditate every day on the things written for our instruction.
“I belong to my beloved, and his turning is toward me.” To this beloved, speaking in the voice of the Church, it is said in Psalm sixty-two: “My soul clung after you; your right hand upheld me.” Let us also see elsewhere how the Church speaks to her beloved, in Psalm seventy-eight: “But we are your people, and the sheep of your flock.” And how is his turning toward me? The prophet says, in Isaiah, chapter sixty-six: “Before they call, I will answer; while they are still speaking, I will hear, says the Lord.” But there is no turning of Christ toward the Church more evident than this, in the Gospel of John, chapter one: “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”
“Come, my beloved, let us go out.” Christ goes out with the Church into the field when, together with the saints already mentioned, he visits the world. As it is written in the Gospel, in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter thirteen, and the Gospel of Mark, chapter fourteen: “The one who sows the good seed is the Son of Man,” and, “the field is the world.” “Let us stay in the villages”—that is, among the believing peoples spread everywhere. Christ dwells in them through faith, not apart from the Church, because he counts these very people among his own members and makes them his Church.
“Let us rise early and go to the vineyards.” This means that souls already made worthy to be cultivated through faith should recognize, in the morning light, that Christ has risen, so that they may no longer doubt the resurrection of their own body.
“Let us see whether the vineyard has blossomed.” Let it become clear whether the faithful proclaim with their mouth what they have believed in their heart. As the Apostle says, in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter ten: “With the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.” Or, let it become clear whether the blossoms are giving birth to fruit—that is, whether works of devotion have come forth in them through holy desires.
“Whether the pomegranates have blossomed.” This asks whether those who have believed in Christ are advancing in such great love that they are even glad, in every way, to be killed for his sake. Do you want to see a pomegranate? Listen to the apostle Paul in the Acts of the Apostles. He says: “I am ready not only to be judged in Jerusalem, but also to die for Christ.”
“There I will give you your breasts.” In this holy confession my teachers will be joined to you, prepared to suffer for you; and they too will reign with you without end.
“The mandrakes have given their fragrance.” Those who are founded in faith with a deep root, and who are known to flourish through a certain austerity of discipline and self-restraint, have instructed the peoples through public preaching, offering the sweet fragrance that comes from a most holy way of life.
“All gifts, new and old.” For in the patriarchs and prophets, and also in the apostles and martyrs, I myself have kept new and old gifts for you. The former proclaimed you and believed that you would come; the latter never cease to preach that you were born of the Virgin, suffered for the salvation of all, and, after the resurrection, were taken up into heaven.

Chapter eight: The Church Is Sealed by Christ’s Love

“Who will give you to me as my brother?” Christ suckles at his mother’s breasts when the Christian people are refreshed by the mysteries of the ancient Law, or when teachers nourish them with spiritual sacraments and with a most devout interpretation, as though feeding them with true milk.
“So that I may find you outside and kiss you.” She is saying: “You, whom the figures of the Old Testament kept hidden, I will now truly find through the Gospel.” And she rightly says, “I will find you outside,” because when the younger son returned from a far country, the father came out to meet him, as the Gospel of Luke says in chapter fifteen. “Then I may kiss you,” she says, “and be found worthy to be reconciled to you; and then no one will despise me.” In other words, no one will say to Peter, on account of Cornelius, whom the grace of the Holy Spirit had gone before: “Why did you go in to men who are uncircumcised and eat with them?” Instead, Peter himself will declare, as the Acts of the Apostles says in chapter eleven: “In truth I have discovered that God shows no partiality. In every place and in every nation, whoever fears him and does what is right is acceptable to him.”
“I will take hold of you and lead you.” Once I have taken hold of you and clung to you by faith, it remains for me to bring you into my mother’s house, that is, into the assembly of Jerusalem. I will surely bring you there when, together with all the good things I have received through your grace, I myself am found worthy to be brought there also—that is, to the heavenly Jerusalem.
“There you will teach me, and I will give you a cup.” There you will teach me, so that I may no longer see in a riddle, as in a mirror, but face to face; and so that I may no longer know only in part, as I do now, but then know as I have also been known, as Paul says in his first letter to the Corinthians, chapter thirteen. “And I will give you a cup from spiced wine, and the new wine of pomegranates,” when I have brought forth the finest and sweetest fruits of the holy martyrs. Then, as Psalm thirty-five says, “The children of men, who hope in the shadow of your wings, will be filled with the richness of your house, and you will give them drink from the torrent of your delights.”
“His left hand is under my head, and his right hand will embrace me. I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem.” These words written here have already been stated earlier and, with Christ’s illumination, have already been explained. But I think they were written a second time in this book so that they might sink more deeply into the heart, and, by being repeated, be renewed in a more living memory. They teach us that temporal things must be despised and placed beneath us, like the left hand set under the head. They also remind us to wait for the heavenly glory that embraces us from above, like the right hand. Or else they teach this: that those who are going to serve the Lord should carry out holy duties willingly, not reluctantly, in keeping with what the apostle Peter teaches in his first letter, chapter five: “Shepherd the flock of God that is among you, not under compulsion, but willingly.”
“Who is this who comes up from the desert?” She is rightly described as coming up from the desert, for the prophet says to her in Isaiah, chapter sixty: “Because you were abandoned and hated, and there was no one passing through you,” and so on. Of her children, too, the Psalms sing, in Psalm ten: “They wandered in the wilderness, in a dry place; they did not find the way to a city where they could dwell. Hungry and thirsty, their soul failed within them. Then they cried to the Lord in their trouble, and he delivered them from their necessities. He led them by the right way, so that they might go to a city where they could dwell.” Therefore the text continues.
“Overflowing with delights.” For this reason, when the people of Israel came out of the desert, they entered the promised land, flowing with milk and honey. And the son who longed to feed on the pods of swine but could not be satisfied was brought in to be refreshed at the banquet prepared by his father from the fattened calf that had been killed. Coming from the desert, the Church finds these delights in many forms in Christ. And she is described as leaning upon her beloved, because that same beloved says to her through the prophet, in Isaiah, chapter forty-six: “I have made; I will carry; I will bear; and I will save.”
“Under the apple tree I awakened you.” On the fragrant wood of the cross, where I lay down in death, there I raised you from the destruction of sins, when I command that you, as a believer, be marked beforehand with the sign of the cross.
“There your mother was corrupted.” In the very place where you were found worthy to be raised up through my death, there the Synagogue was trapped by her own ungodliness, when she cried out, “Crucify him,” and, as Pilate sat in judgment, answered, as the Gospel of Luke, chapter twenty-three, relates, “His blood be upon us and upon our children.”
“Set me as a seal.” Then you will be able to say with the psalmist, in Psalm four: “The light of your face, Lord, has been sealed upon us; you have given joy in my heart.” Since things that have been sealed cannot easily be violated, receive my faith in such a way that you guard it whole and undefiled. And do not in any way fear that anyone can touch the treasure of righteousness that you received through the seal of the cross—the treasure I have placed within you.
“Set me as a seal.” Understand that the seal is to be placed first upon the heart, and then upon the arm. That means: in both thought and action, run to my sign, and by this show that you belong not to another, but to me. Then, with me watching over you, you will persevere unharmed.
“For your love is strong as death.” I loved you so much that I was willing to die for you; and this was the strength of love: the bitterness of death. “Jealousy is hard as hell,” so that, in return, the saints do not fear being killed for Christ, since they know without doubt that he accepted death for their salvation.
“Its lamps are lamps of fire.” The vessels in which chastity dwells are always shining and burning with the fire of the Holy Spirit.
“Many waters could not extinguish love, nor will rivers overwhelm it.” No troubles, no persecutions, however powerful, can disturb or overcome the love that comes from God and is directed toward God. This agrees with what the Apostle says in Paul’s letter to the Romans, chapter eight: “Who will separate us from the love of Christ? Will trouble, or distress, or persecution, or hunger, or nakedness, or the sword, or danger? For I am certain that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature will be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.”
“If a person should give away all his possessions.” It is as if Scripture were saying, in the words of Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter thirteen: “Even if I distribute all my goods as food for the poor, and even if I hand over my body to be burned, but do not have love, it profits me nothing.” For nothing is more precious than love, as the apostle John says: “God is love, and whoever remains in love remains in God.”
“Our sister is little, and she has no breasts.” This refers to the Church coming from the Gentiles: still inexperienced in believing, and not yet supplied with learned men capable of teaching. It is like what the Apostle says in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter three: “I could not speak to you as to spiritual people, but as to little children in Christ. I gave you milk to drink, not solid food; for you were not yet able to take it. Indeed, even now you still cannot, for you are still fleshly.” And elsewhere, in Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter six, he says: “If you have disputes about worldly matters, appoint as judges those who are least esteemed in the Church. I say this to your shame: is there no one wise among you who can judge between brothers?” By rebuking them for such smallness, he clearly shows that this people is still little in faith and not yet ready for spiritual nourishment.
“What shall we do for our sister?” That is: how shall we arrange the Church at the time when she is to be called to faith?
“If she is a wall, let us build upon it.” Those who have begun to grow in faith and become so firm that by their own strength they can encourage the weak should, after receiving the privilege of office, be appointed to teach the rest. As Paul says to Timothy in his second letter, chapter two: “Entrust these things to faithful people who will be capable of teaching others also.” And again Paul says to Titus, chapter one: “For this reason I left you in Crete: that you should correct what is lacking and appoint presbyters in each city, as I directed you.” When these things are arranged in this way in the Church, it is as though silver battlements are added to the walls.
“If she is a door, let us fasten it together.” The prophet says in Psalm one hundred forty: “Set a guard, Lord, over my mouth, and a door of restraint around my lips.” So, if someone is fit for speaking, and, like a door that is opened and closed, understands the proper time to speak and the proper time to be silent, let us fasten such a door with cedar boards. Then, once he has received fragrant wood within himself, he can say with Paul, in his letter to the Galatians, chapter six: “Far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world.”
“I am a wall, and my breasts are like a tower.” This is the voice of the Church as she advances both in faith and on behalf of the faith. “I am a wall,” because the one who built me fights for me with supreme strength. “And my breasts are like a tower.” Built up by God, they have grown tall through righteousness. In me, they nourish the little ones who are in Christ, and they do not stop raising them until they reach the food of maturity—until the voice of Wisdom says to them: “Leave childhood behind, and come walk in the ways of prudence.”
“From the time I became so before him.” From the time I found peace in Christ, from that time I received, with beauty, the protection of walls and the defenses of towers. Along with them, I was found worthy to be a city established by the one who created me: having fortifications in the saints, and preserving beauty in the elect.
“The vineyard belonged to the Peaceful One.” This means that Christ has continually cultivated the Church, the Church that contains those peoples of whom the Psalm says: “Planted in the house of the Lord, they will flourish in the courts of our God,” in Psalm ninety-one. “He entrusted it to keepers.” That is, he committed it to the apostles to govern, or to their successors, the bishops. Under the name of Jerusalem, the Lord says to this Church through the prophet: “Upon you and upon your walls I have appointed your watchmen; all day and all night, until the end, they will not be silent,” in the book of Isaiah, chapter eight.
“The man brings a thousand silver pieces for its fruit.” This man is Christ, of whom the prophet says: “Behold the man; his name is the Rising One,” in the book of Zechariah, chapter six. Christ gives the kingdom of heaven in return for holy labors and works of mercy, according to what is said in the Gospel: “Come, you blessed of my Father; receive the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. For I was hungry, and you gave me food; I was thirsty, and you gave me drink; I was a stranger, and you welcomed me; I was naked, and you clothed me; I was sick and in prison, and you came to me,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-five. And in the thousand silver pieces, the fullness of the whole reward is shown: the completed recompense of the righteous.
“My vineyard is before me.” This agrees with what is sung in the Psalms: “The eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous, and his ears are open to their prayers.” For the Apostle says: “The Lord himself, with a command, with the voice of an archangel, and with the trumpet of God, will descend from heaven; and the dead who are in Christ will rise first. Then we who are alive, who remain, will be caught up together with them to meet the Lord in the air. And so we will always be with the Lord,” in Paul’s first letter to the Thessalonians, chapter four. Therefore, because after the resurrection the saints are always with the Lord, the Lord says, “My vineyard is before me.”
“The thousand are yours, O Peaceful One, and two hundred belong to these.” If the twelve are multiplied by a hundred, they make twelve hundred. This number fittingly belongs to the apostolic men who, through the Gospel, have been spiritually begotten from the twelve in Christ. Established in the highest perfection, they answer their Maker like that best soil, by yielding a hundredfold fruit. These are also rightly described as peaceful people—that is, as Christ’s disciples—both because they guard the fruit of the Church, and because they either frighten away the evil people outside by the voice of their teaching, or, by their prayers, they never stop defending the Church planted by Christ from wicked dogs and from the bites of wild beasts.
“You who dwell in the gardens.” That is: you who abound in peoples established under the cultivation of righteousness, or peoples always watered by the stream of spiritual teaching.
“Friend, listen; let me hear you.” In the Gospel, the Lord says to his disciples: “I will no longer call you servants, but friends,” in the Gospel of John, chapter twenty-five. And again: “The one who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the friend of the bridegroom, who stands and listens to him, rejoices with joy because of the bridegroom’s voice,” in the Gospel of John, chapter three. “Let me hear your voice”—that voice, surely, with which you speak when rewarding the faithful: “Well done, good and faithful servant. Because you have been faithful over a few things, I will set you over many. Enter into the joy of your Lord,” in the Gospel of Matthew, chapter twenty-eight.
“Flee, my beloved, and be like a gazelle or a young stag upon the mountains of spices.” You who lived with us on earth, return eagerly to the heights above. And as you ascend on high—you who now appear to bodily eyes—be always present to the eyes of the heart, so that we may hold you by faith, though we do not see you by sight. “Be like a gazelle or a young stag,” because you are the one who took on the flesh of mortals and, with a swift leap, passed beyond all the heights of the heavens. “Upon the mountains of spices”: that is, you exist higher than all the glory of the angels.
